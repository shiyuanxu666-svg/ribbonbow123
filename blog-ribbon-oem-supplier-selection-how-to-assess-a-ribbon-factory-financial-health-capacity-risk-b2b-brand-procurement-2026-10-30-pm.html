Why Supplier Assessment Is a Process, Not a Catalogue

Most ribbon buyers do not choose a factory badly. They choose one under time pressure, with three quotes in hand, on the basis of a price and a photograph of a warehouse. The problem is not the decision itself — three quotes and a price comparison is a reasonable shortlist — it is that a price comparison measures the smallest part of what you are buying. You are not buying metres of ribbon; you are buying the probability that metre number nine thousand is the same as metre number one, that the next delivery is on time, that the certificate on the file actually covers the article in your carton, and that the factory is still trading in three years when you want to place the repeat order.

That last point deserves more attention than it usually gets. In private-label sourcing, the supplier relationship almost always outlives the buyer who set it up. A programme begun in 2026 is often still running in 2031, with a different procurement manager, a different designer, a different set of market conditions, and a brand identity that has moved on. The supplier you choose is effectively the custodian of your brand's physical presentation for a period of years. Choosing well is a process, and the process has steps.

This guide sets out ten checks that, taken together, tell you more about a ribbon factory than any number of quotes or any glossy capability deck will. It is written for brand owners, private-label managers and procurement teams who need to commit volume to a manufacturing partner and who want to know what they are committing to. It is also written from the manufacturing side, because the most useful version of this advice is the one that explains what a good factory is actually trying to do — which is to build you a stable, repeatable product, year after year, in a way you can plan around.

At Smith Ribbon, in Xiamen since 2004, we have been assessed by buyers using exactly these checks for more than two decades, and we have watched the industry shift from price-led selection toward capability-led selection. That shift is not a fashion; it is a direct consequence of supply chains becoming less predictable, and it is worth understanding the checks on that basis rather than treating them as a checklist to be passed.

Check 1-3: Process Capability, Capacity Headroom and Financial Health

Check 1: Is the process actually in-house? The single most useful question to ask a ribbon factory, and the one most often not asked, is which parts of the chain happen in its own building. A factory that weaves but buys in dyeing, printing and finishing is a different business from one that does all four, and the difference shows up in the ways that matter: lead time, because each external step is somebody else's queue; consistency, because each handover is a chance for variation; and pricing, because each external step carries its own margin and its own risk of substitution.

Ask specifically: which processes are in-house, which are subcontracted, and what happens to the schedule if a subcontractor misses a date. Then check whether the answer is consistent with the plant tour. A factory claiming in-house dyeing should have dyehouses you can see, with the capacity to run your colour volume. A factory claiming in-house printing should have printing equipment that can handle your design and your quantity. The check is not adversarial — plenty of excellent factories deliberately run a lean model with a small core — but the buyer needs to know which model they are buying from, because the answers differ about everything that follows.

Check 2: Is there capacity headroom? Not "what is your capacity" — everybody has a number — but "how much of your capacity is committed right now, and how much is uncommitted". A factory running at 95 per cent of capacity has no room for your programme to grow, no room to absorb an urgent repeat, and no room to absorb a mistake. A factory running at 60 per cent has the opposite: it can start your programme immediately, it can flex when demand surprises you, and it has the margin to fix problems without them becoming your problem.

Ask for the current figure and for the same figure six months ago, and ask what happens to your lead time during the peak season. Peak-season lead time is the real lead time; the off-season figure is marketing. At Smith Ribbon, with 100,000 metres of daily output across 200+ staff, we plan programmes against scheduled headroom rather than against theoretical capacity, because the difference between the two is the difference between a supplier who can absorb your growth and one who will ask you to wait for a slot.

Check 3: Is the business financially sound, and how do you assess that? This is the check buyers most want to make and least know how to make. Financial statements for a Chinese supplier are usually not shared with a prospective buyer at this stage, and asking for them directly is often unwelcome. There are better proxies.

Look at the age of the business and its ownership structure. A family-owned factory that has been running for fifteen or twenty years, with the same name over the door, has survived multiple cycles and is structurally more stable than a trading company formed two years ago. Look at whether the factory owns its building and land, which is a meaningful signal in the Chinese manufacturing sector. Look at whether the business has diversified customers rather than depending on one, because a factory with 80 per cent of its volume from a single buyer is exposed to that buyer's decisions in a way that is not visible on the surface. Look at the payment terms they are willing to accept and how they react to being asked for a deposit: a factory with real cash-flow confidence is generally comfortable with reasonable terms, while a factory under pressure tends to push for faster payment than the market requires. And look at whether the factory pays its own suppliers on time, which you can often learn through the conversations you have with the people who supply it.

None of this is conclusive, and no buyer should present it as conclusive. But a business with a long operating history, owned premises, a diversified order book and sensible payment terms is very unlikely to disappear mid-programme, and that is the question the check is actually answering.

Check 4-6: Certification Scope, Tooling Ownership and Quality History

Check 4: Does the certification actually cover your article? Every factory in this industry holds certifications, and the certificate on the wall is often held for a different product, a different material, or a different factory within the group. The check is not "do you have OEKO-TEX" but "is there a certificate number that covers this specific article, with the right product class, and can we see the scope"?

Ask for the certificate number and look it up rather than taking a photograph of a document. Check the certificate holder's name against the legal entity on the proforma invoice. Check the product class — for OEKO-TEX Standard 100, Class 1 is for baby articles, Class 2 for articles with direct skin contact, and the class matters enormously for hair accessories and childrenswear. Check the issue date and the validity, because a lapsed certificate is a common and serious problem. And ask whether the certificate covers the mill or the trading entity; in our industry that distinction is the difference between a compliant product and a compliance claim.

Similar logic applies to BSCI, SEDEX, ISO 9001 and FSC. Each is a real thing, each has a scope, and each scope narrows what it means. A buyer who treats "we are BSCI audited" as a summary answer is accepting a weaker statement than the one they could have had. At Smith Ribbon we hold OEKO-TEX, FSC, BSCI, SEDEX, ISO 9001 and SMETA, and we give buyers the certificate numbers to check rather than the logos, because a logo proves very little and a number proves a great deal.

Check 5: Who owns the tooling, screens, dies and designs? This is the question that most often causes trouble two years into a programme, and it is almost never raised at the start. If your brand's printed ribbon requires four screens, and the factory made those screens, who owns them? If the relationship ends, do the screens leave with the factory? Is there a documented agreement that the designs, the artwork and the tooling belong to you?

The same question applies to the design itself. If a factory has developed a jacquard pattern for you, and the relationship ends, that pattern may be in the factory's portfolio, and it may not be unusual for it to appear on another customer's ribbon. This is not a hypothetical risk; it happens, and it is one of the more common sources of unpleasant endings. Put the ownership position in writing at the beginning, when it is a minor administrative point and not a confrontation. The reasonable expectation, and the one we hold ourselves to, is that your brand's designs and the tooling developed for them belong to you, that the factory is manufacturing to your design, and that the arrangement is documented.

Check 6: What is the factory's actual quality history? Ask for three specific things: what went wrong recently and how it was resolved, what the defect rate on the last production run was, and whether the factory performs its own incoming and outgoing inspection or relies on the customer to find problems. Then compare the answer to the quote. A factory with no recorded defects on a long programme is either exceptionally good or not measuring. A factory that reports a modest defect rate, explains how it measures it, and describes what it did about the last one is a factory with a functioning quality system.

The revealing question is usually about a failed lot. Every factory has had one. The question is not whether it happened but whether they tell you about it, whether they understood the cause, and whether the corrective action changed something. A supplier who cannot name a specific past failure, who responds with a general assurance about quality, is telling you that the concept has not been tested.

And Check the Correction Path. Ask what happens if a shipment is rejected. Who inspects it, at whose cost, in what time frame, and what remedy applies? A factory with a written, referenced claim procedure — a third-party inspection report, a pre-shipment inspection, a documented AQL — is telling you that it expects problems and has a process for them. This is a more meaningful quality signal than any certification, because it is the part of quality that only shows up when something is wrong.

Check 7-10: Business Continuity, Management Depth, Communication and Price Structure

Check 7: What happens if something disrupts this factory? A supply chain has to be designed for the assumption that something will go wrong: a fire, a flood, a pandemic lockdown, a power outage through a hot summer, a port closure, a key person being unreachable. Ask directly what the business continuity plan is, and what the alternatives are for your specific programme if a major disruption occurs. A factory with a named second source for its own critical inputs, a documented recovery sequence, and adequate insurance is answering from experience. A factory that has never had to execute the plan will describe one in the future tense, and you can hear the difference.

The related check is the one most buyers skip: is this factory the only one you have for this programme, and what would it take to move? If the answer is that the ribbon is entirely custom and the setup belongs to the factory, then you do not have a second source and your real leverage is different from what you may think. Know this before you commit, not afterwards.

Check 8: How deep is the management? Ask to meet the person who will run your programme, not only the salesperson. In a factory of a couple of hundred people, the difference between a one-person sales operation covering everything and a structure with a dedicated production manager and quality function is large. If the person who takes your order is also the person who handles complaints, the person who handles complaints and the person who manages the weaving floor, then a single absence becomes a programme risk. And ask who covers for them during holiday periods — Chinese New Year is a period when factory attendance drops and management attention is harder to obtain. Programmes that must ship in January need this settled in advance.

Check 9: How does the factory actually communicate? This is a soft check, but it is predictive. Send a technical question that requires some thought — not a price request, but a question about shrinkage, tolerance, or which test method they would use for a specific application — and observe the answer. A good factory replies with substance, because it has the knowledge. A weak factory replies with a price sheet. Then send a follow-up on a non-urgent issue a week later and see whether it is answered. The response rate to a non-urgent question is one of the more reliable indicators of how quickly a real problem will be communicated to you.

Also observe the speed of quotation. A factory that takes three weeks to quote a straightforward brief is telling you something about its order flow, and a factory that quotes in two days is telling you something too.

Check 10: How is the price structured? A price is more informative than it looks. Ask for the price broken down: material, decoration, finishing, setup or development charge, packing, and freight. A supplier who will show the structure is telling you that they understand where the cost sits, and often that they have been asked to quote against a competitive structure before. A supplier who returns a single number and resists a breakdown is either hiding a margin recovery in an unusual place or operating without the cost visibility to explain their own price.

Then check the three commercial terms separately, because each carries its own risk. The setup or development charge tells you how much of the programme is a fixed cost — and therefore how the price behaves as volume grows. The minimum order quantity tells you how much of your plan the supplier controls. And the payment terms tell you how much risk the supplier is carrying on your behalf. A supplier asking for 100 per cent payment before production has a different risk profile from one asking for 30 per cent deposit and 70 per cent against the bill of lading, and the difference is worth understanding even if it is not worth fighting over.

Reading a Quotation for Hidden Risk

Once the checks are done, the quotations themselves carry more information than most buyers extract from them. Three patterns are worth watching.

The price that is too low. A quotation meaningfully below every other in the comparison is not a gift. It is a signal that something has been omitted from the specification it was quoted against — a certification, a test, a lining, a finishing step, or a margin that will have to be recovered later through a variation order. The correct response is not to accept it but to ask what is different about it, in detail, and to compare specifications rather than prices. Two quotations for different products cannot be compared, and comparing them is how buyers end up with a product that is not what they specified but is priced as though it were.

The quote with no setup charge. No setup charge usually means the setup is being amortised into the per-metre price, which is fine and often preferable, but it also means the price will not reduce at volume, because there is no fixed component to spread. Ask what happens at ten times the volume. A supplier whose price does not move at all as volume multiplies either has a very good reason or has not thought about it.

The quote with no minimum order stated. A quotation without an MOQ is usually a quotation that has not been costed properly, or one where the MOQ will appear later. A real factory knows the minimum at which its setup economics work and will state it, because stating it is what makes the quote meaningful.

And there is one more thing worth requesting from every factory you are considering: the actual lead time for your programme, broken into stages, with the stage that has the least slack identified. A factory that shows you a schedule with float built into it is telling you it manages its own dates. A factory that quotes a single total is telling you something else, and you will find out which it is at the point where a delay matters most.

Red Flags, Green Flags and the Questions That Separate Them

Red flags. A factory that cannot or will not answer a technical question. A certification with no verifiable number. A refusal to discuss tooling ownership. Quality claims with no measurement behind them. A quotation that does not itemise. A refusal to be visited, or a visit arranged without access to the production floor. Payment terms that demand more money up front than the relationship justifies. A communication pattern that disappears after the order is placed. Enthusiasm that is notably higher than the level of detail available when you ask a hard question.

Green flags. A factory that answers the hard question before you have asked it. Certificate numbers offered without being requested. A willingness to discuss what went wrong on a previous order. A schedule with visible float. An engineer joining a commercial call without being prompted. A refusal to take an order at a volume that would not work for them — because they know where their own economics break. And a factory that tells you when your specification is wrong, or when your plan will not work as designed.

That last one is worth dwelling on. A supplier who tells you that your idea will not work, that the quantity is below the point where your chosen construction makes sense, that the decoration method you want will not survive the end use, or that you are better off buying a plain ribbon than the decorated one you specified, is telling you something that no certificate can tell you. It means the supplier is thinking about your programme's survival rather than about the next order. Over two decades we have found that buyers who value that candour are the ones who stay for the life of the programme, and it is the relationship we most want to be part of.

Building a Two-Supplier Strategy That Actually Works

The safest structure for a serious private-label ribbon programme is not two factories fighting over the same order. It is a primary supplier with the capability, capacity and tooling to run the programme, and a qualified second source that is genuinely ready to step in — which means the second source has been set up, not merely identified.

For a second source to be real, three things must be true. The specification must be transferable, which means it must be written rather than conversational — the specification discipline is not only about inspection, it is what makes a second source possible at all. The second source must have made the product before, or be prepared to run a trial order into the qualification before you need it, because a supplier who has never made your ribbon cannot make it in a hurry. And the quality parameters must be defined in a shared standard, which is why the approved-sample-and-revision discipline matters commercially as well as technically.

The practical sequencing that works is: qualify a second source during the first programme year, at a modest volume, on a non-critical SKU. Then a second source is not a contingency, it is a demonstrated fact, and the first source knows it. That knowledge is itself a form of insurance, because it changes the economics of every negotiation that follows.

At Smith Ribbon we are a single-site manufacturer rather than a multi-plant group, and we think that is the right structure for what we do — one site means the weaving, dyeing, printing and finishing are all visible to us and to our customers, and a problem in one process is a problem we solve rather than a problem we hand to another company. We also think buyers should know the value of a genuinely qualified second source, and that the right way to create one is to transfer a complete written specification to another mill and prove it can be built from the document alone. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I check whether a ribbon factory is financially stable?

Use proxies rather than financial statements, which are rarely shared at this stage. Look at how long the business has operated under the same name, whether the factory owns its premises, whether the order book is diversified or dominated by one customer, and what payment terms they propose. A factory asking for 100 per cent payment before production, or insisting on unusually short terms relative to the market, is signalling cash-flow pressure. A long-established, owner-occupied factory with a broad customer base and conventional terms is very unlikely to disappear mid-programme. None of this is conclusive, and no buyer should treat it as such, but combined it substantially narrows the risk.

What does a ribbon factory need in order to quote accurately?

A specification that removes the ambiguity, and that is why the discipline of writing one is worth the effort rather than just tidier. At minimum the factory needs: the construction and process route, the base material and fibre content, the width and its tolerance, the weight or thickness, the weave and surface, the colour with its reference and tolerance, the decoration with its process and artwork status, the edge treatment, the finishing operations, the packing and carton configuration, and the delivery terms. With those in place the quote is a precise document. Without them, the factory will quote its standard product, which is a reasonable thing for it to do and rarely what the buyer wanted.

Should I require a factory visit before placing a first order?

Yes, and specifically a visit that includes the production floor rather than the reception room. The things worth seeing are whether the claimed in-house processes are visibly present and running, whether the working conditions are orderly, whether there is a quality inspection point in the line rather than a final inspection at the end of it, and whether the people on the floor appear to be working on more than one customer at once, which is a practical indicator of capacity. A visit also changes the relationship in a way that a factory tour video never does, because the buyer's seriousness becomes visible to the people who will manage the programme.

How many ribbon suppliers should a brand have?

For most private-label programmes, one primary supplier that runs the programme properly plus one qualified second source is the right structure. The second source only counts as real if the specification is transferable, if the second factory has made the product before, and if the quality parameters are defined in a shared standard. A list of ten factories you have emailed is not a second source; it is a list of factories you have emailed. The most useful time to qualify the second source is during the first programme year, on a non-critical SKU at modest volume, so that when it is needed the capability is demonstrated rather than assumed.

What is the most important question to ask a ribbon supplier?

The most revealing one is usually about a past failure: which lot went wrong recently, what caused it, and what changed afterwards. Every factory has had a failed lot. The difference between suppliers is whether they can name one, whether they understood the cause rather than the symptom, and whether the corrective action actually altered a process. A supplier that responds with a general assurance about quality has not been tested. The closely related question, and one that separates real capability from sales ability, is which of your proposed specifications the factory believes will not work and why.

Does a factory holding BSCI or ISO 9001 guarantee quality?

No, and it would be wrong to treat it that way. BSCI is a social compliance audit framework covering working conditions and labour practices; ISO 9001 is a quality management system certification, which verifies that the factory has documented processes and runs an internal audit, not that your product will meet your tolerance. Both are worth having, and both are worth verifying — check the certificate holder against the legal entity on your proforma invoice, check the validity dates, and check the scope. The quality assurance that actually protects your programme is a written specification with measurable acceptance criteria, a documented approval process, and a stated claim procedure. At Smith Ribbon we hold OEKO-TEX, FSC, BSCI, SEDEX, ISO 9001 and SMETA, and we give buyers certificate numbers to verify rather than logos to admire.
