Why Shortlists Usually Fail at the Scoring Step

Most shortlists fail in the same way. Everyone on the list is scored on the criteria that are easy to measure, and the criteria that decide whether the programme works are left until after an award has been made. Scoring should not reward a supplier for sending a complete quotation. It should tell you which supplier you can place a repeat order with, and whether the number in the quotation is a number you can plan against.

There are two distinct failures that get conflated. The first is a scoring failure, where the criteria chosen do not reflect the risk you are actually carrying. The second is a cost validity failure, where the numbers being scored are not comparable because the underlying specifications, volumes or cost scopes differ. A scorecard can be perfectly well designed and still produce a wrong answer if the costs being scored are not valid.

Both failures are invisible at the point of award, which is what makes them expensive. You discover a scoring failure when the programme misses a season. You discover a cost validity failure when the reorder price is higher than the quote you approved, or when the supplier you chose cannot hold the specification you assumed you had bought.

Building Criteria That Match Your Actual Risk

Start from the programme you are actually running, not from a generic supplier scorecard library. The criteria that matter for a first programme in an unfamiliar category are different from those that matter for an extension with a proven supplier, and the weights should differ accordingly.

Six criteria cover the practical territory for ribbon and trim OEM programmes, and each one should be defined by what evidence would convince you, not by a feeling.

  • Technical capability - can this factory produce this construction, width, decoration and finish at the volumes you need? Evidence: machine list with working ranges, plus relevant production records.
  • Quality and compliance - written standards, in-process control, certification scope covering your product, and evidence of corrective action that closes. Evidence: inspection records and certification documents with dates.
  • Capacity and lead time - not the stated ceiling but the demonstrated output, plus the flexibility available in your delivery window. Evidence: eight weeks of loading records.
  • Cost structure and transparency - can the price be decomposed into components, and are the assumptions stated. Evidence: a line-by-line build-up with volume assumption.
  • Commercial terms - payment terms, Incoterm, price validity period, tooling ownership, lead time commitment in writing.
  • Communication and service behaviour - responsiveness during the quotation process, clarity of the technical response, how they handled a question they could not answer immediately.

Define each criterion before you collect any scores, and write down the evidence each score must cite. This prevents the most common scoring failure of all: the criteria quietly collapsing in importance because one supplier happened to answer one of them more conveniently than the others.

Testing Whether a Quoted Cost Is Valid

A quoted cost is valid if it means the same thing as every other quoted cost on the table. Three tests establish that, and all three must pass before the numbers are allowed anywhere near a scorecard.

Test one: identical specification

Every mandatory field in your request for quotation must be priced by every supplier, at the same level of detail. If supplier A has priced a 10mm printed polyester satin ribbon and supplier B has priced a 12mm woven grosgrain, no normalisation method makes those numbers comparable. The quotation template must mark fields as mandatory and require the supplier to confirm they have priced against the specification as issued. A supplier who cannot price a mandatory field has given you a shortlist data point, not raised a clarification query.

Test two: identical quantity and volume assumption

A unit price at 1,000 metres and a unit price at 50,000 metres are different products. Ask every supplier to quote a defined quantity, and critically, ask what their price becomes at your realistic annual volume, not just your trial order. The gap between trial order pricing and production pricing is often where the actual programme economics live, and it is frequently not visible in the first quotation.

Test three: identical scope and price basis

Confirm the Incoterm, the freight assumption, the packaging scope and the currency in every quotation. A comparison between an EXW price and a delivered price is not a comparison, and one that happens frequently enough to be worth naming explicitly. If quotations arrive on different bases, request them again on a common basis rather than adjusting them yourself - your normalisation will not match the supplier's, and you will lose the argument later.

Making Two Quotations Actually Comparable

Once the three tests pass, the comparison can be done properly. The method below is a normalised landed cost per unit at your annual volume, which is the only figure worth ranking.

For each supplier, build a single per-unit number that includes: the quoted unit price at your annual volume, amortised setup and tooling divided across the number of orders you realistically place per year, colour development amortised the same way, the testing and certification costs you will pay once per programme, packaging costs, inbound freight and duty, and the expected quality loss rate applied to your own sell-through volume.

That last line is the one most scorecards omit and it deserves attention. A supplier with a one percent defect rate at a 50,000 metre annual volume is not a cheaper supplier than one at two percent - they are a supplier who will send you replacement units, and the replacement is your cost, not theirs, unless your contract says otherwise. If your expected loss rate differs between suppliers, you are comparing different products and you need to normalise it.

Present the result as a table with the components visible, not as a single ranked number. The ranking tells you who appears cheapest; the component table tells you why, and the why is what you negotiate against. If two suppliers land within a few percent of each other, the difference is inside the noise of the assumptions, and you should choose on capability rather than pretend the arithmetic decided it.

The Cost Lines That Sit Outside the Quotation

Certain costs reliably appear after the award rather than in the quotation. Not because suppliers are being dishonest - often because the cost genuinely is not in the factory's scope - but because nobody asked for them. These are the lines to request explicitly in the request for quotation, so that any supplier who cannot include them tells you so on day one.

  • Decoration tooling and setup - screen preparation, plates, foil tooling, die cutting. Sometimes a one-time fee, sometimes recurring, sometimes folded invisibly into the unit price. Ask for it as a named line and ask whether it recurs on reorder.
  • Colour development and lab dips - the number of lab dips included, the charge per additional dip, and whether a dip becomes a chargeable colour once approved.
  • Samples and prototypes - including the cost of shipping them and of making samples that are never ordered.
  • Packaging development - polybag, carton, liner, labels. Often assumed to be included; frequently charged.
  • Testing and certification - chemical compliance testing, colour fastness, flammability. If a retailer audit requires a specific test report, know who pays and when.
  • Freight and insurance beyond the Incoterm - and the cost of the first shipments where volume is too low to fill a container.
  • Rework and sorting - what the factory assumes it absorbs, and what it will pass back to you. If the answer is vague, add it to the contract as a defined cost rather than a goodwill item.
  • Working capital cost - the gap between payment terms and the point at which you can sell the goods. At a 30 per cent deposit, 70 per cent against documents and 60 day retail terms, a programme can carry meaningful financing cost that never appears in any quotation.

Ask for the full build-up. A supplier who cannot decompose a price is telling you something important, and that something is more valuable than the price.

Weighting Criteria Against Your Programme Type

Weights encode your risk position, and they should change with the programme. A defensible pattern for a first order in a new category: technical capability and quality 30 per cent each, capacity 20 per cent, cost structure 12 per cent, commercial terms 5 per cent, communication 3 per cent. Cost is deliberately not the largest number, because at that stage you do not yet know whether the low price reflects efficiency or a different scope.

For an extension with a proven supplier, capacity and commercial terms can rise to 25 per cent each, technical capability can fall to 20, and cost structure can rise to 20 - because the operational risk is now understood and priced, and the negotiation question has shifted to unit economics and terms. Using one weighting for both situations systematically under-weights the risk you are actually carrying, which is the most common reason a scorecard produces a decision the buyer does not believe.

One rule worth defending: if any criterion scores 1 out of 5, it is a veto regardless of the total. A supplier with excellent capability and unacceptable compliance records should not win a weighted scorecard, because the compliance risk is not a matter of degree - it is a gate. Scorecards that allow averaging away a veto are the reason compliance gaps surface after an award instead of before it.

The Scoring Sheet and How to Use It

A practical sheet has one row per criterion and one column per supplier, with three additional columns that are easy to omit and hard to do without: the weight, the evidence citation for each score, and the open questions each supplier still owes you.

Run the scoring with the people who will live with the result, not with procurement alone. A score decided by a single buyer is a score without an institutional memory. The two-hour session where a category manager, a quality engineer and a merchandising lead each place a score against the same evidence is worth more than any spreadsheet refinement, because the disagreement it surfaces is usually about a criterion that nobody had weighted properly.

Finish by writing the open questions column. Every supplier will have two or three items unresolved - a volume assumption not confirmed, a certification scope that does not clearly cover your construction, a lead time offered informally. Collect those questions, send them in writing, and make the answers part of the award record. A supplier who answers all three clearly within a week is telling you something about how they will handle a quality escalation in year two.

Signals That Should Override the Score

A few signals justify departing from the arithmetic.

  1. A price far below the field. An outlier twenty to thirty per cent under comparable quotes usually indicates a different specification or a different service scope, not a better factory. Diagnose before celebrating.
  2. Quotations that cannot be decomposed. If a supplier will not state the volume assumption or the cost build-up, the price is not a plan you can rely on at reorder.
  3. Certification that does not cover your product. A certificate for the wrong fibre, the wrong construction or a narrower product scope than yours is not partial compliance, it is no compliance.
  4. Unwillingness to commit a lead time in writing. A verbal lead time is a hope. A committed date with a defined remedy is a term you can plan against and, if needed, enforce.
  5. Terms that do not match the risk. A new supplier requesting full prepayment plus no defect remedy transfers the two largest risks in the programme to you at the moment you know least about them.
  6. A shortlist with no credible runner-up. If there is no second supplier you could actually award, you have a dependency rather than a choice, and it should change how you write the contract.

Once the award is made, keep the scorecard, the evidence file and the open-questions record together for the life of the programme. A year later, when prices move or a delivery slips, the question that matters is not who was cheapest but who was best on the criteria you said mattered - and that answer is only available if you wrote it down while you still had the evidence in front of you.
