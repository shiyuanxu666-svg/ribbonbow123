Why Colour Fastness Is a Specification Problem, Not a Quality Complaint

Every ribbon programme eventually has a buyer who opens a carton, dampens a ribbon, and asks the supplier a question that has no answer in the purchase order. They ask why the colour ran. Or they ask why the ribbon shrank. Or they ask why the red has faded to pink after three washes of a pet collar. Each of these is a reasonable question and each of them is unanswerable, because the specification that was agreed said nothing about wet behaviour, exposure limits, or what the standard test actually was.

The reason colour fastness sits in this awkward position is that it is invisible at the point of purchase. A buyer can assess width, weight, handle, edge treatment and print registration on a sample swatch without any equipment. Fastness requires a wash, a rub, a light exposure and an instrument to read the result. So it does not get assessed during sampling, and if it is not written into the order, it is never assessed at all. The failure then surfaces in the market, often months later, in the form of returns from a customer the buyer no longer controls.

This guide sets out what colour fastness actually means for polyester and satin ribbon, which test methods a mill can run and a buyer can reasonably specify, how to convert those test numbers into a written standard that a mill will work to, and where the practical limits of dyed polyester sit. It is written for brand owners and procurement managers specifying decorative ribbon for gift packaging, apparel trim, home textiles, pet accessories and seasonal programmes, where the ribbon is frequently the visible brand surface and where a colour failure is a brand failure.

What Colour Fastness Actually Measures

Colour fastness is a measure of how much a colour changes when the material is put through a defined stress. The number alone is meaningless; the stress is what gives it meaning. This is why two suppliers can report "fastness 4" and one of them is wrong. The rating almost always comes from a standard method, and the standard specifies the exposure, the instrument, and the way the colour change is judged. Two different methods can produce different ratings for the identical ribbon.

Four exposure types account for nearly all ribbon fastness work, and they should be treated as separate requirements because they stress the dye in different ways:

  • Wash fastness. The ribbon is washed in a specified detergent, at a specified temperature, for a specified number of cycles, and the colour change is judged against a grey scale. This is the relevant test for anything that will be laundered or that will meet water in service: apparel trim, home textile, pet accessories, and ribbon used on packaging that will be handled in a wet environment.
  • Rub fastness. The ribbon is rubbed against a standard abrasive surface, dry or wet, a set number of times, and the colour transferred to the abradent is judged. This is the test that matters for ribbon that will be handled. A ribbon wrapped around a bottle and shipped, or used in a gift box that is repeatedly opened and closed, will abrade at the fold and edge.
  • Light fastness. The material is exposed to controlled light, usually an accelerated xenon arc, and the colour change is judged after a stated exposure in hours. This is the relevant test for ribbon in permanent display: retail fixture trim, gift set bows that sit on a shelf, seasonal decoration that is displayed in a lit window, and any ribbon used as an in-store branding element.
  • Perspiration and dry-cleaning fastness. For apparel and skin-contact applications, the ribbon is exposed to an acid or alkaline perspiration solution or to a dry-cleaning solvent. Many polyester dyes are stable to these, but printed ribbons and certain disperse dye shades are not, and a ribbon used next to skin should be tested for the specific exposure it will meet.

There is also a fifth measurement that is not strictly a fastness property but is usually discussed alongside them, because it produces the same commercial outcome: dimensional stability. A ribbon that holds its colour perfectly but shrinks eight percent in a wash has failed just as badly in the finished article. For a buyer specifying ribbon for any washable or moisture-exposed application, shrinkage should be specified in the same clause as colour, not as a separate afterthought.

Choosing a Method That Both Sides Can Agree On

The practical problem with fastness specification is that the mill and the buyer must be talking about the same test. This is where the ISO and AATCC standards earn their place, because they specify the exposure conditions, the equipment and the assessment scale in a way that any competent laboratory can reproduce.

For ribbon work, the most commonly used references are the ISO series for wash, light and rub fastness, and the AATCC series covering the same ground with a more retail-oriented application focus. The key requirement for a buyer is not to become an expert in textile testing but to specify the standard number and the exposure level, and to require that the report states which method was used. A mill that reports "fastness 4 to ISO 105-C06, 30°C domestic wash" and a mill that reports "fastness 4 to AATCC 61, 5 washes" have given you two comparable numbers. A mill that reports "fastness 4" with no method has given you a number you cannot use to write a claim clause, and if a claim does arise you will be unable to prove anything.

Three decisions need to be made in the specification, and they are worth making deliberately rather than accepting a default:

  • The exposure severity. Most wash methods offer severity options, from a mild domestic wash through to a severe or very severe cycle, and each has a defined temperature, agitation and detergent dose. Choosing a severity is choosing the real-world conditions the ribbon will face. A ribbon for a machine-washable pet collar should be tested at domestic severity with a defined number of cycles, because that is the condition it will meet in service. A ribbon for a gift box that may occasionally contact a damp hand needs a much lower bar, and specifying a severe cycle would push the cost of the dye programme up for no commercial benefit.
  • The number of cycles. A single wash says very little. Most meaningful ribbon specifications call for three to five cycles, because most colour failures that matter in retail emerge progressively rather than on the first wash. A ribbon that passes the first wash and fades by the fourth is a common and entirely predictable failure mode, and it is only caught if the specification asks for multiple cycles.
  • The assessment scale. Fastness is normally graded on a scale where 5 is the best and 1 is the worst, with a half-step. Most buyers specify a minimum grade of 4 for wash fastness on visible trim, and 3 for applications where the ribbon is in a folded or covered position. The grade must be evaluated against a specified reference, and the reference must be stated, because a grade is a judgement made by an examiner and examiners differ.

A further refinement that costs nothing to specify and removes a common source of argument is to require that the test be run on the finished decorated ribbon rather than on the greige base. A solid dye lot that performs well can still bleed or crack when it is printed, when it is foil-stamped, or when it is edge-cut, because the decoration process adds heat, tension and chemistry that the base dye was never tested under.

What Ratings Are Realistic for Dyed Polyester Ribbon

Buyers sometimes write specifications that no mill can meet, then treat the resulting samples as failures of diligence rather than as a specification error. It is worth knowing where the realistic performance boundaries sit for polyester ribbon so that a specification is demanding but achievable.

Polyester, and the disperse dyes used on it, is generally a strong performer. This is one of the practical advantages of polyester over natural fibre ribbons, and it is a legitimate reason to specify it for washable or moisture-exposed applications. Disperse dyes on polyester typically achieve wash fastness grades of 4 to 4-5 under domestic severity conditions, and light fastness grades of 5 to 6 under moderate exposure. Rub fastness is more dependent on construction than on dye, and satin constructions with a high float length will abrade more readily than matte polyester grosgrain or woven constructions with a tight weave.

Two situations reliably pull performance down, and both should be anticipated in the specification rather than discovered in testing. The first is deep, saturated shades. Very dark navy, deep burgundy, black and strong primaries are harder to stabilise than mid-tones and pastels, and a mill quoting a grade of 4-5 to 5 across a range that includes both a pale blush and a saturated midnight blue is quoting a range, not a guarantee. The second is the effect of the finish. Softeners, lubricants, anti-static agents and water-repellent finishes applied after dyeing can all affect rub and wash performance, and some of them reduce it.

A practical recommendation is to require the mill to state, in the quotation, the fastness grades they hold for the specific colour and construction being quoted, on a scale, with the method named. A supplier who cannot state this is telling you something important about how their quality system works.

Dimensional Stability and the Shrinkage That Follows the Wash

Colour fastness and dimensional stability are separate properties with a common commercial failure mode. A buyer who specifies fastness but not shrinkage will receive ribbon that passes the wash test and then fails on the assembly line, because the ribbon is no longer the length that was ordered.

Shrinkage in polyester ribbon comes from three sources. Relaxation shrinkage is the most common and is a consequence of tension in the yarn and the fabric being held under strain during weaving and winding. When the ribbon is first exposed to water or heat, the fibre relaxes and the length changes, usually by a small percentage. Heat-setting shrinkage occurs when the ribbon is exposed to temperatures above its setting point, which in practice means ironing, heat-sealing, or passing through a hot gluing or laminating process. Wet relaxation is the combination of the two and is usually the largest component, because the material relaxes both thermally and because the water swells the fibre temporarily and it then sets in a changed state.

The specification practice that works is to state a percentage tolerance on length and width after a stated conditioning procedure, and to be explicit about the conditioning. A common and defensible approach for ribbon intended for washable or heat-processed applications is to specify that length change after one wash cycle at domestic severity, measured on a conditioned sample, shall not exceed a stated figure, commonly in the range of two to three percent for woven constructions and three to five for satin, where the higher float constructions generally show more dimensional movement.

The second dimension of this is what happens to a ribbon that is not dimensionally stable when it is used in a specific way. Ribbon that shrinks is a problem in three distinct situations. It is a problem when a continuous length is cut into a fixed-size trim and the pieces then vary in length, because the trimming operation was set for the original length. It is a problem when a bow is assembled from a fixed length and the resulting bow changes size, which matters most for retail display where consistency between units is visible. And it is a problem when ribbon is used as a size-bearing element, which is uncommon in decorative ribbon but relevant to elasticated and stretch trims where the elasticity itself is being specified.

Writing the Clause Into the Purchase Order

The value of all of the above is realised only when it appears in the order as an enforceable clause. A clause that names the method, the severity, the number of cycles, the assessment scale and the reporting requirement will be met without difficulty. A clause that says "good colour fastness" will be met in whatever way the mill chooses to interpret it, and the interpretation will not match the buyer's expectation.

A workable clause has five components. First, the standard and method, named with number and year where the buyer expects a specific revision. Second, the exposure conditions, meaning severity, temperature, cycle count and any agitation or solvent specification. Third, the minimum acceptable grade, on the stated scale. Fourth, the state of the sample tested, whether greige, dyed, or fully decorated and finished, because this materially changes the result. Fifth, the reporting requirement, which should specify that the report names the method, names the grade achieved, and identifies the lot tested, so that the evidence is traceable to the shipment that will be received.

Two further provisions are worth including and are routinely omitted. The first is a requirement that a new colour development is tested before the bulk run rather than after it. This removes the single most expensive failure mode in ribbon sourcing, which is a bulk run of several thousand metres in a colour that subsequently fails a wash test and cannot be sold. The second is a retest provision that specifies what happens when a lot fails, whether it may be reworked, whether the mill must investigate and report the cause, and what happens to the shipment in the interim.

It is worth noting how much of this specification work is a one-time investment. Fastness and shrinkage testing does not need to be repeated on every subsequent order against the same colour and construction. It needs to be repeated when the dye house, the yarn supplier, the construction, the finish or the decoration process changes. Recording those change points in the purchase agreement means the buyer pays for the testing once and then re-triggers it only when something has actually changed.

Sampling and Test Reporting That Actually Help

Fastness data is only commercially useful if it can be connected to the material that will actually arrive. This is a sampling question, and it is more often mishandled than the test itself.

The first requirement is that the tested sample comes from the production route, not from a separately produced laboratory sample. A mill that tests a hand-woven sample from the dyehouse and a bulk lot produced on different equipment has not tested the bulk lot. The sample should be drawn from production output, and ideally it should be drawn from a roll that the mill will actually ship, so that a claim can be traced back to a specific lot rather than to a general capability statement.

The second is that the report is legible. A buyer asking for a fastness report should receive, for each tested colour and construction, the method reference, the exposure conditions, the grade achieved, the date of testing, and the identification of the lot or batch number. A one-page PDF containing a table of grades with no lot identification provides no traceability and therefore no protection when a claim is raised months later.

The third is the retest and claim mechanic. When a buyer receives a shipment and suspects a fastness problem, the standard industry practice is to have the dispute material tested by an agreed independent laboratory, at a cost to be shared or allocated by the result. This clause needs to exist before it is needed, because the moment of a dispute is the worst possible time to be negotiating the terms of the test.

Practical Guidance by End Use

Pulling the general principles down to specific applications, the following guidance reflects what is typically both achievable and worth paying for.

For pet accessories, where ribbon is laundered or wiped regularly and often handles moisture, specify wash fastness at domestic severity across a minimum of three cycles at a minimum grade of 4, tested on the finished decorated ribbon, and add wet rub fastness because collars and harnesses take abrasion against fur and hardware. Add a light fastness requirement at moderate exposure if the product sits in a lit retail environment.

For home textiles and upholstery trim, where the ribbon is a permanent visual element and washing is occasional, the priorities shift. Light fastness becomes the primary requirement because the ribbon is displayed under sustained lighting, and a grade of 5 at moderate exposure is a reasonable target. Wash fastness can be specified at a lower requirement, but should still be specified, because consumers do wash soft furnishings and the trim is the part that shows.

For apparel trim, where the ribbon is part of a garment and may be dry cleaned or washed, the specification should cover both wash and dry-clean fastness, plus dimensional stability, because garment trims are usually cut to a fixed length and any shrinkage shows as an inconsistent finished garment. Perspiration fastness should be specified where the trim sits against skin.

For retail display and gift packaging, where the ribbon is handled more than it is washed, the priorities are rub fastness at the fold and edge, light fastness where the display is lit, and a dry rub requirement for the fold specifically, because the fold is where the abrasion concentrates. Colour fastness in this context is less about washing and more about the friction of the packaging being handled repeatedly in a retail environment.

For seasonal and Christmas programmes, the requirement is almost entirely light fastness, and the relevant exposure is long-term display rather than washing. Because these programmes ship in volume with a hard season deadline, the testing needs to be done early and on the actual colour, and the mill should be asked to hold the dye lot recipe on file for the following season, because re-matching a colour in the next year without the retained recipe is the single most common cause of a seasonal colour mismatch.

Preventing Failures Rather Than Detecting Them

As with every other quality attribute, the cheapest control is upstream. A buyer who has selected the fibre, the dye house and the finish in the right order will spend far less on testing than one who specifies a grade and then inspects the result.

The first control is to use the same construction for the same colourway wherever possible. The fastness rating of a colour is a property of the colour-plus-construction-plus-finish combination, not of the colour alone. A navy that passes at grade 5 on a matte woven polyester may not hold that grade on a satin with a different finish and a different float length. Treating the colourway as the unit of specification, rather than the colour, removes a category of mismatch that otherwise appears only in testing.

The second control is to insist that the mill retains the dye recipe, the yarn lot references and the process settings for the duration of the programme, so that a repeat order can be produced to the same result. Repeatability is the practical test of whether a fastness grade is real or whether it was a fortunate outcome on one lot.

The third is to align the buyer's own testing regime with the mill's, so that failures are found at goods-in rather than in the market. A buyer who tests every incoming lot against the same standard is building a record of performance by supplier and by colourway, and that record is what allows a future specification to be tightened with confidence rather than guessed at.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a good colour fastness grade for decorative ribbon?

For most decorative and branded ribbon applications, a minimum grade of 4 on the ISO or AATCC scale is a sensible default for wash fastness under domestic severity, and a grade of 5 or better for light fastness under moderate exposure where the ribbon is displayed for extended periods. The correct number nonetheless depends entirely on what the ribbon will actually experience. A ribbon used in a gift box that is opened and closed repeatedly, and never washed, needs a rub fastness requirement rather than a wash requirement, and specifying a demanding wash grade for it adds cost without protecting the buyer from the failure that will actually occur, which is abrasion at the fold. The right approach is to specify the property that matches the exposure, and to resist specifying every property at maximum severity simply because it is possible.

2. How many wash cycles should a ribbon be tested for?

Three cycles is the practical minimum for most washable applications, and five is better where the ribbon will see repeated laundering over its life. Single-cycle testing is close to meaningless for retail purposes, because colour failure in polyester dye systems is very often progressive rather than abrupt. A ribbon that passes one domestic wash and shows a visible shift by the third cycle will pass your specification and fail in the market, which is the specific failure a buyer is trying to avoid. The marginal cost of the additional cycles is small because the sample is the same sample and the labour is the same; the marginal cost of discovering the problem later is a returned or discounted product line.

3. Should fastness be tested on the greige ribbon or the finished decorated ribbon?

On the finished decorated ribbon, and this is worth specifying explicitly because the default assumption is often the other way round. Decoration introduces heat, tension and chemistry that the base material was never tested under. Printing, foil stamping, edge cutting, ultrasonic cutting and adhesive backing all change the material, and each can degrade a fastness result that looked excellent on the greige. Testing on the greige measures the dye, and the dye is not what the customer sees. The one legitimate exception is where the decoration is applied in a way that covers the fault entirely, and the buyer explicitly accepts the risk; that should be a conscious decision recorded in the specification, not an accidental default.

4. Can any mill achieve grade 5 on every colour?

Not every colour in every range, and a supplier who claims to is either quoting a narrower range than they will supply or describing capability rather than typical performance. Dark, saturated shades are consistently harder to stabilise than mid-tones and pastels, because a high concentration of disperse dye in the fibre is harder to fix and more vulnerable to surface loss under abrasion. The practical question to ask a mill is which grades they hold for which specific colours in the range you intend to buy, with the method named. A supplier who answers that question precisely with a short list and a documented test programme is more reliable than one who answers yes to everything.

5. Does ribbon shrinkage matter if the ribbon is never washed?

Sometimes, and the reason is not always washing. Heat-setting is the other major source of dimensional change, and a great deal of ribbon is exposed to heat without ever seeing water: through hot-melt or ultrasonic bonding during assembly, through ironing or heat pressing during garment or gift production, through the dwell of a laminating or packaging process, and through transport in a hot vehicle where a tightly wound roll can take a set. A buyer whose assembly process applies heat should specify a shrinkage or heat-stability requirement even for a ribbon that will never be washed, because the assembly line is where the length will change and where the cost of discovering it is highest.

6. How should a buyer handle a fastness claim on a delivered lot?

By having the process written down before it is needed. The purchase order should state which method and severity governs the claim, that dispute material is tested by an agreed independent laboratory, how the cost is allocated depending on the result, and the commercial remedy for each outcome, which typically covers replacement, credit or rework for a confirmed failure. This clause is a standard part of a quality agreement and costs nothing to include. It is worth remembering that fastness claims are unusual, and when they do occur they are usually a specification problem rather than a supplier problem, which is another reason to keep the specification, the reports and the samples.

7. Does the order of testing matter when several tests are needed?

Yes, and it is a detail that catches suppliers out. Each test consumes or alters the sample. A ribbon that has been rubbed, washed and light-exposed cannot then be tested for shrinkage, because the conditioning history is no longer the one the shrinkage standard assumes. The correct practice, and it is standard in textile testing, is to take a separate specimen for each test from the same lot, and to specify that in the testing instruction. Any report should identify each specimen and its test. A single specimen used for all four tests will produce a fastness result that is technically defensible and practically unreliable, and it is worth a buyer checking this on a report that arrives suspiciously complete.

8. Why does the same colour sometimes fail on the next order?

Almost always because something upstream changed and was not recorded. The usual causes are a change of yarn supplier or yarn lot, a change of dye house or a different dye lot within the same dye house, a change in the finish or softener, a different loom or a different construction, or a change in the decoration process. None of these is unusual in an ongoing programme, and none is inherently a problem, but each of them can move a fastness result. This is precisely why the retained dye recipe and process settings matter, and why the specification should state what changes trigger a retest. A supplier who can explain a result change and adjust the process in response is doing the job properly; a supplier who cannot is the one you need to watch.
