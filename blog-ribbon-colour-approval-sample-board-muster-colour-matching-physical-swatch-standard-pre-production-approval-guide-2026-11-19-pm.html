Ribbon Colour Approval - Sample Boards, Physical Swatches and the Colour Matching Standard (2026)

Published: November 19, 2026  |  By Smith Ribbon  |  B2B Quality and Compliance

A colour approved from a photograph is not a colour approval. A ribbon sample board, used as a controlled physical standard and referenced in the purchase order, is what makes a shade defensible months later - and it costs almost nothing to do properly.

Buying custom ribbon for a multi-colour programme means buying colour, and colour is the one thing in this industry that cannot be described in writing and nailed in a photograph. A screen shows a shade under one light source, on one device, in one colour profile. The ribbon arrives under a warehouse light, on a shelf, next to other materials, and it looks like a different colour altogether. The sample board is the tool that closes that gap, and most buyers treat it as a disposable step in the approval process rather than as a controlled document that has to survive the whole programme.

This guide covers how to build a ribbon sample board that still matches your production two years later, how to run a physical colour comparison properly, what to record on the board itself, and how to prevent the approval from quietly drifting.

Why a Photograph Fails as a Colour Reference

The instinct to approve a colour from a photo is understandable. It is fast, it costs nothing, and it fits into an email thread. It is also unreliable in a specific, predictable way.

A photograph of ribbon captures the interaction between a light source and a surface, then compresses that result into numbers that assume a particular display profile. The same ribbon under a different light, on a different phone, or on a laptop with brightness at 70 percent will produce visibly different pixels. On screen, a saturated red satin can appear closer to orange; a cool grey grosgrain can appear blue. More importantly, the eye is extremely good at adjusting to a light source. When you look at a physical swatch under a cool fluorescent tube, your brain partially compensates and you see the colour as warmer than it really is. In a photograph, that compensation does not happen, and neither does the adaptation when you move the swatch to daylight.

There is a second failure mode that costs more. Screens are emissive: they make their own light. Physical ribbon is reflective: it returns light from the room. This is the fundamental reason no photograph of a material can substitute for the material, and it applies to silk, velvet, metallic, and anything with a sheen. A metallic ribbon that looks brilliant gold on a screen can look bronze under a shop's downlights, because the specular highlight behaves completely differently once the light has a real source and a real angle.

None of this means a sample board is optional. It means the sample board is the standard, and everything else is a convenience that you should not use as the final record.

What a Working Ribbon Sample Board Contains

A sample board is a physical archive of the shades your programme depends on. It needs to hold material from several different production runs and survive being handled, shipped between offices, and pulled out of a drawer a year later when a new order lands.

The physical construction

The board itself matters less than how the ribbon is fixed to it, but the fixation is where most home-made boards fail. Three options work, in descending order of durability:

  • Mounted permanently with adhesive tape across the full width. The most secure method. Sample tape on both ends, leaving the middle of the swatch free. Never use a single point of attachment across the top edge, because ribbon flexes and will lift at the corners over time.
  • Folded into a card pocket. Good for short swatches and for travelling. The fold must be at one end only, so the swatch can be unfolded to full width.
  • Loose in an envelope. Acceptable only for reference and only for short periods. A loose swatch loses its association with the label the moment it is handled.

Whatever the method, every swatch needs to be physically cut to a consistent size, usually around 50 by 100 millimetres. Consistent size matters for a reason that is not obvious: it makes the swatch wide enough to fold and self-support, and it lets you stack two swatches and compare the edges for shade difference. A narrow strip cannot be compared this way because the eye needs a broad adjacent area to judge the shift.

What to attach to each swatch

The label is the part of the board that carries the specification. A handwritten label on masking tape will fade, bleed or be lost. Use a printed label that is attached to the board, not to the ribbon, so the swatch can be removed without losing its identity.

Each swatch should record at minimum:

  • Shade name and internal code, matching the code used in orders and purchase orders
  • Material and construction: polyester satin, grosgrain, organza, velvet, metallic woven, and so on
  • Width in millimetres or inches
  • Supplier batch or lot reference, if known
  • Date the swatch was produced and the date it was received
  • Which side is the face, if the ribbon is reversible or the face differs from the reverse

Add a physical reference scale or a printed shade card alongside if the programme involves fine colour differences below what an untrained eye can reliably separate. This is not about precision for its own sake. The purpose is to give the person comparing swatches a reference that makes them slower and more careful, which is exactly what colour disputes need.

Running a Physical Colour Comparison Properly

A comparison done badly produces a wrong result with total confidence, which is worse than not comparing at all. The procedure below takes about ten minutes and removes most of the variables that cause avoidable disputes.

Control the light

Use daylight-balanced light with a colour temperature around 5000 kelvin, or natural daylight from a north-facing window. Avoid the two commonest mistakes: direct sunlight, which is variable and has a warm cast, and warm domestic lighting, which pushes everything towards yellow and makes cool shades look wrong.

If you only have the lighting in the office, hold the comparison anyway, but record what lighting you used. A shade approved under warm office lighting and rejected under the buyer's cool warehouse lighting is a process failure, not a manufacturing failure, and the record is what lets you prove that.

Control the surroundings

Place a neutral background behind the swatch, ideally a mid-grey card. A white background makes every shade look darker and more saturated; a black background makes every shade look lighter and duller. The same swatch against both will show two apparently different colours to an inexperienced observer.

Keep the comparison surface clear of other ribbon. Two swatches sitting next to each other on a table are compared against each other rather than against the target, and the eye adapts to the pair. This is called simultaneous contrast, and it is why a shade can look correct when held alone and wrong when placed next to the rest of the palette.

View it the way it will be used

Hold the swatch at a distance of roughly 30 to 50 centimetres. This is a normal viewing distance for a person examining ribbon in a shop or on a table. Looking at a swatch from a few centimetres away exaggerates texture and can hide a shade shift, or exaggerate the texture and make you reject a shade that is perfectly acceptable in use.

Where the ribbon will be used against a specific background colour, such as on a dark bottle against a printed carton, compare it in that combination. Many shade disagreements resolve immediately once the swatch is placed in the actual assembly rather than viewed in isolation on a desk.

Use a viewing frame if the differences are fine

A simple card with a rectangular window about 20 by 30 millimetres lets you compare the swatch through the aperture. The window masks the surrounding context and puts the two areas of colour side by side with no distraction. This is the standard method used in textile inspection and it costs almost nothing to make from card stock.

Read the comparison as a pass or fail against the specification, not as a judgement of whether you personally like the colour. A buyer who prefers a slightly warmer tone than the approved shade will reject a correct production every time. The comparison answers one question only: does this material match the approved reference, under stated conditions.

Records, Revisions and Keeping the Approval Alive

The most useful property of a sample board is that it survives. The second most useful property is that the records attached to it explain what changed and when.

Version the board, not just the swatch

When a programme changes, do not overwrite the board. Add the new swatch and keep the old one, marked with its revision status. A shade may be adjusted for a new season, or re-specified because a packaging substrate changed, and the buyer will need to demonstrate that a received shipment matches the revision that was actually approved rather than the one that happened to arrive at the factory.

Record the conditions, not just the verdict

For each approval, log the lighting, the background used, the date, the people present, and the verdict. A one-line note such as "approved, D65 booth, 2026-03-11" is worth a great deal when a claim is raised eight months later. It separates a manufacturing deviation from a difference of opinion, which is the only way to settle such a claim without both sides becoming defensive.

Link the board to the purchase order

Reference the board and the swatch code on every purchase order for that colour. This sounds obvious and is frequently missing. Its practical effect is that goods arriving against a purchase order have an unambiguous colour reference attached to the contract, which makes the pre-shipment inspection and the arrival check straightforward rather than a matter of who remembers what the colour was.

Set a review point

Shade perception and material condition both drift. A board kept for five years in a warehouse will have faded swatches, and a faded swatch will fail a correct production batch. Review the board annually, and replace or re-approve any swatch that has been stored in direct light. If the board has been stored in the dark, the risk is lower, but the fibre and any optical brighteners will still have aged.

Handover Between Factory, Buyer and Third Parties

The board usually has to work across more than two companies, and this is where colour programmes most often break down.

Factory to buyer. The factory sends the board with the shipment, or holds it against the buyer's order number for collection. Either way it needs a documented chain of custody so that a dispute about which swatch was sent can be resolved. Photograph the packed board at the point it leaves the factory, with the order reference visible.

Buyer to supplier. Third-party suppliers, contract packers and fulfilment houses need a reference too, often for a single problematic SKU. In that case a single swatch travels with the goods and a copy stays on the master board, with the relationship recorded on the board itself.

Multiple factories. If the same shade is produced at more than one mill, the board becomes the mechanism that keeps the shades aligned. Without it, each factory is working from a photograph and a verbal description, and the shades will drift within a few production runs.

Print and packaging suppliers. A ribbon shade is frequently specified to coordinate with a printed carton or a printed ribbon. The colour reference for the print is often a separate PDF colour value, and the two references drift apart. Record both on the board, and check them together at least once per production run.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Approving from the factory's marketing photographs. These are usually shot under controlled studio lighting with the ribbon styled to show sheen, which is the least representative condition possible. Useful for a brochure, not for an approval.

Storing the board in a plastic sleeve in sunlight. A clear sleeve protects against handling but traps humidity and can yellow from the plastic itself over time. Store flat, in a box, away from light, and not sealed in plastic for long periods.

Comparing in daylight against direct sun. Direct sun shifts perceived colour significantly and changes through the day. If you must work in daylight, use indirect north light only.

Using a different board for different factories. Two boards drift apart within months. One board, controlled and versioned, is the entire point.

Losing the board when the buyer changes staff. A physical object in a drawer is invisible to the next person. Record where it lives, who owns it, and link it from the specification system so it is found during onboarding.

Treating the board as disposable. It is not a sample, it is a standard. Samples are given away; standards are kept.

What to Put in the Purchase Order and Specification

Colour approval lives or dies on whether the reference is attached to the contract. The following clauses make the approval enforceable.

  • Reference sample designation. State that the colour is defined by the physical swatch on board, identified by board revision and swatch code, and that the physical reference prevails over any digital colour value in the order documentation.
  • Precedence order. Where a digital colour value also exists, state explicitly which governs. Ambiguity here is the most common cause of a dispute that cannot be settled, because both parties believe their reference was the controlling one.
  • Comparison method. Specify the reference method for acceptance, such as a comparison under 5000 kelvin light against a mid-grey background at a stated viewing distance. This removes the argument about conditions after the fact.
  • Tolerance statement. Either state a tolerance, such as an agreed maximum Delta E under a stated method, or state explicitly that the acceptance method is visual comparison against the retained reference with no numeric tolerance. Both are workable. Leaving it unstated is not, because it guarantees that a dispute becomes a debate about method.
  • Retained master. Require the factory to retain a sealed, signed and dated master sample of the approved colour, and state that the buyer's board and the factory's master are considered equivalent references for the same shade.
  • Dispute procedure. Say what happens when a batch is questioned: who compares, under what conditions, and how the result is recorded.

Colour Approval Checklist

Before signing off a shade or accepting a shipment against one, confirm the following.

  • The swatch is mounted and labelled with shade code, material, width and batch reference.
  • The comparison is made under 5000 kelvin light or north daylight, not warm office light and not direct sun.
  • A neutral mid-grey background is used, with other ribbon out of the immediate field.
  • The swatch is viewed at 30 to 50 centimetres, the way it will be seen in use.
  • Where relevant, the swatch is compared in its actual assembly against the packaging or product.
  • For fine differences, a viewing frame is used to compare adjacent areas of colour.
  • The verdict is recorded with date, lighting, background and observers, not only as pass or fail.
  • The board and swatch code are referenced on the purchase order and in the inspection record.
  • The master sample at the factory and the board at the buyer are both sealed, dated and identifiable.
  • Board revisions are additive and superseded swatches are marked, not discarded.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Can I approve ribbon colour from a photo or a PDF colour value?

Use them to shortlist, not to approve. A photograph captures one light source, one device and one colour profile, and it cannot reproduce the reflective behaviour of a satin, velvet or metallic ribbon under real lighting. A digital colour value describes a pixel, not a material. A photograph can trigger a conversation about shade, but the approval that matters is a physical swatch compared under stated conditions against a retained reference. If a buyer approves from a photo and the production batch arrives looking different, the buyer has no reference with which to make a claim.

2. What lighting should I use to compare ribbon swatches?

Daylight-balanced light around 5000 kelvin, or indirect north-facing daylight. Avoid direct sunlight, which is variable and warm, and avoid warm domestic or office lighting, which pushes every shade towards yellow and makes cool shades look wrong. If you are working under office lighting only, record that fact in the approval note. A shade approved under warm lighting and rejected under the buyer's cool warehouse lighting is a process failure rather than a manufacturing failure, and the record of the conditions is what proves that.

3. How should the swatch be fixed to the sample board?

Mount it permanently with adhesive tape across the full width at both ends, leaving the middle free so the ribbon can flex without lifting at the corners. A single attachment point across the top edge will lift over time as the ribbon moves. Cut every swatch to a consistent size, usually around 50 by 100 millimetres, so that swatches can be stacked and their edges compared for shade shift. Attach a printed label to the board rather than to the ribbon, so the swatch can be removed without losing its identity.

4. How do I keep an approved ribbon colour consistent across different factories?

Use one controlled board as the reference for every production site and require each factory to retain a sealed, signed and dated master sample of the approved shade. Each master and the buyer's board are treated as equivalent references for the same shade. Without a shared physical reference, each factory works from a photograph and a written description, and the shades drift apart within a few production runs. Version the board rather than overwriting it, so that a shipment can always be checked against the revision that was actually approved.

5. What should I specify in the purchase order for ribbon colour?

State that colour is defined by the physical swatch identified by board revision and swatch code, and that the physical reference prevails over any digital colour value. Set a precedence order if both exist, name the comparison method and lighting, and either give a numeric tolerance such as an agreed maximum Delta E under a stated method or explicitly say that acceptance is by visual comparison with no numeric tolerance. Also require the factory to retain a sealed master sample and state where the buyer board is stored. Leaving the reference method unstated is what turns a colour question into an unresolvable argument about method.
