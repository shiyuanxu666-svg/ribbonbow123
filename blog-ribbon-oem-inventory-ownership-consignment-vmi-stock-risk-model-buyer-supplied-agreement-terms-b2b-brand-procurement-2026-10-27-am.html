The Question That Determines the Whole Programme: Who Owns the Stock Sitting at the Mill

Before any discussion of price, lead time or quality, a ribbon programme has to answer a deceptively simple question: when finished ribbon is sitting at the supplier's warehouse waiting for your reorder, who owns it, who carries the risk of it deteriorating, and who gets the tax benefit of it having been sold? Almost every commercial dispute in this category that is not about price turns out to be a downstream consequence of the fact that nobody wrote this down clearly. The question is not academic. It determines who absorbs a stock write-off if the colour goes off-trend before it is sold, who bears the insurance risk if the warehouse floods, who pays if the goods are destroyed in transit from the mill to the buyer's distribution centre, and whether the inventory is an asset on the buyer's balance sheet or appears nowhere until it is consumed. It also determines the behaviour of the two parties, and this is the part that surprises procurement teams. When the mill owns the stock, the mill has an incentive to hold more than you need, because excess stock is not their risk. When you own the stock, the mill has an incentive to make you commit to quantity too early. Neither behaviour is malicious; both are rational responses to an unclear ownership arrangement, and both cost money. The correct sequence is therefore: decide the ownership model first, write it explicitly, and only then negotiate the commercial terms that depend on it. Buyers who treat inventory ownership as a detail to be settled at the end of contract negotiation usually find that the price they agreed in month one assumed a model nobody actually documented, and there is no version of the dispute resolution process that fixes an unstated assumption.

The Four Structures Buyers Actually Use

In practice there are four inventory ownership structures, and almost every programme is some combination of them. The first is buyer-owned stock held at the mill: the buyer pays for the goods, the goods are shipped to the supplier's warehouse or to a third-party warehouse, and the buyer retains title until consumption or sale. This is the most common structure for a first programme and it has a clear virtue, which is that it is easy to understand and easy to audit. Its weakness is that the buyer carries the risk of obsolescence, storage cost and shrinkage on inventory that sits outside its control. The second structure is consignment: the mill manufactures and holds the goods, but title remains with the buyer until the goods are drawn against a purchase order. Consignment appears in the ribbon trade more often than most buyers expect, and it works well for slow-moving or long-lead programmes because it removes the buyer's cash exposure to unsold stock. Its weakness is that it requires a real agreement, because without one the mill has goods it cannot sell, cannot bill for, and cannot write off, which it will eventually stop accepting. The third is vendor-managed inventory: the mill holds the goods and the buyer draws against it, but ownership passes at manufacture rather than at consumption, so the stock is effectively sold to the buyer whether or not it is used. This is the simplest structure commercially and the most common in commodity trim. Its weakness is precisely that it offers the buyer nothing: no risk sharing, no protection against colour drift on a trend line, and no mechanism to stop the mill building stock you will never consume. The fourth is the hybrid, and it is where most sophisticated programmes end up. Under a hybrid, a small quantity of buffer stock is held at the mill under a replenishment arrangement while the balance remains on an order-triggered basis, and the two are governed by different terms in the same agreement. Recognising that a hybrid is the normal answer for most programmes, rather than treating hybrid as a failure to decide, saves a great deal of contractual argument.

What Actually Shifts Between the Models

It is worth being precise about what ownership changes and what it does not, because buyers frequently overstate the effect of a consignment arrangement. What shifts is four things. First, title and therefore the risk of loss. Second, the point at which the buyer can claim the cost against inventory, which is the inventory accounting treatment. Third, the point at which the mill has earned revenue, which is usually the trigger for payment terms and therefore for the mill's working-capital position. Fourth, and most operationally significant, the mill's incentive to hold stock: under buyer ownership the mill is a warehousing provider for that portion of the programme, and it will usually price the warehousing, whereas under vendor-managed inventory the mill holds stock as part of its own working capital and is considerably more willing to do so. What does not shift is the quality obligation, the specification obligation, or the obligation to handle a quality claim. A common and costly error is to assume that consignment changes the quality dynamics; it does not. The mill remains responsible for producing to the approved article, and the risk of a latent defect that surfaces six months after the stock was drawn sits with whoever owned the goods at the time of manufacture, which is precisely why the ownership wording and the quality-remedy wording have to be read together rather than separately. The same applies to obsolescence: if the buyer owns the stock and the colour falls out of fashion, the buyer writes it off. If the mill owns the stock under consignment and the colour falls out of fashion, the mill has a strong incentive to argue that the goods were made to an approved specification that the buyer signed off, and that argument is generally correct. Consignment therefore shifts a specific set of risks and does not shift the others.

Six Risk Allocations That Need Explicit Wording

The difference between a consignment arrangement that works and one that becomes a dispute usually comes down to whether six specific allocations are stated. The first is the deterioration risk: what happens to goods held for a long period, particularly dyed ribbon, which is subject to light, humidity and time, and particularly printed ribbon, where the print adhesion changes with storage. The agreement should state whether the mill must rotate or inspect aged stock, at what age, and who bears the cost if the goods no longer meet the colour tolerance because of storage rather than because of manufacturing. The second is the insurance question: which party insures the stock, for what value, and does the mill's policy name the buyer as an additional insured for goods on consignment. A third is the shrinkage and loss question, including what happens to partial deliveries, to damage in the mill's own handling, and to stock that cannot be located at a stocktake. The fourth is the obsolescence question, which is the hardest one and the one most often left unstated: whether the buyer can return or re-purpose aged consignment stock, whether the mill must buy it back at an agreed formula, and what notice period applies. The fifth is the quality-claim window on consignment stock, which is different from the window on newly manufactured goods because goods sitting in a warehouse for eight months and then being consumed deserve a different claim period than goods consumed within weeks of manufacture. The sixth is the end-of-programme position: what happens to remaining stock when a colour is discontinued, when the programme is ended, or when the buyer switches suppliers. That clause is the one that most often turns a tidy arrangement into an expensive dispute, because it determines whether the mill can sell the residual stock independently, whether the buyer must buy it, and whether the mill may ship it to a third party. Six allocations, written explicitly, are usually the difference between a working consignment programme and a working relationship.

Writing the Consignment Agreement: The Terms That Decide It

A consignment agreement for ribbon and decorative trim is shorter than most buyers expect and longer than most suppliers expect, and the difference is usually caused by the parties describing the same thing in different vocabulary. Six term groups carry the substance. The first is the consignment schedule: a line-by-line list of every SKU, with the quantity, the colour and width reference, the unit price for eventual invoicing, and the agreed rate at which the mill is remunerated for holding the stock. Without a schedule there is nothing to draw against, and the arrangement degrades into an informal understanding that nobody can enforce. The second is the draw-down mechanics: how a purchase order is raised, the minimum order quantity, whether the buyer may draw partially, what happens when a draw leaves a residual balance, and how often the parties reconcile the physical count against the system balance. The third is the storage and handling terms: the environmental conditions the stock is held under, the rotation requirement, the permitted locations, the prohibition on commingling with other customers' goods, and the requirement that the stock remains separately identifiable. The fourth is the title and risk clause, which states precisely when title passes and who bears the risk of loss in each stage, including the stage from the mill's loading bay to the buyer's warehouse, which is where the stage-question is most often left ambiguous. The fifth is the term and exit clause: the notice period, the treatment of residual stock, the buyer's option to call the stock back at cost, and the survival of the quality and indemnity obligations after the agreement ends. The sixth is the dispute and set-off clause, which matters commercially because a consignment arrangement without an agreed reconciliation and set-off procedure tends to accumulate small disputed balances that eventually stop the programme. Notably absent from a good consignment agreement are the quality specification and the quality remedies, because those belong in the supply agreement that sits underneath it. Splitting the two documents this way keeps the consignment terms stable while the specification evolves through the programme, which is a material advantage in a fast-moving category.

Accounting, Tax and the Cross-Border Questions

For buyers outside China, the ownership model has consequences that are not commercial but fiscal, and these are worth settling early because they are rarely quick to change. The first is where the inventory sits for accounting purposes. Under buyer ownership with stock held at the mill, the buyer has inventory on its balance sheet that is physically in another country, which raises questions about the accounting treatment and, in some jurisdictions, about the tax consequences of goods stored outside the customs territory of final consumption. The second is transfer pricing and the arm's-length treatment of a consignment relationship, because a consignment arrangement where the mill holds the buyer's inventory and is remunerated for storage is a service arrangement as well as a supply arrangement, and the price for that service has to be documented on a defensible basis. The third is customs and duty treatment: goods that enter the destination country as owned inventory, or as consignment stock released later for domestic movement, can be treated differently, and the treatment affects cash flow at the border. The fourth is VAT or sales tax timing: under a consignment model, the taxable event for the eventual sale may occur on consumption rather than on manufacture, which changes the period in which the buyer recognises the tax, and this can be significant when the programme runs across a year end. The fifth is write-off risk and its accounting consequence, because consignment stock that the buyer cannot consume sits on the buyer's balance sheet and eventually becomes an impairment question rather than a commercial one. None of these questions has a single global answer, and any buyer working across more than one jurisdiction should treat them as a matter for their own finance and tax advisers rather than for their ribbon supplier. What the supplier can do, and should do, is provide the documentation that makes the treatment possible: the material and quantity records, the serial or lot-level traceability, the warehouse location records and the evidence of the date of manufacture, which is what supports both the inventory accounting and the durability discussion. VMI, Replenishment and When the Model Should Change

Inventory ownership models are not permanent, and a programme that never revisits the model is a programme paying for the wrong one. The trigger for change is usually a change in demand behaviour. When a programme moves from a forecast-driven model to a fast-replenishment model, with shorter cycles and more frequent smaller deliveries, vendor-managed inventory usually becomes the better answer, because the stock turns faster and the buyer's cash exposure is small. When a programme moves in the other direction, towards a seasonal or trend-driven colour that may sit unsold for a season, consignment becomes more attractive, because the risk of obsolescence shifts to the party better placed to manage it. There is also a transition point in the programme's own life. Early in a launch, buyers usually want to hold less stock because the demand profile is unproven, and a hybrid arrangement with a small consigned buffer is usually right. Later, once sell-through is reliable, a firm order with vendor-managed replenishment is cheaper and simpler. The mistake is treating this as a change of contract requiring renegotiation from scratch; in practice it is an adjustment to the schedule and the draw-down rules within an existing agreement. A second trigger for change is supply-side risk: if the mill is running near capacity in a peak season, a buyer that depends on vendor-managed stock may find that allocation is what gets deprioritised, because the mill's obligation to hold stock for the buyer competes with its obligation to supply the buyers who have firm orders. Buyers who want genuine priority in a constrained period need that stated explicitly, and it is worth stating it in terms of the quantity the mill must hold available rather than in general language about priority. A third trigger is a change in the retail calendar, where a promotional peak changes the shape of demand enough that a model tuned for the base rate produces either a stockout in the promotion or an overhang after it.

Building an Inventory Ownership Model That Survives Contact With the First Peak Season

The useful test of an inventory arrangement is not how it behaves in the calm months; it is how it behaves in the first peak season, when demand is uneven, the forecast is wrong in both directions at once, and the parties are under time pressure. Four disciplines carry most of the value. The first is to agree the reconciliation mechanism before it is needed, because a consignment balance that is only reconciled when there is a dispute will not be reconciled at all. A monthly physical-to-system reconciliation with a short window for raising differences, and a defined process for resolving them, is a small piece of work that prevents a large piece of argument. The second is to write the obsolescence clause at the beginning of the programme rather than at the end of it, when the position is already established and the negotiating balance has shifted. Agreeing, in advance and in a formula, how residual stock is treated is what makes it possible for a buyer to place a firm order without fear and for the mill to accept that order without holding inventory it does not want. The third is to review the model at a fixed point in the programme - typically once the first full season of sell-through data exists - using actual consumption rather than the original forecast. That review should be a scheduled item with a written output, not a conversation that happens only when something goes wrong. The fourth is to keep the documentation current and in one place, because the ownership arrangement is only as good as the record that supports it: the consignment schedule, the draw-down records, the warehouse location records, the manufacturing dates, the quality records and the shipping documents. When a buyer can reconstruct, at any point, what stock is held, where it is held, who owns it and in what condition, the model is working. When that reconstruction is not possible, the programme is running on an agreement that exists only in the memory of the two people who negotiated it, and the first peak season will find that out.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between consignment stock and vendor-managed inventory in a ribbon programme?

The timing of when title passes, and therefore where the risk sits. Under consignment, the mill manufactures and holds the goods but title remains with the buyer until the goods are drawn against a purchase order, so the buyer owns stock sitting at the mill and carries the obsolescence and storage risk; the mill is remunerated for holding it. Under vendor-managed inventory, title passes at manufacture, so the stock is effectively sold to the buyer whether or not it is ever used, which means the buyer pays for it either way and the mill holds it as part of its own working capital. Consignment suits slow-moving or long-lead programmes where the buyer wants to avoid cash exposure on unsold stock; vendor-managed suits fast-replenishment programmes where stock turns quickly. The quality obligation and the specification obligation do not change between the two - the mill is still responsible for producing to the approved article in either case - so consignment shifts a specific set of financial and obsolescence risks and leaves the rest alone.

Who should own ribbon stock that is sitting at the supplier's warehouse?

That depends on how well the demand profile is proven and how quickly the material deteriorates, and the honest answer is that a hybrid usually serves a programme better than either extreme. Early in a launch, when the demand profile is unproven, the buyer should hold less inventory and a small consigned buffer at the mill is usually the right structure, because it caps exposure without stopping the mill from holding stock close to the consumption point. Once sell-through data exists and demand is reliable, a firm order with vendor-managed replenishment is simpler and cheaper. Stock that is dyed or printed and sits for a season without moving is a poor candidate for buyer ownership, because light, humidity and time degrade it and the buyer carries the write-off. Whatever structure is chosen, the decision has to be written down, because the incentive for the mill to hold more than you need, or for you to commit to quantity too early, follows directly from whichever document is silent.

What should a ribbon consignment agreement actually cover?

Six term groups carry the substance. The consignment schedule, listing every SKU with quantity, colour and width reference, unit price for eventual invoicing and the storage rate, because without it there is nothing to draw against. The draw-down mechanics, covering how a purchase order is raised, minimum draw quantity, whether partial draws are permitted, and how often the physical count is reconciled against the system balance. The storage and handling terms, covering environmental conditions, stock rotation, permitted locations, a prohibition on commingling and a requirement that stock remains separately identifiable. The title and risk clause, stating exactly when title passes and who bears risk of loss in each stage including mill to buyer's warehouse. The term and exit clause, covering notice period, treatment of residual stock, the buyer's option to call stock back at cost, and survival of quality obligations after the agreement ends. And the reconciliation, dispute and set-off procedure, without which small disputed balances accumulate until the programme stops moving.

Does consignment change the mill's quality obligations?

No, and this is a costly assumption to get wrong. The mill remains responsible for producing to the approved article and for the conformity of the goods, whether those goods are held under consignment or sold outright. What changes is who absorbs a latent defect that surfaces after the stock has been sitting in a warehouse for months. That sits with whoever owned the goods at the time of manufacture, which is why the ownership wording and the quality-remedy wording have to be read together rather than separately. A sensible structure keeps them in separate documents for practical reasons - the specification evolves through the programme while the ownership terms should stay stable - but the quality remedy clause in the supply agreement should explicitly state whether it continues to apply to consignment stock after it has been drawn and after the general claim window on newly manufactured goods has closed.

How do you handle obsolescence risk on consignment ribbon stock?

You agree the treatment before it becomes a problem, because once the stock is sitting there the negotiating balance has already shifted. The clause should cover whether the buyer can return or re-purpose aged stock, whether the mill must buy it back at an agreed formula rather than at an open-market price, what notice period applies to a call-back, and what happens at the end of a colour or a programme. The aged-stock question should also specify what the mill must do to slow deterioration in the meantime: rotation, racking away from direct light, humidity control and periodic inspection with a defined frequency. For dyed ribbon in particular, time and light matter, so an inspection interval with a colour re-check against the approved standard is more useful than a general statement that the mill will take reasonable care. Without the buy-back formula, the conservative mill behaviour is to stop accepting consignment stock for ageing colours, and the buyer loses the option they were relying on.

What happens to consignment stock if we end the programme or switch suppliers?

This is the clause most often left out, and it is the one most likely to cause a dispute. The agreement should state what happens to residual stock when a colour is discontinued, when the programme ends, or when the buyer changes supplier: whether the mill must buy it back at cost or at a formula, whether the buyer must take delivery of it, whether the mill may sell it to a third party and on what terms, and how the quality and indemnity obligations survive the end of the relationship. In practice, the most common outcome is a negotiated call-back at cost against a defined notice period, with the mill free to dispose of stock after the call-back window if the buyer has not exercised the option. The alternative - leaving it unstated - reliably produces an argument at exactly the moment when the relationship is already under strain, which is the worst possible time to be defining terms.

Are there tax and accounting consequences to choosing consignment over vendor-managed inventory?

Yes, and they differ by jurisdiction, so they belong with your own finance and tax advisers rather than with your ribbon supplier. The main consequences are where the inventory sits for accounting purposes when the buyer owns stock physically located in another country, the treatment of the storage service as a separate priced element for transfer-pricing purposes, the customs and duty treatment of stock released later for domestic movement, the timing of the taxable event for VAT or sales tax when consumption rather than manufacture triggers the sale, and the impairment question when consignment stock cannot be consumed. What the supplier can and should provide is the documentation that makes any of these treatments supportable: lot-level traceability, manufacturing and batch dates, warehouse location records, the consignment schedule with its draw-down history and the shipping documents.
