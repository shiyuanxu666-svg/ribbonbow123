The core insight: colour is the one attribute of a ribbon where the buyer believes they have agreed something, and the mill has actually agreed something else. You have approved a swatch. The mill has approved a dye recipe. The two only describe the same colour by coincidence, and the coincidence holds until the first bulk run lands. Every commercial negotiation you have won on price is worthless if the goods arrive in a shade your customers describe as "not quite the pink".

This guide is about closing that gap. It is written for brand owners, product developers and procurement managers who specify ribbon by colour for packaging, gift sets, apparel trim, hair accessories or home textiles, and who want colour to be a controlled specification rather than an ongoing negotiation.

We cover what a ribbon shade physically is, why five separate variables push it around, what Pantone references do and do not guarantee, how lab dips are used and misused, how to read a delta E number without becoming a colour scientist, why approved samples so often fail to predict the bulk roll, and how to write a colour specification that a dye house can actually hit repeatedly. The tone is deliberately practical: this is a subject where confident-sounding advice is common and useful advice is rare.

The financial argument comes first, because it explains why colour deserves this much attention. A ribbon programme that reorders four times a year at 20,000 metres per reorder, where one delivery lands wrong, does not lose 20,000 metres. It loses the sorting time of a warehouse team, the air freight to replace it, the schedule slot on the shelf, and — depending on the brand — the credibility of a seasonal launch. On a premium gift programme the replacement cost is a rounding error. The commercial cost is not.

Why Colour Is the Most Expensive Agreement You Did Not Negotiate

Most ribbon purchase orders describe colour with a single reference. Sometimes that is a Pantone code. Sometimes it is a hex value from a design file. Sometimes it is the phrase "same as last time", attached to a swatch card that is now several years old and has faded on the shelf of the office. Each of these is a reasonable shorthand to a buyer and an incomplete instruction to a dye house.

The problem is that a dye house cannot reproduce a visual impression. It can reproduce a recipe: specific fibre, specific dye class, specific bath temperature, specific dwell time, specific pH, specific finishing conditions. If you hand them a Pantone code they will use it as a starting reference, but a Pantone book is printed on coated paper with a specific illuminant, and ribbon is a woven textile with a specific surface, a specific sheen and a specific way of scattering light. The two are related, not equivalent.

This produces a predictable dynamic. The mill asks for a colour reference, the buyer provides one, a lab dip is produced, the dip looks acceptable to everyone in the room, and the buyer signs it. Then three weeks later the bulk arrives and it is visibly different — often not dramatically, sometimes only visible side by side, but visible, and always at the worst possible moment, because retail photography and physical shelf placement make even a small difference obvious.

Why does it happen so often? Because a lab dip and a bulk run are not the same thing. The lab dip is small, often on a different substrate, usually at a slightly different concentration, and is evaluated in a lighting condition that may not resemble where the product ends up. The buyer has approved a representation of the colour. The mill has produced a sample of the recipe. Approving the representation and the recipe are different acts, and only one of them guarantees the bulk.

There is a second, subtler reason. In most buying relationships, the person who approves colour is not the person who specified it and not the person who will receive the bulk. A designer approves a swatch on a screen or under showroom light. A buyer orders the mill's volume. A quality inspector receives the shipment. Nobody in that chain is responsible for the gap between what the designer imagined and what the mill produced. Colour fails precisely because ownership is fragmented.

What a Ribbon Shade Is Actually Made Of

To manage colour you need to know what it is composed of. A ribbon shade is the result of at least four layers stacked on top of each other:

The substrate. The base fibre and construction do most of the quiet work. A satin weave and a grosgrain weave reflect light differently, so the same dye in each produces visibly different results. Polyester, nylon, rayon, cotton, acetate and blends each behave differently in a dye bath. Polyester is typically the hardest to shade consistently across lots; cotton takes dye readily but fades differently under light; rayon has a characteristic lustre that shifts perceived tone depending on the angle. You cannot decouple "the colour" from "the fibre", because they are one material decision.

The dye and its method. Reactive, disperse, direct and acid dyes are used for different fibre families, and two dyes from the same family can behave quite differently on the same substrate under slightly different bath conditions. Pigment colours sit differently on the surface and give flatter, more matte results. Metallic, pearlescent and neon effects are not colours at all in the classical sense — they are optical constructions that behave differently from any solid dye.

The finishing. This is where buyers most often get caught. Calendar heat, glazing, brushing, mercerising, washing, resin finishing, embossing and coating all alter final shade and hand. A washed ribbon will always look slightly different from the same ribbon unwashed, and a calendered satin will look deeper and more lustrous than an uncalendered one. If the lab dip is taken before finishing and the bulk is finished, the comparison is invalid.

The optical environment. Colour is not a property of an object alone; it is a property of an object under light. The same ribbon under a warm 2700K shop light, under 4000K office lighting and under D65 daylight will not look the same. Metamerism — where two samples that match under one illuminant diverge under another — is a real and common phenomenon with textiles. This is why a physical sample, not a screen image, is the only acceptable basis for colour approval, and why viewing conditions must be controlled.

Understanding these four layers is what separates a buyer who orders a colour from a buyer who manages one. Each layer is a place where variation enters, and each layer has a specification you can write.

The Five Factors That Move a Ribbon Shade

Within those layers, five variables account for nearly all the movement a buyer will observe between two deliveries of the same nominal colour. Knowing them is most of the battle.

1. Fibre lot variation. Fibre arrives in lots with slightly different polymer characteristics, dye affinity and shrinkage behaviour. This is normal and unavoidable; it is one reason a factory holds working stock. But it means the dye house may need to adjust the recipe for a new fibre lot to hit the same target, and that adjustment is where the risk sits. A mill with an experienced dye house handles this routinely. A mill that has never seen the colour before is running the recipe blind.

2. Dye concentration and bath ratio. Every dye house works to a specific concentration and liquor ratio. Scale-up from a lab dip to a production kettle changes the ratio unless it is carefully reproduced, and concentration errors show up directly as a shade shift. This is the single most common technical cause of a failed bulk match, and it is entirely within the mill's control, which means it is entirely reasonable for you to ask how they manage it.

3. Temperature and dwell time. Dyeing is a chemical process with kinetics. A three-degree difference in bath temperature or a fifteen-minute difference in dwell time produces a different colour. Modern dye houses control this tightly and measure it, but a mill running an old kettle with poor temperature control has a materially higher chance of lot-to-lot drift on difficult shades.

4.Finishing conditions. As above. Heat, pressure, washing and any resin or surface treatment all shift the final shade. This is also where the lab dip and the bulk diverge most often, because the dip may be finished differently from production.

5. Storage and light exposure. This one arrives after approval. Ribbons stored in a warehouse with windows, near a south-facing wall, or in unlined cartons will fade unevenly, and the roll at the core of a batch will not match the roll at the outer edge. Suppliers who work with a strong seasonality should be able to describe how their finished goods are protected from light, and you should ask.

None of these five is exotic. All five are manageable. The reason colour problems persist is not technical complexity but the absence of an agreed written standard, which is a procurement failure rather than a dye house failure.

Pantone References and What They Can and Cannot Tell You

Pantone is the most widely used colour communication tool in packaging, and it is also the most widely misused. The distinction matters, so it is worth being precise.

What a Pantone reference genuinely does: it identifies a specific colour in a specific simulated printing condition, on a specific substrate, as viewed under a specific standard illuminant. It gives you a shared vocabulary. When a designer in your studio says "Pantone 186 C" and a mill in Xiamen says "Pantone 186 C", both parties are pointing at the same coordinate in the same system. That coordination value is enormous and is why Pantone works as well as it does.

What a Pantone reference does not do: it does not describe a dye recipe, and it does not promise that a textile will match the printed swatch. Dyes and pigments behave differently, the colour space of a printed coated surface is not the colour space of a dyed textile, and the mapping from one to the other is approximate. A mill targeting Pantone 186 C with polyester disperse dye will land somewhere in the neighbourhood. Whether that neighbourhood is acceptable to your brand is a judgement you have to make, and making it requires a physical comparison rather than a code.

There is a further practical point that surprises buyers: Pantone codes are not stable across guides and across digital versions of the guide. The coated, uncoated and fabric books differ from each other, and the display of a code on a screen does not reflect either. If you intend to use Pantone as a reference, specify which guide — typically the FHI Fabric Textile Guide for textiles, or the coated guide for packaging applications — and require the mill to work from a physical swatch rather than a screenshot.

The correct use of a Pantone reference is as a starting coordinate for a conversation that ends with a physical approved standard. The incorrect use is as a substitute for that conversation. If a purchase order says only "Colour: Pantone 186 C", it is not a colour specification. It is an aspiration.

Lab Dips: What They Are and What They Are For

A lab dip is a small swatch of the actual construction, dyed with the actual intended dye class at approximately production concentration. It exists to answer one question: does this recipe, on this fibre, hit the target shade? It is the correct and standard tool for that question.

What lab dips are good for: selecting between candidate recipes, confirming that a target shade is achievable on your chosen fibre before you commit a production run, catching an obviously wrong dye class early, and giving a brand a physical standard to hold the mill to.

What lab dips are not good for: predicting the finished bulk roll. They are small pieces, frequently dyed in a different vessel from the production kettle, often subject to a different finishing route, and judged under whatever light happens to be in the room. A dip that matches perfectly can still yield a bulk that drifts, and — less commonly but importantly — a dip can look slightly off while the bulk is closer to the target, because the finishing step moves the shade in a predictable direction.

Two practical rules follow. First, always ask for the dip on your specified construction, not on a generic swatch substrate; a dip on cotton tells you almost nothing about a polyester satin. Second, and more importantly, treat the lab dip as a directional instrument rather than a final standard. The final standard is the approved production roll, and it should be sealed, dated and retained.

On sequence, one further point is worth making because it is regularly mishandled: the lab dip should come after the construction is fixed, not before. Swatches dyed on a different fibre, a different weave or a different width will not tell you what you need to know, and dip requests made against an unsettled specification produce dips that have to be redone — at cost and at delay.

Delta E and the Language of Acceptable Difference

Delta E, written dE, is the standard numerical measure of the difference between two colours. It is a single number summarising a three-dimensional difference, and it is the most useful tool a buyer can have for colour conversations because it converts a subjective argument into a threshold.

How it works in brief: two colours are converted into a common numerical space, and the distance between them is measured. The scale runs from 0 (identical) upwards, and the direction of the difference can be broken into components — lightness, chroma and hue — which is what makes the number actionable rather than merely a score.

Commonly used interpretation bands, though these vary by industry and should be agreed with your mill rather than assumed:

  • dE below 1.0 — a difference that is essentially invisible side by side under controlled light. Appropriate for a flagship colour where the whole programme depends on the shade being exactly right.
  • dE 1.0 to 2.0 — acceptable for most solid-colour commercial programmes, including packaging and gift trim, where the roll will not be compared against a swatch in a light box.
  • dE 2.0 to 3.0 — tolerable for structural or technical trim where colour is not a selling point, and where batch-to-batch drift within this band is not visible in finished product.
  • above 3.0 — a visible difference. Almost never acceptable for a solid-colour programme, and the point at which you should stop accepting this as normal.

Two important qualifications. First, dE measures difference, not acceptability — a 2.5 difference between a brand's current best-selling peach and a marginally different peach may be commercially fine, while a 2.5 difference in a flagship holiday colour is a complaint. Set the threshold against your own product risk, not against a generic table.

Second, dE should always be interpreted alongside a light source. Measurement under D65 daylight and measurement under a store's warm LED give different results, and metameric failures show up as a passing dE under one illuminant and a failing one under another. If your ribbon goes into retail environments with mixed lighting, ask for the measurement under more than one illuminant.

Note the practical asymmetry: buyers who do not ask for dE rarely get it, and buyers who ask for it once rarely have to ask again. It is a low-friction, high-signal request.

Batch Consistency: The Problem That Appears After Approval

Getting the first bulk run to match the approved standard is only half the requirement. The other half is that every subsequent delivery continues to match, including when the fibre lot changes, the mill is running a different shift, and your programme has been quiet for four months and now reorders urgently.

Batch consistency is where most colour relationships quietly deteriorate, because it is not usually specified and therefore not usually managed. A buyer approves a shade, places a reorder, and receives a roll that is clearly the same family of colour but marginally lighter. Nobody says anything because nobody has a reference to say anything against. The next reorder drifts further. A year later the programme has accumulated a range of shades that were all individually approved and collectively inconsistent.

Four practical measures reduce this risk, and all four are worth asking about before you place a large order:

Retain a sealed standard. Keep a length of the approved bulk, sealed in an opaque bag, with the date, lot and colour code written on it. This is your reference for every future delivery and it costs nothing but discipline.

Ask about the dye house's own control method. A competent dye house retains a master batch of the approved recipe, re-tests against it at the start of each run, and calibrates instruments on a schedule. If the answer is vague, that is information.

Define a retest trigger. Agree in advance what happens when a delivery arrives outside your tolerance: who measures it, in what lighting, against what reference, and what remedy applies. Doing this after the fact, with a shipment already on the dock, is a much weaker position.

Order a re-standardisation periodically. After a period of inactivity, or after a substantial change in the mill's process, ask for a fresh production sample before the full reorder. This costs a few days and prevents a programme from drifting quietly for years.

The Dye House: Where Decisions Are Actually Made

The dye house is the room that determines your colour, and understanding how it works tells you what to ask. A mill running a modern dye house with automated temperature control, recipe management and spectrophotometric measurement is a fundamentally different proposition from one dyeing to a recipe kept in a notebook, even if both are called "factory".

What you can reasonably ask any ribbon mill:

  • Whether the colour will be dyed in-house or subcontracted, and where.
  • How many candidate recipes are typically tried before one is selected, and what the lab dip process looks like.
  • How the target shade is measured, and with what instrument and light source.
  • Whether a master batch of the approved recipe is retained, and for how long.
  • How the finished colour is checked after finishing, since finishing shifts the shade.
  • Whether production runs are measured against the approved standard before dispatch, and by whom.

These are ordinary, professional questions that any serious supplier will welcome. The quality of the answers tells you a great deal about whether colour control in that factory is a systematic capability or an individual's personal skill. The second situation is fragile: it works, it depends on one person, and it will not survive their absence or a change of pressure.

One question that is well worth asking is whether the mill holds a standing colour card for your programme, physically indexed to your reference. For a brand with an ongoing range in a specific palette, this is a remarkable convenience, and for a mill it is trivial to maintain once the practice is established. It turns "please match the pink" into "run recipe 14-B".

Why Approved Samples Do Not Match the Bulk Roll

When a bulk roll arrives off-shade, the cause is almost always one of a short list. Knowing the list means you can diagnose rather than simply complain, and it makes the complaint much harder to dismiss.

The dip was taken on the wrong construction. A lab dip on a different fibre, weave, width or finish is not a forecast of your ribbon. This is common when the dip request was made before the specification was settled.

The dip was not finished the same way. If the bulk goes through heat setting, washing or brushing and the dip did not, the comparison is meaningless. Ask explicitly whether the lab dip was finished and how.

Scale-up was not controlled. Concentration, bath ratio, temperature and dwell time all changed between the laboratory and the production kettle. A capable dye house controls these and verifies the result before discharge. An incapable one runs the recipe and hopes.

A new fibre lot required a recipe adjustment. This one is normal, and the question is whether the mill adjusted and measured, or adjusted and assumed. If they adjusted, they should have the measurement record to show you.

The bulk was finished differently from the dip. Finishing conditions vary with machine load, and a finishing run that behaved differently can shift the shade without anyone noticing, because the shift falls inside a band nobody is checking.

The inspection happened in unsuitable light. In-house inspection under a workshop fluorescent tube is not the same as your studio daylight. This is one of the most common and least discussed causes, and the cheapest to fix: specify the illuminant for the final check.

Two structural improvements eliminate most of these. The first is a pre-production sample taken from the actual production kettle, not from the laboratory, so that it carries production conditions. The second is a dispatch measurement against the retained standard, with the result recorded and attached to the shipment. Neither is exotic. Both are standard practice among mills that export to brand programmes with strict colour requirements, and asking for them is a reasonable expectation.

How to Write a Colour Specification That Survives Production

A workable colour specification has five components. Missing any one of them leaves an ambiguity that the mill will resolve in whichever way suits them, and you will discover the resolution in a shipment.

1. Construction and fibre. State the fibre content, the weave, the width and the finish. A colour specification attached to a construction is meaningful; attached to a swatch alone, it is not.

2. Target and reference. Give a target description and a physical reference. The reference should be a physical piece of ribbon — ideally a sealed length from an approved bulk run — with an identified colour system reference as a secondary aid. Where a Pantone is used, specify which guide and note that it is a guide, not a guarantee.

3. Tolerance. State an acceptable difference and the method. A dE threshold with a named light source, or a named instrument, is far more workable than "no visible difference", which is unfalsifiable and therefore unenforceable.

4. Measurement method and light source. State where and how the measurement happens: at your incoming inspection, at the mill's dispatch, or both, and under which illuminant. If the mill measures under a different condition than you accept, the number means nothing to you.

5. Remedy. State what happens on failure: replacement at the mill's cost, credit, or rework, and within what window. This is the clause that converts a technical standard into a commercial one, and it is the clause buyers most often leave out.

Written plainly, a complete colour clause looks roughly like this: "Colour to match sealed reference sample R3 (dated 12 May 2026) on 100 per cent polyester 38 mm satin, calendered, matte finish. Target dE not greater than 1.5 when measured under D65 illuminant. Mill to measure against retained master batch before dispatch and to record the result. Buyer to re-measure on receipt. Unrepresented difference: mill to replace at own cost within 21 days or issue equivalent credit, buyer to notify within 10 days of receipt."

That clause is longer than most buyers are used to writing and shorter than most colour disputes. The difference between the two is the entire cost of the programme's colour risk.

Lab Dip Cost, Lead Time and How to Sequence Them

Colour development costs money and time, and the sensible approach is to spend both only where they reduce risk. A rough sequencing discipline works well:

Stage one — feasibility. Before anything else, ask the mill whether the target shade is achievable on your chosen fibre and construction, and roughly what it would take. This costs a conversation. It prevents the expensive discovery that your shade and your fibre are incompatible, which is far more common than buyers expect, particularly for saturated shades on polyester and for pale shades that require a special base white.

Stage two — recipe selection. Lab dips, typically a small number of candidates, from which one is selected. This is where the money goes and it is money well spent, provided the construction is already fixed.

Stage three — production validation. A pre-production sample drawn from the actual production run, in the finished state, against the approved dip. This is the stage that protects the first bulk delivery, and it is the stage most often skipped in the interest of saving a week.

Stage four — production and dispatch verification. Measurement before dispatch, recorded and attached.

On cost, two observations. Lab dip charges are usually modest in absolute terms but can include a setup or recipe-development fee that is not trivial on a small programme, so ask how that fee is structured and whether it is charged once per colour or per attempt. On lead time, the honest answer is that colour approval typically adds one to three weeks to a new programme's first delivery, and considerably less to a repeat, because the recipe is known. A mill that quotes a shorter timeline for a completely new shade is either confident or has not thought about it, and it is worth asking which.

One note on sequencing for brands with many colours: each additional colour is a separate colour development, and the total time to develop a palette is very close to the time to develop one colour plus the number of additional colours multiplied by the per-colour cycle. If a seasonal range has eight shades, ask for a schedule that treats the palette as a programme rather than as eight unrelated requests, and consider whether a narrower palette with more deliberate design would serve the range better.

A Working Colour Approval Workflow

Pulling this into a process that a brand and a mill can both run without ambiguity:

1. Fix the construction first. Fibre, weave, width, finish, decoration method. Nothing about colour is meaningful until this is settled.

2. Set the target in writing. A physical reference plus a written description of the shade, including a note on what you are trying to achieve — for instance, "a pink that still reads pink in warm retail lighting, not orange".

3. Agree the tolerance and the method before any dip is produced. dE threshold, light source, and where the final measurement happens.

4. Receive and evaluate lab dips under controlled light. Daylight-balanced, not office fluorescent. If you cannot evaluate the dips properly, do not approve them.

5. Approve a physical standard. Seal a length of approved bulk with date and lot, and keep it. This becomes the reference for everything that follows.

6. Validate at production scale. A pre-production sample from the actual run, in finished form, measured against the standard.

7. Verify at dispatch and at receipt. Both, recorded, against the same standard and the same illuminant.

8. Re-verify on the first reorder. Reorders are where colour quietly drifts, and they are the point at which a standard earns its keep.

The whole sequence costs maybe two weeks of lead time on a new programme and removes the category of problem that generates the most expensive conversations.

The Working Programme

If you do only a few things from this guide, do these:

This week. Find your last three ribbon deliveries. Did anyone measure them against a reference? If not, you have no colour control, and everything else on this list depends on fixing that first.

This month. Build a physical standards file. One sealed length per colour per programme, labelled with construction, date and lot. It costs an afternoon and it is the single highest-value artefact in this entire area.

Next programme. Write the five-part colour clause into the purchase order or the quality agreement: construction, target and reference, tolerance, method and light source, remedy. Send it as a draft and let the mill mark it up — a clause they have reviewed is a clause they will meet.

Next audit. Ask the four dye house questions. A mill that answers them precisely and shows you a measurement record has a system. A mill that answers them vaguely has a person.

Ongoing. Measure every delivery, not just the first. Keep the numbers. Colour problems are always visible in the trend before they are visible in any single delivery, and a programme that measures will always catch a drift before it becomes a complaint.

Colour is the part of a ribbon specification that most often fails quietly and is most often blamed on the mill. It is worth remembering that it is a joint specification: the mill controls the recipe, the buyer controls the reference and the tolerance, and neither can do the job alone. A buyer who arrives with a construction, a physical standard, a tolerance and a method will get predictable colour. A buyer who arrives with the phrase "same pink as before" will get a surprise, and the surprise will arrive on the same dock as the seasonal launch.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a lab dip in ribbon production, and how many are usually needed?

A lab dip is a small swatch of the actual construction dyed at production concentration with the intended dye class. Its purpose is to test whether a particular recipe on a particular fibre reaches the target shade. Typically two to four dips are produced for a new colour, from which one is selected, and sometimes a further round is needed after colour correction. The count depends on how difficult the target is and how settled the mill's understanding of the colour already is.

2. Will a ribbon colour matching a Pantone code exactly?

No, and it is worth being clear about why. Pantone describes a colour as it appears in a simulated print condition on a coated or uncoated paper stock under a standard illuminant. A ribbon is a dyed textile with a specific weave, fibre and sheen, and the mapping between printed colour and dyed textile is approximate. A mill targeting a Pantone reference will land close to it, and the practical question is whether that closeness is sufficient for your product, which is a decision that needs a physical comparison rather than a code.

3. What delta E value should I specify for ribbon colour?

For most solid-colour commercial programmes, a dE of 1.0 to 2.0 measured under a defined illuminant is a workable target. Below 1.0 is appropriate for flagship colours where the programme depends on the shade being exact. Above 2.0 becomes visible side by side, and above 3.0 is generally unacceptable for a solid-colour programme. Set the threshold against your own product risk rather than a generic table, and state the light source alongside it, because metameric failures can pass under one illuminant and fail under another.

4. Why does the bulk roll not match the lab dip I approved?

The usual causes are structural rather than mysterious. The dip may have been taken on a different construction, or not finished the same way as production. Scale-up from laboratory to production kettle can shift concentration, bath ratio, temperature or dwell time. A new fibre lot may require a recipe adjustment. In-house inspection may have happened under workshop lighting rather than your reference conditions. Asking the mill which of these occurred converts a complaint into a diagnosis.

5. Should I ask the mill to measure colour before dispatch?

Yes, and it is a reasonable expectation for any brand programme with repeat volume. Ask what is measured, against which retained standard, with what instrument and under which illuminant, and request the result attached to the shipment. Mills that regularly supply brand programmes with strict colour requirements do this as standard, and it shifts the dispute from a rejection conversation to a measurement conversation.

6. How should I store ribbon so the colour does not fade or drift?

Store in the dark, at stable temperature and humidity, in opaque or light-blocking packaging, and away from direct sunlight and strong artificial light. Keep rolls in their original winding rather than re-winding them, because re-winding introduces creases and handling marks that are themselves a defect. Ribbons with a satin or lustre finish are particularly vulnerable to handling damage, so avoid compressing rolls under heavier goods, and keep reel cores with the ribbon if you intend to put the stock onto a rewinder later.

7. How long does ribbon colour development take?

For a new colour on a settled construction, expect roughly one to three weeks from specification to an approved production sample, including the lab dip rounds and one colour correction cycle. A first bulk run typically adds another one to two weeks. Repeat programmes are faster because the recipe is known, often two to four weeks for the first delivery. If a supplier quotes materially less for an entirely new shade, it is worth asking what is being skipped, because the skipped step is usually the production-scale validation that protects the first bulk delivery.

8. Does colour approval need to be repeated for every order?

No, not as a full approval. Once a standard is approved and the mill retains the recipe and a master batch, reorders normally need only a quick confirmation against the retained standard, and the mill's own pre-dispatch measurement should be sufficient. What is worth repeating periodically is a physical check against your sealed reference, particularly after a long period of inactivity, after a change of fibre lot, or when you are about to commit a large volume to a seasonal programme.

9. What is the difference between a shade, a colour and a colour fastness requirement?

They are three different things that buyers routinely conflate. A shade is which colour it is. A colour standard or tolerance defines how close to the shade the delivery must be. Colour fastness defines how well the colour holds up under exposure to light, rubbing, washing, perspiration or water, and it is a performance property rather than an appearance property. A ribbon can match its approved shade perfectly and still fail a fastness requirement badly, so the two need to be specified separately if your product is exposed to any of those conditions.
