Retailer Due Diligence: What a Ribbon Supplier Is Actually Selling You

When a category buyer shortlists ribbon suppliers, the evaluation usually starts with the wrong document. The certificate arrives, the price list is quoted, the showroom photos look good — and the decision is made on a feeling that the supplier "looks solid". Six months later, the goods arrive with a shade that does not match the approved lab dip, a carton count that does not reconcile with the packing list, and a supplier who is genuinely surprised by the complaint because nothing in the process ever established what was actually agreed.

That is not a supplier failure. That is an evaluation failure. Due diligence in ribbon sourcing is not a background check you run once before signing; it is a set of controls that determine whether the goods you receive are the goods you specified. The purpose of this guide is to lay out what a serious buyer verifies, in what order, and what evidence to keep in the file when a claim is later raised.

Since 2004, Smith Ribbon in Xiamen has supplied branded ribbon, bows, tassels and decorative trim to brand owners, gift-packaging buyers and retailers across more than 50 countries. That means we sit on both sides of this process — the one being audited and the one doing the auditing. This guide is written from the factory floor outward, because the differences between a supplier who passes due diligence and one who merely holds certificates are visible on the production floor long before they appear in your inbox.

Step One: Establish Identity Before You Establish Capability

Almost every supplier shortlist collapses at the first stage, and the reason is mundane: the buyer confirms the supplier is capable but never confirms the supplier is who they say they are. The entity on the invoice, the entity that owns the factory, the entity holding the certificates, and the entity that will actually run your production can be four different legal structures. In a trading-company arrangement that is completely normal and legitimate. In a direct-factory arrangement that you believe you have, it is a material misrepresentation of the supply relationship — and it changes your entire risk model.

Three things should be verified before any commercial discussion goes further:

  • Business licence and registered scope. The registered name, Unified Social Credit Code, registered address and business scope should cover textile or trim manufacturing and trading. A scope that lists only "cultural and creative products" while the factory runs looms tells you something useful about how the paperwork will behave when a claim needs to be made.
  • Export registration and the actual shipper of record. If you are buying FOB Xiamen, the exporter named on the invoice should match the entity that signed the contract. If it does not, you need to know why before you fund a production run.
  • Factory address versus office address. Visit or audit the address where the goods are actually made. A company with a prestigious downtown office and no weaving capability is a trading company, and you should price it as one.

The fastest way to establish which kind of supplier you have is to ask one direct question: which steps of the ribbon process happen in your own building? A manufacturer answers with the process — winding, warping, weaving, dyeing, finishing, printing, slitting, assembly. A trading company answers with a list of partners. The answer tells you what your quotation is actually buying: a factory's margin, or a trading company's margin plus a factory's margin.

Step Two: Verify Capability Against the Work You Will Actually Run

A supplier's general capability is not the same as its capability on your product. A mill that runs 200,000 metres of 38 mm grosgrain every month may have no jacquard looms, no heat-transfer line and no velvet capacity. This is the single most common cause of a sourcing relationship that starts smoothly and then stalls.

Capability verification should be done against your specific brief, not against a capability brochure. For each element of the brief, confirm that the equipment exists, is currently running, and is available for your volume:

  • Construction and width. Does the mill run the exact width, and at the speed your order implies? Narrow-width weaving on 6 mm and 13 mm requires different looms from 38 mm and 100 mm.
  • Material. Polyester satin, RPET, organza, velvet, grosgrain and wire-edged constructions each have distinct process routes. Ask which base substrate the mill runs itself and which it buys in.
  • Decoration. Screen print, foil stamping, heat transfer, jacquard weave and embroidery are separate capital lines. The decoration method in your brief must map to a specific machine in a specific room.
  • Finishing. Does the mill do its own edge-locking, mercerising, calendaring, wire-insertion and hemming, or does it outsource? Edge locking in particular determines selvedge quality and is very visible on finished product.
  • Assembly. If your brief includes pre-tied bows, cut-and-fold bows, cake ribbon sets or co-packed kits, confirm the assembly line exists separately from the ribbon line.

Ask for the machine list with approximate loom count and width range. It sounds like a trivial request, but a factory with eight wide looms cannot serve a brand programme that needs 100,000 metres of 12 mm trim at 90-day intervals, no matter how good the sample looks.

Step Three: Test the Quality System on Paper and on the Floor

Quality management systems are the easiest thing for a supplier to hold and the easiest thing for a buyer to over-trust. ISO 9001 certification tells you a documented system exists and has been audited by a third party. It does not tell you that the system is applied to your specific product, or that anyone in the building will use it on your order.

Verification at this stage has two halves. The paper half: request the certificate itself, confirm the issuing body's accreditation, check the certificate number against the issuer's public database, confirm the scope covers the site you are buying from, and check the expiry date. A certificate that names a different address than your production site is not covering your production site. This is worth stating plainly because it is a routine finding and it is rarely questioned.

The floor half: ask how the system touches a specific order. Concretely:

  • What is the incoming inspection standard for yarn and greige fabric, and who records it?
  • Is there a written colour tolerance, and against what instrument is it measured?
  • Is there a pre-shipment inspection with a defined sampling plan, and is photographic evidence produced?
  • How is a non-conformance recorded, and who is allowed to release a concession?

A manufacturer that answers these concretely — with document names, frequencies and names of roles — has a working system. A manufacturer that answers with a general description of "total quality management" is reciting a brochure. At Smith Ribbon the same logic applies internally: our QC records are per lot, our colour checks are instrumental, and a lot that fails inspection is quarantined rather than released with a note.

Step Four: Check Capacity Against Your Own Forecast

Capacity is the most under-verified item in ribbon due diligence, and the one that most often generates emergency air freight during a peak season. Suppliers are asked "what is your monthly capacity" as if capacity were a single number. It is not. Capacity is a function of loom count, width compatibility, colour availability, decoration method, shift pattern and current load — and it moves every week.

A useful due diligence question is not "what is your capacity" but "what is your available capacity for my construction, my width and my decoration method, in week 34 of this year, given what you have already booked". A supplier who can answer that question precisely is running real scheduling. A supplier who answers with a peak number is selling you capacity they have already sold to someone else.

At Smith Ribbon our daily output is around 100,000 metres across the plant, but the honest answer to a capacity question always specifies construction and width, because a 6 mm jacquard order and a 100 mm grosgrain order do not draw on the same looms. For buyers, the practical step is to make your forecast a dated document with a locked horizon, then require the supplier to respond against it in writing.

Step Five: Verify Financial and Continuity Signals

Most buyers verify financials only when they suspect a problem. By then the problem is usually visible in the goods. A basic continuity review each year, and before any peak season commitment, is inexpensive and highly protective.

Useful signals include: years in operation and whether the legal entity has changed; whether the factory owns its production space or leases, and on what terms; the size and age of the loom fleet and whether capital investment is visible; whether the workforce is stable or heavily seasonal; and whether the supplier can name their own upstream dependencies — dye house, jacquard greige supplier, foil supplier — and describe what happens if one of them stops. A supplier with a single upstream dependency for a critical material should be treated as a single-source risk even if the ribbon itself is made in-house.

The most direct continuity question is a switching-cost question: if this supplier disappeared tomorrow, how long would it take to qualify a replacement and land equivalent goods? Buyers who ask it annually tend to be the buyers who never need to use the answer.

Step Six: Confirm the Commercial Terms Are Complete

Due diligence is not finished when the capability is confirmed; it is finished when the commercial terms remove the ambiguity that creates disputes later. The single most valuable document in a ribbon supply relationship is not the purchase order — it is the specification and the quality agreement that sit behind it. At minimum, the following should be settled in writing before the first production order:

  • Specification. Construction, material, width, thickness tolerance, colour reference and colour tolerance, decoration method, packaging format, labelling requirements and the test methods that define acceptance.
  • Approval regime. Which samples must be approved before production — lab dip, strike-off, pre-production sample, PPAP-style pack approval — and who signs each one.
  • Acceptance criteria. Sampling plan, acceptable quality limit, defect definitions, and the evidence the supplier must produce at pre-shipment inspection.
  • Defect remedy. What happens on a failed lot: rework, replacement, credit, or return, and in what proportion. A ladder with defined percentages and a defined timeline resolves most disputes before they start.
  • Price mechanics. Currency, Incoterm, what the price includes and excludes, price-adjustment triggers such as raw material or freight movement, and the price-protection window on reorders.
  • Lead time and tolerance. Production lead time, transit time, and the agreed tolerance on both.

Two of these are routinely skipped by first-time buyers and both cause predictable problems. The first is the colour tolerance: without an agreed Delta E limit, "matches the approved sample" becomes an argument. The second is the reorder price-protection window: without one, every reorder is a fresh negotiation, and the buyer quietly accepts a price increase they did not agree to.

Step Seven: Build the File You Will Need in Year Three

The seventh step is the one buyers skip and regret. The documentation you assemble during due diligence is the documentation you will need the first time a claim is raised, a retailer audits your supplier, or a compliance question arrives from a legal team. Assemble it once, properly, and store it where it can be retrieved by someone who was not in the room.

A complete supplier file for a ribbon programme contains: the supplier's business licence and export registration; current certificates with expiry dates recorded; the approved specification and colour standard with the signed approval record; the sample ladder with approval dates; incoming and pre-shipment inspection reports for recent lots; the signed commercial terms and price-adjustment clause; and a note of every quality incident with its outcome. If your brand makes sustainability or recycled-content claims, the chain-of-custody evidence for that claim belongs in the same file, not in a separate folder someone cannot find.

Two disciplines make this file trustworthy. Record expiry dates in a single calendar with alerts at ninety, sixty and thirty days. And, for every certificate and every approved colour, record which specific site and which specific product scope it covers — because "the supplier is certified" and "this product is certified" are different claims, and retailers increasingly ask for the second one.

What a Good Audit Looks Like

If you visit or audit, go with a sequence rather than a checklist tour. Arrive on a production day, not a showroom day. Ask to watch a lot run from greige through finishing. Look at the quarantine area — a functioning QMS has visible holds, and a non-functioning one has an empty one. Ask to see the most recent customer complaint and how it was closed; the answer quality tells you more than any presentation. Check that the certificate on the wall matches the site you are standing in and the product you are about to buy. Take your own samples, not theirs alone, and keep them sealed as your reference stock.

At Smith Ribbon, audits are welcome and we would rather a buyer took independent samples of our own production than approved a supplier's selection. Our plant is 15,000 m² with over 200 employees, and we have held OEKO-TEX, FSC, BSCI, SEDEX/SMETA and ISO 9001 since long before private-label programmes became standard. A buyer who wants to see whether a quality system is real can watch it operate on someone else's order instead of ours.

The Mistakes That Cost Buyers the Most

Buying from the entity that quotes, not the entity that makes. A low price from a trading company competing against a factory is not a bargain — it is a margin difference that has to come from somewhere. Ask what is being sold: the factory's output, or a factory's output plus an intermediary.

Treating certificates as evidence of capability. A certificate is evidence of a documented system and an audited site. Capability lives on the floor.

Accepting a colour sample without a numeric standard. Samples age, batches drift and the reference disappears. Put the number in the specification.

Not dating the forecast. Capacity commitments without a horizon are wishes. Give the supplier a dated forecast and require a written response against it.

Skipping the reorder price-protection window. The second order should not be a fresh negotiation, and a buyer who never secures the window never discovers how much it was worth.

Letting the file live in an inbox. When the audit or the claim comes, an inbox is not a record.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, category buyers and procurement managers most often ask us about verifying a ribbon supplier. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between supplier due diligence and a factory audit?

Due diligence is the desktop and record-based verification done before you commit: legal identity, business scope, certificate validity and scope, capability mapping, financial and continuity signals, and completeness of commercial terms. A factory audit is the on-site, physical verification of those same claims — watching production run, checking quarantine holds, taking your own samples. The two work together. Paper verification without a floor check accepts whatever a supplier writes; a floor visit without paper verification accepts whatever paperwork is produced for the day. Most serious programmes do both, with the audit scheduled before the first production order rather than after a problem.

2. How do I tell whether a ribbon supplier is a factory or a trading company?

Ask one direct question: which steps of the ribbon process happen in your own building? A factory answers with the process — winding, warping, weaving, dyeing, finishing, printing, slitting, assembly — and can show you the machines. A trading company answers with a list of partner mills. Then verify it against the address on the business licence and the address where the goods will actually be made. If those differ from the site you visited or audited, you are buying through an intermediary, which is entirely legitimate, but you should price it that way and understand that your quality claims travel one step further.

3. Should I reject a supplier whose certificate does not cover my production site?

It depends on what the certificate covers versus what you are buying. A certificate naming a different legal entity or a different address does not certify the site producing your goods. That is not automatically disqualifying — the site may have equivalent certifications under a different certificate number, or the certificate may simply be filed under a subsidiary — but it is something you must resolve in writing before you order. Ask for the certificate that actually names the production site and confirm the number directly with the issuing body. If no in-scope certificate can be produced for the site, treat that as a genuine gap in your compliance file rather than a technicality.

4. How do I verify a supplier's production capacity for my order?

Replace the question 'what is your capacity' with a specific one: what is your available capacity for my construction, my width and my decoration method, in the specific weeks I need it, given what you have already booked. Capacity is not a single number — it depends on loom count, width compatibility, colour availability, decoration method, shift pattern and current load, and it changes weekly. A supplier who answers precisely is running real scheduling. Give them a dated forecast with a locked horizon and require the answer in writing against that forecast, so capacity becomes a commitment rather than an estimate.

5. What should a ribbon supplier due diligence file contain?

The supplier's business licence and export registration; current certificates with expiry dates; the approved specification and colour standard with the signed approval record; the sample ladder with approval dates; incoming and pre-shipment inspection reports for recent lots; the signed commercial terms including the price-adjustment clause; and a record of every quality incident with its outcome. If you make sustainability or recycled-content claims, the chain-of-custody evidence belongs in the same file. Record expiry dates in a single calendar with alerts at ninety, sixty and thirty days, and for every certificate record which site and product scope it covers — 'the supplier is certified' and 'this product is certified' are different claims.

6. How long should a first-time ribbon supplier evaluation take?

Plan on four to six weeks from first contact to a signed specification with an approved sample, longer if a custom colour, custom construction or new decoration method is involved, because colour development and sample rounds drive the timeline. The paper-only portion — identity, certificates, capability mapping, capacity answer, commercial terms — can realistically be closed in two to three weeks if the supplier is organised. A supplier who takes much longer to produce a coherent quotation with a clear structure is telling you something about how their internal communication works, which will be relevant later when you need a fast answer to an urgent problem.
