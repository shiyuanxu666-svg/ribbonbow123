The core insight: a jacquard ribbon is not a plain ribbon with a pattern added. The pattern is the structure of the fabric. That single distinction drives everything else on this page - the minimum order, the unit price, the lead time, the way you brief a designer, the way you approve samples, and whether your reorder lands as the same ribbon your customer already loves.

If you are sourcing patterned ribbon for gift packaging, apparel trim, home textiles or a seasonal collection, the difference between jacquard and printed ribbon is not an aesthetic preference. It is a different commercial structure, with different economics and different failure modes. A buyer who treats a jacquard programme like a printed one will either pay too much or end up with a pattern that drifts between orders.

This guide is written for brand owners, product developers and procurement managers who need to decide between woven jacquard, printed ribbon and solid ribbon, and who need to understand the mechanics well enough to write a brief a mill can actually quote against. It covers how the weave is built, why minimum order quantities behave the way they do, how repeat patterns are managed across reorder cycles, where the cost sits, which design decisions move your unit price, and how colour and pattern repeat are controlled from batch to batch.

We have kept the language practical. Jacquard is often discussed in terms that are intimidating to a buyer who simply wants a lovely ribbon for a gift box, and the practical reality is much more approachable once you understand that you are asking a mill to change a few needles in a loom, not to commission a textile artwork.

Jacquard Is Not a Decoration. It Is the Ribbon.

Start with the mechanism, because everything else follows from it.

A printed ribbon starts life as plain, undyed or solid-dyed cloth. The pattern is then applied on top, usually by screen printing, rotary printing or heat transfer, using pigment or disperse dye. The underlying construction stays simple: the same warp and weft, the same width, the same base cloth, whatever the decoration.

A jacquard ribbon starts life as a patterned cloth. A jacquard loom - named after the inventor of the mechanism - can raise individual warp threads independently of the weft, creating a pattern directly in the weave. Because the pattern is formed by the interlacing of the threads rather than by a layer of colour sitting on top, a jacquard ribbon has no separate print step. The pattern is intrinsic to the fabric.

There is a third route, which sits between the two: woven patterns achieved by dobby or auxiliary attachments on a plain loom, and printed stripes on a jacquard base, where a structural pattern is woven in and colour is then added. Hybrid constructions are common in commercial ribbons, and understanding which part is woven and which part is printed tells you where the repeat risk sits.

Three practical consequences follow immediately:

There is no separate decoration step, so there is no decoration setup. Printed programmes carry a screen or plate setup that is often amortised across the order. A jacquard programme does not have that particular cost, but it does have a loom setup: a new pattern requires the mill to build or re-thread the loom to produce it. That work has to be paid for somehow, and understanding where it lands is the subject of the tooling section below.

Jacquard has a minimum viable pattern complexity that printed does not. A weave has physical resolution. You can print a six-pointed star or a fine hairline. You cannot weave them at that density on a ribbon loom. Patterns below roughly 60 to 70 picks per centimetre will not render properly, and very fine detail in a small repeat either blurs or disappears. A jacquard design must be simplified to the grid the loom can physically resolve, and that constraint has to be respected at design stage, not discovered at strike-off.

Jacquard tends to be more physically stable. Because the pattern is in the structure, it does not crack, peel or abrade off the surface the way a printed layer can. For a ribbon that will be handled, folded, tied and washed, that durability is often the entire justification for choosing jacquard over print. It is also why jacquard usually commands a higher unit price: you are paying for structure, not for pigment.

How a Jacquard Pattern Is Actually Built

To brief a mill intelligently - and to read a quote intelligently - you need to know how the pattern is described. Four terms do most of the work.

The repeat. This is the smallest repeating tile of the pattern, measured along the length of the ribbon. A 4 cm repeat means the tile is 4 cm long. Repeat length matters commercially because it determines how frequently the pattern renews visually along the ribbon, and it also determines how much fabric is consumed in the pattern setup at the loom ends before saleable goods begin.

The pick count. This is the horizontal resolution - how many thread positions make up one repeat across the width of the ribbon. A higher pick count means a finer, more detailed pattern. Pick count is the constraint that decides whether your artwork can be realised. On a 19 mm ribbon, the usable width between selvedges is narrow, and every pick is a fraction of a millimetre of visible pattern. Wide jacquard ribbons and fine jacquard ribbons are technically different products with different achievable detail.

Colour ways. A jacquard programme may be single-colour (pattern achieved purely through weave structure and the play of light and shadow), two-colour, or multi-colour with additional yarn colours carried in the warp or weft. Every additional colour adds yarns, adds a changeover, adds complexity to the colour repeat control, and adds cost. Single-colour jacquard is dramatically simpler to repeat accurately than multi-colour, and that is a real strategic option for brands that want pattern without the complexity.

Yarn content and construction. Jacquard can be made in polyester, rayon, silk, nylon, cotton or blends, on satin, grosgrain, organza, taffeta or other grounds. The ground construction and the yarn blend determine the drape, the handle, the price and the surface the pattern appears on. A jacquard on a satin ground looks like the pattern is under glass; a jacquard on a grosgrain ground reads more textured and more graphic. This is a design decision with a large cost consequence, and it should be made explicitly rather than inherited from whatever the mill defaults to.

One more structural point worth understanding. Many commercial jacquard ribbons are woven with a selvedge - a denser, usually plain edge that keeps the weave stable. The selvedge is not decorative; it is what makes the ribbon hold its width and stop unravelling. When you are comparing a jacquard supplier's sample to a competitor's, the selvedge quality is often the most telling difference, and it is the first thing to check.

Jacquard, Printed and Solid: A Procurement Comparison

Here is the practical comparison most buyers need. This is not a quality ranking - a well-executed printed ribbon can be better than a poorly-executed jacquard one - it is a comparison of how the three behave commercially.

Solid ribbon is the cheapest and simplest to repeat. There is no pattern to match, so colour continuity is the only concern, and that is a well-understood problem. MOQ is lowest, lead time is shortest, unit cost is lowest, and the risk of visual drift between orders is minimal. If your design intent is a clean ribbon with brand colour, solid is almost always the correct answer. Choose jacquard for structural or aesthetic reasons, never because it sounds more premium.

Printed ribbon sits in the middle on every axis. The base cloth can be simple, so the material cost stays low. The pattern can be as complex as your artwork allows, including very fine detail, gradients and photographic effects. The setup cost is a screen or plate that is typically amortised across the order. MOQ is moderate, and the failure mode is the one buyers know: the print wears, fades or cracks, and the colour depends on the print process. Printed is the right choice when detail matters more than durability, when the pattern is more illustrative than structural, or when the pattern changes seasonally.

Jacquard ribbon is the most expensive per metre, carries the highest structural MOQ, and has the longest first-order lead time - because the loom has to be set up for the pattern. What it buys you is pattern that is part of the fabric, that does not degrade with handling, that has a genuine tactile and light-reflectance quality printed ribbon cannot reproduce, and that reads as a considered design choice rather than a decoration. Jacquard is the right choice for a core brand pattern, a gift set that has to survive handling, an apparel trim where the pattern must be durable, and any pattern you intend to reorder repeatedly for years.

There is a fourth path worth naming: woven trim or tape with a jacquard section, where a plain ground is combined with a patterned band. This is common in apparel trim and in decorative tape, and it lets a brand adopt a signature pattern at a lower volume than a full-width jacquard would require, because the patterned section consumes less of the loom's capacity.

Why Jacquard MOQ Is Structurally Different

Minimum order quantity in a jacquard programme is not set by a commercial policy. It is set by physics and by setup economics, and understanding this prevents a lot of wasted negotiation.

There are three separate contributions to the effective minimum.

The loom setup consumption. To produce a specific pattern, the mill must build the loom programme - the card, punch or electronic configuration that tells the loom which threads to lift for each pick of the repeat. The mill then has to weave through the pattern setup at the start of the run before saleable ribbon emerges, and often continues to weave pattern waste at the end. For a long repeat on a narrow ribbon, this setup waste can be a significant number of metres. That material is consumed on your order. If your order is small, you are paying for a disproportionate amount of setup relative to good product.

The yarn commitment. Jacquard weaving requires specific yarns, and often specific yarn colours, in specific quantities to build the warp and weft. A mill that holds a limited stock of a particular yarn will set a minimum that reflects what they need to consume to justify opening the yarn lot, rather than a neat round number.

The mill's commercial policy on pattern programmes. Because a new pattern ties up loom capacity that could run a stocked, repeatable product, mills price new-pattern runs higher and set a floor. This is the part you can negotiate, and it is worth negotiating - but the underlying physical minimums are not negotiable and pushing against them creates the failures described later.

The practical consequence: the MOQ for a new jacquard pattern is typically several times the MOQ for the same ribbon in solid or printed form, and it falls sharply once the pattern is in repeat production. A mill that is already running your pattern can often weave additional lengths for a smaller increment, because the loom programme is already built and the yarns are already in place.

This is why the commercial question for a brand is not "what is your MOQ" but "what is your MOQ for a first run, and what is your incremental MOQ for reorders". Those are two different numbers, and the gap between them is where the economics of a jacquard programme live. A brand that commits to a repeat pattern and reorders on a schedule can negotiate far better unit economics than one that treats every order as a one-off, even at the same mill.

There is a further nuance around small-volume jacquard that matters for emerging brands: jacquard with a long repeat can sometimes be woven on a narrower supplementary attachment rather than a full jacquard loom, at lower setup cost and lower minimums, with less resolution. Mill terminology for this varies, and a supplier who mentions it is offering you something genuinely useful. Ask directly whether your design can be run on a supplementary or small-pattern loom.

Repeat Patterns, Repeat Orders and the Tooling Question

Every jacquard programme raises the same question: who owns the pattern, and what happens to it when things change?

In the common case, the mill builds the loom programme for your pattern and retains it as part of their setup. This works well when you intend to reorder repeatedly and the pattern stays in the programme for years. The mill keeps the programme, you get consistent output, and nobody pays a setup charge on each reorder.

Three things can go wrong, and each needs an explicit answer in your purchase documentation.

Pattern ownership and artwork. Your artwork is yours. The mill's loom programme - the specific thread-lift instruction set derived from it - is a manufacturing artefact. Standard practice is that the buyer retains ownership of the artwork and the right to move production to another mill, with the mill retaining ownership of, or holding a lien over, the programme until paid for in full. This distinction matters enormously at the moment you need to leave, and it is the difference between a two-week resourcing exercise and a two-month one. Specify it clearly.

Programme retention and archival. A loom programme that only exists on a machine that happens to be running your pattern is a fragile dependency. Ask where the programme is stored, in what form, and for how long. Mills that run long-term brand programmes usually hold the programme in a controlled archive with a version number, and can confirm it on request. Mills that have not anticipated this may lose the programme in a machine breakdown or a reorganisation, and then your repeat order becomes a new-pattern order with a new setup charge and a new MOQ - and potentially a slightly different ribbon.

Engineering change. If you adjust the pattern - a small colour change, a modified motif, a revised scale - does that require a new programme, and is it a full first-run setup? A change of colour yarn within the existing programme is normally a routine adjustment. A change to the motif or the pick count is a new programme. Establish which changes are routine and which are re-setups, and what notice period applies to each. A small design tweak that triggers a full setup charge and a new MOQ is a nasty surprise at the worst moment.

Ask for the programme specification in writing as part of the order documentation: the repeat length, the pick count, the yarn colours and their placement, the ground construction, and the finishing specification. This document is the basis for every future reorder and every quality dispute. Without it, "the same as before" is an unenforceable instruction.

Where the Money Actually Goes in a Jacquard Programme

Jacquard quotes are harder to read than printed quotes, and the reason is that the composition of the cost is different. Rather than a price per metre plus a setup fee, the cost is spread across several interacting components.

Material. Jacquard uses more yarn per metre of finished ribbon than a comparable solid, because the pattern requires additional yarn systems and often a denser construction to hold the pattern. Multi-colour jacquard uses more still. Material cost also depends on the fibre: rayon and silk sit at the top, polyester at the middle, and there is a meaningful gap between them.

Setup and programme cost. The loom programme build, threading, and the setup waste woven out before saleable goods. This is usually quoted separately and sometimes waived at higher volumes. It is also often the number that gets quoted as a fixed charge regardless of the order size, which makes it disproportionately expensive on small runs.

Weaving time. A jacquard loom runs more slowly than a plain loom for the same width, and the pattern density affects that. This is the component that scales with volume and is where repeat economics live: the same programme running over a larger quantity amortises everything that is not weaving time.

Finishing. Jacquard frequently carries additional finishing that solid ribbon does not need - edge treatment on both selvedges, sometimes edge painting or tipping for ribbons used in specific applications, calendering or pressing to set the surface, and specific winding requirements.

Quality control. Pattern inspection is a distinct and slower operation than solid inspection. Checking pattern registration, repeat length accuracy, yarn faults in the patterned areas and selvedge quality all take time that a plain ribbon does not require. Good mills price this in; mills that do not are not doing it.

Packaging. Jacquard ribbon needs to be wound and packaged so the pattern is not distorted. Wider ribbons with longer repeats are more prone to creasing and pattern distortion if wound too tightly or re-wound carelessly.

The practical instruction for a buyer is: when you compare two jacquard quotes, ask for a line-by-line breakdown that separates setup from unit cost. A quote that blends them is impossible to negotiate, because you cannot tell whether a lower number reflects a genuinely cheaper programme or simply a setup fee pushed into the headline. Once separated, you can negotiate the setup, negotiate the unit, and model the two against your real reorder profile.

Design-to-Cost: Four Decisions That Set Your Unit Price

Jacquard has a design stage that printing does not have, and it is the stage where unit cost is largely decided. Most of the expensive surprises in jacquard programmes are design decisions made without knowing their cost consequence.

Decision one: the repeat length. A longer repeat means a more complex loom programme, more setup waste, and potentially slower weaving. A short repeat - a tight geometric motif at 2 to 3 cm - is far cheaper to run than a long pictorial repeat at 15 cm, for the same width and yarn. If your design intent survives a shorter repeat, take it. Many brands scale a motif down rather than accepting a long repeat, and the effect on both unit cost and setup waste is substantial.

Decision two: the pick count and therefore the detail. Every additional pick across the width is more thread control, more density, more risk of yarn faults and more weaving time. Reducing pick count is the direct lever on cost for a given pattern complexity. Designers frequently work at a resolution far above what the ribbon can show, because they are designing at print scale rather than at ribbon scale. Ask for the design redrawn at the actual ribbon width at final size, and check how it looks held at 30 cm - the distance at which it will be seen on a gift.

Decision three: the number of colours. Going from one colour to two to three multiplies the yarn management, the colour changeover, the colour repeat control burden and the risk. A two-colour jacquard that reads as richly as a five-colour one is a good outcome. If the pattern reads well in fewer colours, the programme is simpler, cheaper, and far easier to repeat accurately across batches.

Decision four: the ground construction and yarn blend. This is usually the largest single lever on unit price. The difference between a polyester jacquard on a grosgrain ground and a rayon jacquard on a satin ground is not marginal - it can be a multiple of the unit cost. Make this decision on the basis of how the ribbon will be used and what the brand needs to communicate, not on what the first sample happened to be. Request quotes on two constructions so the trade-off is visible rather than assumed.

A useful discipline here: brief two or three design directions with different cost profiles and ask for the price ladder alongside them. The mill can often show you what a 15% cost reduction looks like as a physical design change - a shorter repeat, fewer colours, a different ground. That conversation is far more productive than negotiating a percentage off a price you have never interrogated.

Colour and Pattern Repeat Across Batches

Once a jacquard programme is running, the standing question is whether order three looks like order one. This is a different problem from colour matching on solid ribbon, because there are more things that can drift.

Yarn lot drift. The most common cause. A jacquard programme draws yarn from specific lots, and a new lot of the same nominal shade can be slightly different. In a multi-colour jacquard, two colours that were balanced in the original lot may no longer be balanced, and the pattern's depth changes. Ask how yarn lots are controlled, whether the mill holds reserved stock against your programme, and what happens when a lot must change.

Pattern registration drift. The repeat is set by the loom programme, so registration within the repeat should be highly repeatable. What does drift is the placement of the pattern along the length - how the pattern falls relative to the cut edge of the product. If you are cutting ribbon into fixed lengths for bows, pre-cut trims or wrapping, a small shift in the pattern position changes how the pattern reads on each piece. If your programme depends on pattern position, specify it: whether the pattern must begin at a specific point on the roll, and how that is controlled and evidenced.

Yarn faults and pattern disturbance. Jacquard places yarn under varying tension across the pattern, so a single yarn fault can be pulled into the design and distort a section visibly. Mills handle this through yarn quality standards and pattern-level fault tolerance. Ask what the acceptable fault level is within the pattern specifically, and how it is judged, because "acceptable" on solid ribbon and "acceptable" on a patterned ground are not the same thing.

Finishing effects on the pattern. Calendering, heat setting, edge tipping and pressing all change the surface and can alter how the pattern catches light. A jacquard that looked right before finishing can look different after. The strike-off must therefore be finished, not just woven, if you want an honest approval.

The practical control is a retained standard: a length of approved finished jacquard ribbon, sealed, stored in the conditions that protect it from fading, and used as the reference for every subsequent batch. This is the same principle that governs solid ribbon colour continuity, applied to a more complex object. Where the programme is multi-colour, the standard should include a representative section of each colourway so the balance across colours can be judged, not just each colour individually.

Sampling Decisions: Swatch, Striped Sample, Production Strike-Off

Jacquard has a sampling ladder with real steps in it, and buyers who skip steps end up approving something the mill cannot repeat.

The woven swatch. A small woven sample showing the pattern in a single colourway. This answers "can this design be woven at all, and roughly how does it read". It does not answer anything about colour, since the swatch is usually one colourway only. Use it to confirm the design is viable before spending time on colour.

The striped sample. A longer woven length showing several colourways side by side, usually woven in sequence. This is where you evaluate the colour balance of the pattern, the relative depth of each colour, and whether the pattern reads as intended in the intended palette. It is also where you confirm the pattern does not depend on a specific colour to work.

The production strike-off. A length woven on the production loom at production settings, fully finished, from the production yarn lot, with the selvedge as it will be delivered. This is the only sample that genuinely predicts the delivered ribbon. Approving a strike-off means you have approved the loom programme, the yarn lot, the finishing and the pattern positioning together.

The rule that follows: approve colour on the striped sample, and approve the product on the strike-off. Colour approved on a swatch is a colour approved on a different yarn lot, a different construction and a different finishing state. It is not a colour approval.

Two more sampling disciplines are worth building into the programme. First, keep your approval record in a form that identifies the programme version - a dated photograph of the strike-off alongside the woven swatch and the colour reference is more durable than a sample in a drawer. Second, for a repeat programme, keep a retained sample from the production run itself, not just from approval, because it shows what the programme actually produced rather than what was planned.

How Jacquard Programmes Fail

The failure modes in jacquard sourcing are consistent enough to name, and naming them is the fastest way to avoid them.

Approval on a swatch. The single most common failure. The buyer approves a colour from a woven swatch, and the bulk arrives in a shade that nobody recognises as approved. The remedy is procedural, not technical: define in your purchase documentation exactly which sample constitutes a colour approval, and require the strike-off to be finished and from the production lot.

Designing beyond the loom. The artwork is drafted at print resolution and handed over, and the mill has to simplify it. The result is not what the designer intended, and the design was already approved internally. The remedy is to require the mill to redraw the pattern at the ribbon's actual resolution and width and return it for approval before the strike-off is produced.

Committing to a volume the pattern cannot support. A brand orders 20,000 metres on a long-repeat programme because the unit price at that volume looked attractive, and the mill cannot run it. This shows up as a delivery date slipping, a partial shipment, or a mill proposing a shorter length at a different price. The remedy is to ask for the programme's realistic maximum run length before you commit, and to hold back a reasonable share of the volume as a second call-off.

Losing the programme. The mill loses the loom programme, or retires the loom, and the next order becomes a new programme with a new setup charge, a new minimum and a slightly different ribbon. The remedy is contractual: programme retention for a defined period, and an obligation to maintain or hand over the programme specification on exit.

Treating the pattern as a one-off. The brand runs a jacquard pattern for a single season, then changes it, then discovers the reorders were not amortising the setup and the landed cost per metre was never what the quote implied. The remedy is to model the jacquard programme over its expected life - two years, three years, five - and price the volume commitment against that horizon rather than against a single purchase order.

Ignoring the garment or product interface. The jacquard looks superb on the swatch and behaves badly in the product - the pattern does not align with a seam, the width is 2 mm narrower than the seam allowance, the handle is too stiff for the application. The remedy is to bring the end product or a representative product sample into the development stage, not into the final approval stage.

Structuring a Jacquard Programme That Works

Pulling the threads together, here is a structure that has repeatedly worked for brand programmes buying patterned ribbon.

Start with the design, but design to the loom. Establish the repeat length, the pick count the ribbon width can physically carry, the colour count and the ground construction before anything is drawn in full colour. If you cannot get these parameters from your mill early, you are designing blind. Ask for the mill's achievable limits for your ribbon width as an early deliverable.

Get a price ladder, not a single price. Ask for quotes at three volumes, with setup shown separately at each. This gives you the amortisation curve and lets you see where the programme turns from setup-dominated to volume-dominated. It also gives you something to negotiate against.

Agree the programme specification before the first order. Repeat length, pick count, yarn colours and placement, ground construction, finishing, selvedge treatment, edge condition and the pattern-position requirement - all written down, referenced in the purchase order, and treated as the basis for reorder.

Build the sample ladder and approve at the right level. Woven swatch for design viability, striped sample for colour balance, finished strike-off from the production lot for product approval. Record all three. A colour approval is only valid when it comes from the striped sample or above.

Define the repeat mechanism. A retained finished standard, stored and protected; a pre-dispatch comparison against it; a defined route for a batch that falls outside tolerance; and a defined notification process if the mill needs to change a yarn lot.

Commit volume over a horizon, not on an order. A jacquard programme with a committed reorder profile behaves like a partnership. One without it behaves like a series of expensive one-offs. Where your business genuinely cannot commit, find a structure that reduces the risk for both sides - a shared setup charge, a first order large enough to amortise, or a programme the mill is willing to keep alive for a stated period.

Set the exit terms at the start. Who owns the artwork, who holds the programme, what happens if you move to another mill, how long the programme is guaranteed to be retained, and what evidence of the programme specification you receive at the end. These clauses cost nothing to agree at the beginning and are worth a great deal at the end.

None of this is exotic. It is the difference between treating a jacquard programme as an art request and treating it as a manufacturing relationship with an artistic component - which is what it is. The mills that run the best long-term jacquard programmes for brands are the ones that have a structured handover process, and buyers who supply a structured brief get a structured response. The programme you can repeat is the programme you can plan around.

For brand buyers working with a manufacturer who can weave patterns in house, the practical starting point is a design review with the mill's own technical team - a half day spent agreeing repeat length, pick count, colour count and ground construction will save weeks of sampling and several rounds of rework.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the difference between jacquard ribbon and printed ribbon?

Jacquard is woven: the pattern is created by the interlacing of warp and weft threads on a loom that can lift individual threads independently, so there is no separate print step and the pattern cannot crack or peel off. Printed ribbon starts as plain cloth and has the pattern applied on top in a second operation using pigment or disperse dye, which allows finer detail but is less durable under handling and abrasion.

2. Why is the minimum order quantity for jacquard ribbon higher?

Three physical reasons combine. The loom has to be programmed and set up for the specific pattern, and setup waste is woven out before saleable ribbon emerges. The programme also requires specific yarns in specific quantities, which may have to be opened as a lot. And a new pattern ties up loom capacity that could otherwise run stocked product, so mills price new-pattern runs with a commercial floor. Once the pattern is in repeat production the incremental minimum falls sharply, because the programme and the yarns are already in place.

3. How much jacquard ribbon should I order to get a reasonable unit price?

There is no universal metre figure, because it depends on repeat length, ribbon width, pick count and colour count, all of which affect setup waste. The useful approach is to ask for quotes at three volumes with the setup charge shown separately at each. That produces the amortisation curve for your specific programme, and you can see where the programme moves from being setup-dominated to volume-dominated. Committing volume over a multi-year horizon rather than per order produces a materially better price.

4. Does jacquard ribbon keep the same colour between production batches?

It can, but it needs an active control mechanism rather than luck. The dominant cause of drift is yarn lot variation - a new lot of the same nominal shade is not identical to the original, and in a multi-colour jacquard two colours that were balanced in the original lot may no longer be balanced against each other. Ask the mill how yarn lots are controlled, whether they hold reserved stock against your programme, and what the notification process is when a lot must change. The control mechanism is a retained finished standard - a sealed length of approved ribbon - used as the reference for every subsequent batch, together with a pre-dispatch comparison against it and a defined route for a batch that falls outside tolerance.

5. What information should be in a jacquard ribbon specification?

At minimum: the repeat length, the pick count or pattern resolution, the yarn colours with their placement in the warp and weft, the ground construction and yarn content, the finishing specification, the selvedge treatment, the edge condition, and the pick-up or colour sequence notes for the pattern. This document is the basis for every reorder and every quality dispute, so without it 'the same as before' is an unenforceable instruction. It should also record whether the pattern must begin at a specific position along the roll, which matters if you cut ribbon into fixed lengths for bows or pre-cut trims and want the pattern to fall consistently on each piece.

6. Who owns the jacquard pattern, and what happens if I change supplier?

Your artwork remains yours, and the right to move production to another mill should be explicit in the purchase documentation. The loom programme - the specific thread-lift instruction set derived from your artwork - is a manufacturing artefact, and mills commonly retain ownership of it or hold a lien over it until paid in full. What matters most is the exit position: ask where the programme is stored, in what form, under what version control and for how long it will be retained. If a programme exists only on the machine running your pattern, it is a fragile dependency, and a mill that loses it turns your reorder into a new-pattern order with a new setup charge, a new minimum and a slightly different ribbon.

7. How long does it take to develop a jacquard ribbon?

Allow considerably longer than a printed programme, because the pattern has to be engineered rather than simply separated into colours. Design development and the initial weave are typically one to two weeks, the striped sample with several colourways normally takes a further one to two weeks, and the finished production strike-off usually requires another two to three weeks, with finishing and drying included. Total elapsed time from brief to approvable finished ribbon is commonly six to nine weeks for a genuinely new pattern, and considerably less for a repeat order on an existing programme. If a supplier quotes materially less for a completely new jacquard pattern, it is worth asking what has been skipped, because the skipped step is usually the production-scale strike-off that proves the programme reproduces.

8. Can I get a small volume of custom jacquard ribbon?

It depends on the pattern, and you should ask the question early rather than assuming the answer is no. The physical drivers of the minimum are setup waste and yarn commitment, and a mill holding a supplementary or small-pattern attachment may be able to weave a reduced design at a lower minimum than a full jacquard programme requires, accepting less pattern resolution. Some mills also run stock jacquard patterns that can be re-coloured or re-dyed to your specification without any programme build at all, which is dramatically cheaper and faster. Whether a small run is realistic for your design is a technical question about repeat length and complexity, so ask the mill directly what their minimum is and what design changes would bring your volume into range.

9. What causes jacquard ribbon to develop faults that are not present in solid ribbon?

Jacquard places yarn under varying tension across the pattern, so a single yarn fault can be drawn into the design and distort a section in a way that would be inconspicuous on solid ribbon. Pattern inspection is therefore a distinct and slower operation than solid inspection, because the inspector has to check pattern registration, repeat length accuracy, yarn faults within the patterned areas and selvedge quality. Acceptable fault levels for a patterned ground are not the same as for solid ribbon and should be agreed separately in the specification. Good mills price this inspection time in; mills that do not are very likely not performing it, and that is worth knowing before the first bulk order rather than after.
