What "Setting Up a Private Label Programme" Actually Means

Most first-time private label ribbon buyers arrive with a product idea and a set of samples they admire, and they expect the programme to begin with a price negotiation. In practice the negotiation is the last step, and it is the easiest step. Everything that determines whether the programme works happens earlier: what the brand stands for, what range it commits to, how the specification is written, and what gets approved at which point. A programme that skips these steps does not fail loudly. It fails as a pile of small, individually reasonable decisions that together produce a product nobody specified and a reorder nobody can repeat.

This guide walks through the setup sequence we work with brand owners and procurement managers, in the order the work actually needs to happen. It covers the brand identity decisions that should precede product development, the range planning that keeps the first order fundable, the brief that makes quotes comparable, the sample ladder and what each sample actually proves, the contract points that are worth settling before tooling exists, and the readiness checklist before a first purchase order is issued.

The perspective throughout is deliberately practical. The factory knows how to weave, dye, finish, print and pack ribbon. It does not automatically know what your brand is trying to become, which is why the brand side has to supply certain decisions explicitly rather than expecting them to be inferred from a mood board and a product photograph.

Step One: Fix the Brand Identity Before You Fix the Product

There is a particular kind of project that stalls in the middle. The brand has commissioned a visual identity, a logo, a colour palette and a tone of voice, and then the packaging conversation starts with the palette, and then the palette turns out to be unusable for ribbon because several of the colours cannot be reproduced in polyester at commercial volume, or the brand guidelines specify a finish the material cannot take, or the logo is a shape that reads badly when it is repeated along a 10 mm band. These are not factory problems. They are consequences of designing the identity without a reference to the physical substrate.

The fix is not to change the identity afterwards, which is expensive and slow, but to bring the product into the identity process at the right moment. Three specific practices help.

Start with the material, not the palette. Decide the construction first, or at least the shortlist of two or three constructions, and then test the brand colours against them. A mill can produce swatches in the brand's key colours across the candidate constructions, and this is a cheap exercise that removes months of uncertainty. The colour that looks correct on a screen and the colour that looks correct as a woven band are frequently different, and the difference is usually in saturation and in how much the material texture interferes with fine detail.

Separate the identity colours from the product colours. A brand identity needs a palette, but that palette does not need to appear on every SKU. The common pattern for a ribbon brand is a small set of core colours that carry the brand signature, and then a wider seasonal range built around or derived from them. Keeping this distinction explicit from the start prevents the situation where the brand insists that every product in the range matches the identity palette, which usually means either a narrow and expensive range or a lot of work on the colour development side of each new colourway.

Decide the decoration method early, because it constrains the logo. Screen printing handles flat single-colour artwork well and has a minimum detail size that is often larger than a logo designer expects. Foil stamping is unforgiving of fine line work. Jacquard weaving is not printing at all, and the logo becomes a woven repeat with its own dimensional constraints. Embroidery suits a different aesthetic again. Deciding the method before the artwork is finalised means the artwork gets designed for the process, rather than the process being asked to reproduce artwork designed for a screen.

One further point on identity, which is commercial rather than visual. Decide who the brand is for and what the packaging has to communicate on a shelf where there is no salesperson. That includes the language of the pack, the presence of a country of origin statement, whether the brand wants certification marks displayed, and whether the pack should carry usage guidance. These are identity decisions as much as compliance ones, and if they are deferred they will be made by default by whoever packages the goods first.

Step Two: Plan a Range You Can Reorder

The most damaging mistake in a private label launch is a range that is too broad for the first order. It feels like good strategy: broad assortment, capture more demand, let the market tell you what works. In ribbon and decorative trim, it does not work that way, because each colourway and each construction carries its own setup cost, and a broad range spreads the first order across a set of small lots that are all more expensive per unit than the same colourway would be at volume.

The arithmetic is straightforward. Suppose a colourway at five thousand units carries roughly the same setup overhead as one at five hundred units, because the setup is a dye recipe development and a print plate, not a per-unit cost. At five thousand units that overhead is a small fraction of the total; at five hundred units it dominates. The effect compounds across the range, so a twenty-SKU launch at low depth is not twenty times the cost of one SKU, it is considerably more, and the exposure at risk on the shelf is far higher because you have committed to a broad assortment of finished goods before you have any reorder data.

The structure that works is a small base with asymmetric depth.

  • One hero construction. A single base product at meaningful depth, in a colour range deep enough to be useful to a buyer who wants to match it to something. This is where the programme learns what your market wants.
  • One or two supporting constructions. A different material or a different width that serves a different use case, ordered more shallowly. These exist to signal range capability and to capture adjacent demand, not to carry the volume.
  • A defined colour structure. A small core set of colours in the hero construction that are always available, plus a seasonal addition. The core set is what you reorder; the seasonal addition is what you do not reorder if it misses.
  • A minimum order quantity that permits reordering. If the mill's minimum on your core colourway is so high that a reorder is a second full programme, then the range cannot be corrected by ordering. This is worth negotiating explicitly at the setup stage, because it is much harder to change once the programme is running.

There is a second-order decision here that matters more than it appears: what gets customised and what stays standard. A private label programme that customises everything pays development cost on every element and inherits a specification burden on every element. A programme that customises the two or three elements that carry brand meaning, and leaves the rest as specified stock, is far cheaper to extend and far easier to reorder. For ribbon, the typical pattern is that the ribbon itself is a specified construction in your colours, while the decoration, the packaging and the label content are where the brand identity lives.

Step Three: Write a Development Brief a Mill Can Quote

The single most useful document in a private label programme is the development brief, and it is the one most often replaced by a product sheet, an email thread and a sample photo. A brief that works is specific enough that two different mills would quote the same product from it. That is the test to apply.

The brief has twelve sections, and each one exists because its absence produces a later dispute.

Construction and material. The fibre and substrate, and the weave, knit or nonwoven type. Polyester satin, grosgrain, velvet, organza, jacquard and crepe are different products with different behaviour, and the difference affects not only cost but how the ribbon performs in the customer's application. If you are not sure, ask for swatches and specify by what the ribbon has to do rather than by what it is called.

Dimensions. Nominal width with a tolerance, and the length unit. Also the spool or reel geometry: core size, wound diameter, and how the ribbon is wound, because this affects how the customer uses it and therefore whether it needs a card, a bag or a dispenser. The core size is a frequent late discovery, and a ribbon wound on the wrong core can be unusable in a standard retail fixture.

Colour. The Pantone reference or an agreed physical standard, plus the tolerance band for shade matching. Also state whether approval is against a lab dip, a production drawdown, or both, and how many colourways are in scope. The colour standard and the tolerance should be written as one sentence, because "Pantone 186 C" on its own is not a specification; "within a agreed Delta E of the approved production drawdown" is.

Decoration. The method, whether artwork is supplied or drawn by the mill, the number of colours, and the repeat length for a patterned or jacquard ribbon. For printed ribbon, state the minimum detail and the registration tolerance. For foil, state the foil type and colour. These parameters determine the tooling cost and the order minimum more than anything else in the brief.

Performance. Colourfastness targets for washing, light and crocking, and the method or standard the buyer will accept as evidence. Shrinkage limits. Abrasion where the trim will be handled. This section is what converts a decorative preference into something a factory can guarantee and a retailer can accept.

Physical constraints. Minimum tensile and tear strength, and stiffness or drape if the ribbon has to sit or fold in a particular way. Wired ribbon, elastic and stiffened ribbon each need their own constraints here, and this is where a product intended for a specific use case quietly becomes a generic product.

Packaging. The consumer pack format, the master case pack, and who owns the barcode and the label content. Also the retailer's requirements if the product is going into a specific retail network, because the dieline depends on decisions made here.

Commercial. Annual volume per SKU, the acceptable price band, the target delivery date, and the Incoterm with the named place. Stating the annual volume honestly is important: it changes the mill's approach to setup and to pricing, and a low estimate that later grows is harder to handle than a modest estimate that is exceeded.

Documentation. Which certifications are required, by whom they must be held, and by when they are needed. Include the evidence format, because a certificate that exists but is in the wrong language or held under the wrong entity name does not satisfy an audit request.

Traceability. What the buyer wants retained: lot identification, dye batch records, the retained sample from each lot. This is the difference between a product you can investigate when a customer complains and one you cannot.

Change control. What constitutes a change, who may authorise one, and how it is priced. Brands frequently omit this and then discover at the second order that a substitution has been made without notice.

The discipline to apply is this: if a field is genuinely undecided, write the decision criteria rather than leaving it blank. A blank field in a brief becomes an assumption inside the quotation, and the assumption is made by the person who did not ask the question.

Step Four: Run the Sample Ladder in the Right Order

Ribbon sampling has a natural sequence, and it is worth understanding what each step proves so that approvals happen on the right evidence. Skipping a rung produces a surprise later, and surprises get expensive because they are discovered after bulk has been produced.

Stage 1: Colour development (lab dip)

A small dyed swatch in the target colour. It proves one thing: that the dyehouse can reach the colour at all. Some brand colours are simply not achievable in polyester, and the lab dip is how you find that out cheaply. It proves nothing about the finished ribbon, because weaving, finishing and decoration have not happened.

Stage 2: The strike-off or decorated sample

A sample of the finished construction with the decoration applied, showing how the colour, the surface and the print or foil work together. This is the visual and tactile approval, and it is where most buyers do their final gut check. It is usually produced on production machinery but not always under production conditions, so it remains indicative rather than final.

Stage 3: The production or pre-production sample

Cut from an actual production lot, and the only sample that can verify the specification. Width, length, selvage quality, print registration, construction, physical performance and shade can all be checked against the signed specification here. Approving on a strike-off and skipping this stage means the first time you see the real product is after it is packed.

Stage 4: The retained sample

A sample from the approved lot, held by both parties and referenced by lot number. This is the reference against which every subsequent reorder is judged. It costs almost nothing and it is the single most useful piece of documentation in the whole programme, because it is what makes a reorder argumentable instead of a matter of opinion.

The three approvals protect three different things, and it is worth stating them plainly: approve colour on the lab dip, approve appearance on the strike-off, approve specification on the production sample. A buyer who approves a lab dip as though it were the finished product will discover a shade or a surface difference after the bulk is dyed.

Step Five: Sign the Agreement Before the Work Starts

Most private label ribbon programmes run on a purchase order and a series of emails. That works until something goes wrong, at which point the question of who owns the artwork, who bears the cost of a failed inspection, or what happens if the programme is discontinued has no answer other than whatever the local custom is.

The agreement does not need to be long. It needs to cover a specific set of points, and each of them is a point that becomes expensive to settle later.

  • Specification attachment. The signed specification and the approved sample references are annexed to the agreement, so that the contract has a single unambiguous technical reference.
  • Artwork and tooling ownership. Who owns the files, who owns the physical plates and screens, what licence the mill has to continue producing, and what happens to tooling on exit.
  • Confidentiality and pre-release protection. An explicit statement about unreleased seasonal artwork, so that it does not appear in a mill's catalogue or on a trade show wall before the brand launches it. This must be written, not assumed.
  • Defect remedy. What happens when goods fail inspection: rework, replacement, credit, and what the rejection window is. The default assumption of the seller is rarely what a buyer expects.
  • Volume and lead time. The expected volume, the quoted price and its validity period, the standard lead time and the notice required for a schedule change.
  • Price adjustment mechanism. How price changes are triggered and communicated, whether by raw material index, currency or both, and the notice period. Without this, every input cost move becomes a negotiation.
  • Capacity commitment. Whether the mill commits to a reserved capacity for the programme, particularly if the brand has seasonal peaks.
  • Certification maintenance. Which certificates must remain current, who bears the cost of renewal and of the audit, and what happens to supply if one lapses.
  • Termination and exit. Notice periods, the fate of tooling, retained stock and open artwork, and how long the mill will continue to supply an existing specification after the programme ends.

The exit clause is the one that gets skipped and the one that most protects the brand. A private label ribbon specification is not trivial to recreate: the colour was developed, the decoration was set up, and the performance was tested. A brand that wants to move suppliers later needs the mill's cooperation in producing against a new mill's specification, and it needs it to be contractually available rather than a favour.

Step Six: Reach First-Order Readiness

Before the first purchase order goes out, a short readiness check clears up almost everything that can go wrong with a first order. It takes under an hour if the documentation is in order.

  • The specification is signed, and it includes the colour tolerance, the performance requirements and the approved sample references.
  • The price on the order matches the accepted quotation, and it is clear whether setup, tooling and decoration costs were amortised or charged separately.
  • The Incoterm and the named place are stated, because a change here changes the landed cost and the point at which risk transfers.
  • The packaging specification, the case pack and the barcode responsibility are confirmed in writing.
  • The certifications required are actually in place for this material and this construction, not merely held by the mill generally. This is a common and serious gap: a mill's OEKO-TEX scope covers the products it actually certifies, and a new construction may fall outside it.
  • The delivery window is realistic for the mode of transport, allowing for sea freight, customs clearance and inland delivery, not just the production date.
  • The inspection method and the rejection window are agreed, and a pre-shipment inspection has been booked if one is wanted.
  • The party that absorbs the cost of a failed inspection at arrival is stated explicitly.
  • Payment terms and the documentation required for the release of payment are agreed, so that payment is not held up by a document nobody mentioned.

On a repeat order, the check is shorter but different. Confirm that nothing has changed at the mill: no construction substitution, no dye recipe revision, no change of decoration method, no change of the core size, no change to the primary packaging substrate. A mill that quietly improves a specification is a good thing, and a mill that quietly changes one without telling you is a supply continuity problem. Ask for the current specification sheet with each reorder and compare it against the last one; in a well-run programme this takes two minutes and catches the majority of changes.

Planning the Reorder

The reorder is where a private label ribbon programme either becomes a business or becomes an inventory problem, and the difference is largely determined by decisions taken at setup.

Three mechanisms make reordering materially better. The first is the retained sample, which makes the reorder argument a comparison rather than a debate: the new lot is compared against a physical reference that both parties agree represents the approved product. The second is the agreed reorder window, where the mill commits to a standard lead time and a price validity period so that ordering does not become a fresh negotiation each time. The third, where volume allows it, is a forward commitment: the mill holds either greige ribbon in your colour or capacity against a rolling forecast, so that a reorder becomes a finishing operation rather than a full production cycle. That last one is the most valuable and the most often unavailable, because it requires the mill to carry inventory risk on your behalf, and it is therefore negotiated in the agreement rather than requested at the moment of reorder.

It is worth being honest about the seasonality risk that sits underneath all of this. Decorative trim demand is often driven by a fashion or gifting cycle that the supplier sees more clearly than the buyer does. A programme that assumes reorder capability can respond to a market that moves faster than a production cycle should cover that risk by keeping reorder quantities modest, by having the reorder path agreed and tested before it is needed, and by accepting that a colourway which misses its season should be allowed to expire rather than reordered on principle.

Finally, the metric that tells you whether the programme is working is not sell-through. It is the proportion of the range that has been reordered, and the proportion of the first order that has been re-ordered rather than merely reordered once. A range where two-thirds of SKUs reorder reliably on a predictable cycle is a healthy programme. A range where everything sells but nothing repeats is usually a range that was planned too broadly in the first place, and the fix is made in the next range plan rather than in the reorder order.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement managers most often ask us when setting up a private label ribbon programme: how many SKUs to start with, what a development brief needs to contain, how long launch takes, what each type of sample proves, who owns the artwork, what to check before the first purchase order, and how to avoid excess inventory.

1. How many SKUs should a first private label ribbon range contain?

For a first order, the practical number is smaller than most brands expect: three to six SKUs, built from one or two base constructions rather than many. The reason is that each new construction carries its own setup cost and its own lead-time risk. A new colourway on an existing construction is cheap and fast, because the dye recipe is a variant rather than a new development. A new construction, a new width or a new decoration method is a fresh development with its own sample cycle and its own chance of failure. The commercial trap is that brands plan the range around what the market would like rather than what the first order can fund. Running a full seasonal palette at launch means every colour is small, which pushes unit cost up, which erodes margin on the SKUs that were supposed to carry the programme. The better structure is one hero construction at meaningful volume, one or two supporting constructions in smaller quantities, and a clear rule that new colourways are added only after the initial range has sold through and been reordered. A range of six that reorders cleanly beats a range of twenty-four that has dead stock in four colourways and no reorder data for the rest.

2. What should a private label ribbon development brief contain?

Twelve fields, written so that a mill can quote without asking a follow-up question. Construction and material: the fibre and substrate, the weave or knit type, and the finish. Dimensions: nominal width with a tolerance, and the length unit and spool or reel geometry including core size. Colour: the Pantone reference, the tolerance band for shade matching, and whether a physical lab dip or a production drawdown is required for approval. Decoration: the method, whether artwork is supplied or drawn by the mill, the number of colours, and the repeat length for patterned ribbon. Performance: colourfastness targets for washing, light and crocking, shrinkage, and abrasion where the trim will be handled. Physical constraints: minimum tensile and tear strength, and stiffness or drape if the ribbon has to sit in a particular way. Packaging: the consumer pack format, the master case pack, and the barcode and labelling responsibility. Commercial: annual volume per SKU, the acceptable price band, the target delivery date, and the Incoterm with the named place. Documentation: which certifications are required and by when. Traceability: what evidence you want retained. If a field is genuinely undecided, write the decision criteria rather than leaving it blank, because a blank field becomes an assumption inside the quote.

3. How long does it take to launch a private label ribbon programme from first contact to first shipment?

A realistic planning assumption for a new brand with a new construction is fourteen to twenty-two weeks from first contact to shipment, and the range widens sharply depending on what has to be developed. If the programme uses an existing construction in an existing colourway, only the packaging and artwork need developing, and six to ten weeks is realistic. If the construction is new and the colour is new, add a dye development and a lab dip cycle, which is typically three to five weeks before a sample can be made. Printed or foil-decorated ribbon adds a pre-press cycle and a physical strike-off, which usually adds two to four weeks. A certification that the brand needs but the mill does not currently hold for that material can add considerably more, and this is the item most likely to be underestimated because it sits outside the mill's own production schedule. The practical way to compress the programme is not to hurry the samples but to run them in parallel: colour development, artwork preparation and packaging design should all be in progress at the same time rather than in sequence. Sequential working is where most of the lost weeks come from.

4. What is the difference between a lab dip, a strike-off and a production sample in ribbon?

They prove different things, and approving the wrong one is a common and expensive mistake. A lab dip is a small dyed yarn or fabric swatch produced to a target colour. It proves whether the mill can reach the colour in the dyehouse, which is the question of whether the target is chemically achievable at all. It proves nothing about the finished ribbon, because weaving, finishing and any printing or foiling have not happened. A strike-off is a sample of the decorated ribbon, produced on production machinery or an equivalent, showing the construction, the print or foil and the finish together. It proves how the finished product will look but is often produced outside the exact production conditions, so it is a visual and tactile check rather than a dimensional or performance guarantee. A production sample, sometimes called a pre-production sample, is cut from the actual production lot. It is the only one of the three that can be used to verify width, length, selvage quality, print registration and physical performance against the specification. The rule that protects a buyer is simple: approve colour on a lab dip, approve appearance on a strike-off, and approve specification on a production sample. Approving a lab dip as though it were the finished product is how a brand discovers a colour difference after the bulk has been produced.

5. Who owns the artwork and decoration tooling when a private label ribbon programme ends?

Ownership should be settled in writing before the first sample is made, because the point at which it is raised is usually after the mill has invested in it. Print screens, printing cylinders, foil plates, embroidery frames, jacquard cards and the digital files that drive them are tooling in the ordinary sense, and the standard commercial position is that the buyer owns the tooling once it has been paid for, or that the tooling is created at the buyer's cost and the mill holds it in the buyer's name. Physical plates and screens should be returned or destroyed on request, with a written record. The design files are a separate and often more important question. Many mills retain a general right to use customer artwork for sample books, promotional material and internal development unless the agreement says otherwise. If a brand does not want its unreleased seasonal artwork appearing in a mill's catalogue or trade show display, that prohibition has to be explicit and it has to be written into the agreement before the artwork is sent. The practical recommendation is a short artwork and tooling clause covering ownership, licence back for production continuity, return or destruction on exit, and a confidentiality carve-out for anything not yet released.

6. What should a brand check before issuing the first ribbon purchase order?

A short readiness check prevents most first-order problems. Confirm that the specification sheet is signed and includes the colour tolerance and the performance test requirements, and that it matches what the sample was approved against. Confirm that the price on the purchase order matches the quotation that was accepted, including whether the decoration and tooling costs were amortised into the unit price or charged separately. Confirm the Incoterm and the named place, because a change here changes the landed cost and the risk transfer. Confirm the packaging specification, the case pack and the barcode responsibility. Confirm that the required certifications are in place for this material and this construction, not merely for the mill generally. Confirm the delivery window against the date the goods are actually needed, allowing for sea freight and customs clearance rather than just the production date. And confirm that the purchase order states who absorbs the cost if the goods fail inspection on arrival, since the default assumption of the seller rarely matches what a buyer expects. If the order is a repeat, check instead that nothing has changed on the mill side, since a construction, a dye recipe or a decoration method can change without the buyer being told.

7. How do you avoid ending up with excess private label ribbon inventory after launch?

Excess inventory in private label ribbon is almost always a range-planning failure rather than a demand-forecasting failure, because trim colours have long tails and short replenishment lead times relative to the fashion cycle that creates demand for them. The practical controls are four. Plan the initial range on a construction and colour logic that supports reorder, so that a colourway that sells well can be repeated without new development cost. Set the minimum order quantity low enough on the first order that a reorder is genuinely possible, because a range that cannot be reordered cannot be corrected. Order the launch range asymmetrically, committing deeper on one or two proven colours and shallower on the rest, so the exposed inventory is a fraction of the range value. And agree a reorder mechanism with the mill in advance, including whether the mill can hold greige or undecorated stock against a forecast, which converts some of the risk from finished goods into a shorter production cycle. The metric worth tracking is not sell-through in isolation but the share of the range that has been reordered, because that tells you which part of the assortment is actually working and which part is dead.
