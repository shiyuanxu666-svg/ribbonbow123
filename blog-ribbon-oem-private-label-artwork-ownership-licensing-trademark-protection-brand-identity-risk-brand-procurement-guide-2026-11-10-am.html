Most ribbon OEM conversations are about colour, width, material, price and lead time. Artwork ownership, trademark protection and licensing rarely appear until something has already gone wrong: a factory's sample room turns up with your motif on a ribbon for a different brand; a licence you assumed was perpetual turns out to have a fixed term; a trademark comes back from production subtly wrong in scale or shade; a former employee takes your print file somewhere they should not.

None of those outcomes is a factory failure. Each one is an agreement failure — a clause that was never written, or was written ambiguously, or was written and then never verified. The remedy is entirely within the buyer's control, and it is inexpensive when done early and expensive when done late.

This guide is about intellectual property in the ribbon supply relationship: how artwork ownership, trademark protection and licensing are three different problems that get confused; who actually owns the print file; how trademark risk enters an otherwise routine order; how to plan around a licence expiry; what to put into the supplier agreement; and the low-cost operational controls that reduce exposure without requiring a legal department.

The Question Nobody Asks Until There Is a Problem

Here is the pattern. A brand buyer requests a printed satin ribbon with their logo. The factory quotes, samples come back, the sample looks right, an order is placed, and the goods arrive four weeks later. The programme runs. Nobody asks about ownership because there is no reason to, and the file is never discussed again.

Eighteen months later, the brand's legal team asks whether the supplier still holds the digital print file for the decorated ribbon. It does. Then it asks whether the supplier has sold that design to anyone else. Nobody can say. Then the brand discovers that the licence covering the motif the ribbon uses expired eleven months ago, and the factory ran production for a further four months before the buyer noticed. Then a buyer on the other side of the world receives a quotation for a ribbon carrying something that looks uncomfortably like the brand's mark, at a different width, with a different repeat length.

Each of these is a routine consequence of not having addressed ownership at the start. None required an unusual factory or unusual conduct. The exposure was created at specification stage and simply stayed unexamined.

The counter-argument is usually that this is a commercial matter between lawyers, not a sourcing matter. That is only half true. The lawyers can draft a clause; the sourcing team is the only party that knows whether the factory actually received the file, whether the physical screens are being retained, whether the artwork is visible in the factory's sample room, and whether the proof that was signed off is the proof that was actually run. The clauses only work if somebody in sourcing asks whether the underlying facts match.

Three Different Things That Get Confused

Most IP problems in this category are not really IP problems. They are three separate questions that get bundled under one heading and answered with one vague answer.

1. The artwork itself

This is the design you created or were licensed to use: your logo, your pattern, your packaging illustration, your seasonal motif. The question is whether you have the right to print it at all, and whether anyone else may. This is a question about your own rights and about your supplier's conduct.

2. The production assets

This is what the factory creates to reproduce the artwork: the colour separations, the screens or plates, the physical samples, the digital print file, any master swatch or reference standard retained for future runs. These are frequently the factory's property under default terms, and they are frequently capable of producing your design without you. They are also sometimes the most practically important item in the relationship — because if you need a colour re-run in eighteen months, the question of whether the factory still holds the separations is an operational question before it is a legal one.

3. The licence

This is your right to use the design, and it has a scope and a term. If the artwork is a licensed character, a third-party graphic, a font, a retailer's private brand mark, or anything you are using under a reseller agreement, your permission to print it may be limited by territory, by product category, by channel, by time, or by some combination of all four. The licence is not something the factory can manage for you, and no clause in a supply agreement can extend it.

When these three are answered together, the answer is usually vague. When they are answered separately, each one has a clear, practical, writable answer.

Who Actually Owns the Print File

Here is the honest default position, which most buyers learn only after the fact.

In the absence of an express agreement, the supplier will typically retain ownership of the production assets it generates — separations, screens, plates, digital files — and will typically retain the right to reuse the construction and, in some cases, the design itself for other customers. Some factories will treat a customer's artwork as confidential informally and say so cheerfully in an email. Very few will have thought about the question at all.

There is nothing wrong with a factory wanting to own its own tooling. Tools are often expensive, sometimes specific to one customer, and amortised over repeat runs. A brand that expects a screen to be handed over on first order is asking for something unusual. But there is a clean, workable middle position that nearly every factory will accept if it is asked for plainly and fairly:

  • You own the artwork. You supplied it, or you have the right to supply it. The factory owns nothing in it.
  • The factory owns its production assets — the screens, plates, dies and separations it used to make your order.
  • Those assets are dedicated to you. They are not offered to other customers, not used to reproduce your design for anyone else, and not included in the factory's own catalogue.
  • The factory grants you a continuing licence to have further production made with the same construction, colours and decoration, for as long as the factory operates and for the term you require.
  • On termination, the assets are either destroyed under your instruction, returned to you, or retained solely for the purpose of producing your reorders, with the design itself not being offered to anyone else.

This arrangement gives the factory what it needs — protection for its tooling and a re-order relationship — and gives you what you need — confidence that your design is not going to appear on somebody else's ribbon. It is also far easier to agree than a demand for outright assignment, and it is far easier to verify.

Two practical notes. First, the licence-back should be written so it survives the supply relationship: if your programme runs for five years and you need to re-order in year six, you do not want to be renegotiating from scratch with a factory that has already stopped making that construction. Second, if you are bringing your own artwork rather than having the factory develop the print, you keep the master file and you do not need to transfer anything at all — but you should still confirm that the factory is not retaining copies beyond what is operationally necessary.

How Trademark Risk Enters a Ribbon Order

A trademark on a ribbon is not a logo pasted onto a label. It is a mark reproduced on a curved, textured, dyed, decorated surface, repeatedly, often at a scale far smaller than it was designed for, and often in a medium that nobody involved has thought about in trademark terms.

Several distinct problems arise.

Scale

A mark designed for a 90 mm box front becomes unreadable at 8 mm on a ribbon, and at some point below that it stops functioning as a mark at all — it becomes a smudge. Below a certain point the reproduction does not weaken the mark so much as replace it with something that looks like a defect.

Colour

A trademark's distinctiveness is partly a function of its colour. Reproduced in a dye lot that shifts under the factory's colour management — a magenta that reads warm in daylight, a yellow that goes greenish in warehouse light — the mark is no longer the same colour as the one you registered. If your registered mark is specified in a particular colourway, reproducing it in a visibly different one weakens the protection.

Distortion

Stretching, mirroring, cropping, re-proportioning or rotating a mark changes it. A mark that is mirrored is a different mark. A mark that is re-proportioned is a different mark. If your specification does not prohibit modification, a factory optimising a print repeat will sometimes do exactly that.

Repetition and dilution

A mark repeated every 30 mm along 20,000 metres is a different commercial proposition from a mark used once on a box. Repetition is normally fine and desirable for brand visibility, but repetition also creates exposure: every metre is an instance of the mark in the marketplace, and every instance must be correct.

The practical response is to write trademark reproduction into the purchase specification rather than assuming it. That means: the exact colour references, the permitted reproduction tolerance in each colour channel, the minimum repeat-mark size, the number and position of repeats, the clear space around the mark, an express prohibition on mirroring, cropping, rotation and re-proportioning, and a pre-production proof that must be signed before the run starts.

The verification step matters as much as the clause. A signed proof is only useful if the factory's in-line inspection actually compares the running ribbon against that proof, and if somebody on your side reviews a photograph of the running lot rather than only the final inspection report. Trademark defects are visually obvious once you look for them, which is why they survive so long when nobody is looking.

Licensed and Character Work: The Expiry Problem

This deserves its own treatment because it is the most common source of catastrophic loss in decorated trim, and because it is entirely predictable in advance.

Consider the sequence. You hold a licence to use a licensed character on your product, granted by the licensor, valid in the European Economic Area, for the gift and stationery category, valid for three years from January 2026. You order a decorated ribbon featuring that character. The factory produces 8,000 metres over two months. Your customer buys the finished goods slowly. Eighteen months later, you still have 3,000 metres of component trim in your warehouse and a shipment in transit, and the licence expires in four months. You plan a reorder because demand is good — and the licensor has already licensed the same character to a different, larger, better-distributed partner, who has filed an opposition to your own pending trademark application because you have been using the mark in a way that suggests you are the licensee.

Nothing the factory did was wrong. The factory produced the motif accurately, to the artwork, on time. The loss falls entirely on the party that owned the licence and failed to schedule against its own rights.

The planning discipline that prevents this is straightforward:

  1. Record the licence parameters. Start date, expiry date, territories, product categories, channels, and any volume or sub-licensing conditions. Put the expiry date in a programme calendar with an alert set at least twelve months ahead.
  2. Work out your sell-through window. From the date the last unit reaches your customer to the date the last unit is consumed or sold, at your actual sales rate for the specific SKU.
  3. Add your pipeline time. The time to specify, sample, approve, produce, ship, receive, and put away a replacement order — realistically six to ten weeks for a decorated programme, longer if a new plate or screen is needed.
  4. Add your consumption window. The period over which existing component stock and work in transit will be used.
  5. Book the final reorder early enough that the delivery lands with margin before the licence ends. If the arithmetic does not work, reduce the final order rather than shipping unlicensed trim.

Then take the same discipline to every licensed asset in the programme, including fonts, photographs, patterns and character elements, and include them in the same calendar. It is entirely normal for a programme to carry three or four separate licences with three or four different expiry dates.

Confidentiality Before the Files Leave Your Office

A written confidentiality undertaking is the cheapest control available and the one most often skipped. It should be a short document, and it should be requested as a matter of routine rather than as an escalation, because a factory that treats it as an escalation is telling you something useful about how it will treat the artwork afterwards.

A workable undertaking covers four areas.

Scope. Your artwork and the files you supply; the files, separations and physical assets the factory generates from them; your product specifications; your prices, volumes and forecasts; and your finished product designs.

Duration and survival. The obligation should run for the duration of the relationship and continue for a stated period afterwards — commonly three to five years for the commercial terms, and indefinitely for the artwork and any trade secret content. State explicitly that it survives the end of the commercial relationship, which is where most informally agreed confidences quietly evaporate.

Access. Restrict access to named personnel who need it to perform the work. Ask how files are stored, whether they are shared with subcontractors, and whether they are stored on shared drives or individual laptops. These are not accusatory questions; a factory that runs a print room and a sample room will naturally share files internally, and knowing that is useful.

Termination and non-use. On the end of the relationship, return or destroy files on request and confirm in writing. Add a non-circumvention undertaking: the factory will not offer your design, or any substantially similar design, to another customer.

Two things to avoid. First, do not accept a mutual undertaking that makes your own disclosures confidential in a way that limits your own supplier discussions — it should be one-directional. Second, do not rely on a clause that says the factory "will not disclose" without saying what happens to the files: a factory that destroys a customer's digital file cannot re-run the colour, and you may want that ability for three years.

Practical Controls That Cost Almost Nothing

Most of the protection available to you does not require a contract amendment or a legal fee. It requires a handful of habits.

Keep a single controlled master

Maintain one authoritative artwork file, with a version number and a date, and distribute only flattened production-ready derivatives. Each time the artwork changes — a new season, a colour adjustment, a mark revision — issue a new version number and confirm in writing which version is on the machine. Most artwork disputes in this industry are simply two different files being called "the logo".

Run a proof with a deliberate eye

When the pre-production proof arrives, check the things a normal eye misses: whether the mark has been mirrored, whether the proportions are identical to your master, whether the colour matches the reference rather than merely looking close, whether the repeat length is what was specified, and whether the mark is too small to read at its intended use size. Print the proof at 1:1 and view it at the distance it will be seen in use. A mark that reads correctly at arm's length on a desk can be illegible on a 15 mm ribbon held in a hand.

Ask for a running-lot photograph

One clear photograph of the ribbon actually running on the machine, with a ruler or a colour card in frame, catches an astonishing proportion of problems. It confirms the artwork is the right way round, that the scale is right, that the colours are right, and that the repeat is right. It is a five-minute request and it prevents a container of wrong trim.

Audit the sample room periodically

Visit the factory, or ask a buying agent you trust to visit, and look at the sample room. It is public-facing in a way that production is not — samples get shown at trade fairs, sent to agents, and photographed for catalogues. If your design is there, find out whether it is labelled with your name. If it is not labelled, ask directly. A factory that answers clearly either way is telling you what you need to know.

Time-box the design

If a motif is seasonal, decide in advance whether it is a permanent part of your range or a limited run, and record the decision. A design that has no retirement plan tends to remain in the factory's decoration library long after it should have gone, and the longer it stays, the harder it is to remove.

Working With a Factory on Brand Protection

A capable OEM ribbon factory will not be unsettled by these questions. In fact, a buyer who asks about artwork ownership and trademark handling is a signal worth having, because it indicates a programme that will run for years and generate repeat business rather than a single comparison order. A factory that reacts defensively, or that cannot answer who holds the separations, is telling you something about how it will handle your programme when you are not in the room.

There are also things worth asking the factory to do, and things worth doing yourself.

Things the factory can reasonably do: keep your separations physically segregated and labelled; produce and retain a master colour reference against which in-line inspection can be checked; provide the artwork proof as a controlled, dated document; restrict sample-room display of your design to named recipients; and confirm in writing that your design is not in its standard decoration catalogue.

Things you need to own: the artwork master and its version history; the licence terms and expiry dates; the approval record of every colour and artwork sign-off; and the programme calendar that drives the final reorder against the licence end date.

One practical point about the factory's own drawings. If your brand artwork includes a brand name, a monogram or a stylised element that the factory will need to translate into a print-ready vector or a separations-ready file, expect them to make a technical drawing. That drawing is a new asset created from your artwork, and it should be covered by exactly the same ownership and confidentiality terms as the artwork itself. Ask to see the drawing before it is used and confirm it is accurate to your master, because an error introduced in that translation step is very difficult to detect downstream.

Finally, treat the factory relationship as the long-term asset it is. Most brand programmes run for years, and the artwork arrangements you put in place for the first order tend to set the pattern for everything after it. A first order handled with a written IP position, a version-controlled master and a licence calendar is a programme that will run smoothly. A first order handled with a friendly email is a programme that will need renegotiating every time something changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, legal counsel and procurement managers most often ask us about ribbon artwork and intellectual property: who owns the print file, how a trademark is protected once it is printed, what happens when a licensed design expires, and what to write into a supplier agreement. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. Who owns the artwork once a ribbon factory has printed it?

It depends entirely on what your agreement says, and the default position is usually not the one buyers expect. Absent an express clause, many factories will assert that they own the tooling, the separated colour files and the digital print file they produced from your artwork, and will retain a licence to use the design for other customers. Some will keep the physical screens, plates or dies. The safest approach is to state ownership explicitly in the purchase agreement: that you own the artwork you supply, that the factory owns nothing in it, that any file, separation, screen, plate or digital asset created in the course of producing your order is either assigned to you on payment or destroyed at the end of the programme, and that the factory will not reproduce the design for any other customer. Where the factory owns the underlying construction or base design, that ownership can and should stay with the factory, provided the customer-facing artwork remains yours.

2. How do I protect my trademark on a printed ribbon?

Protection has two halves, and most buyers only think about one of them. The first is legal: register your mark in the markets where you sell, and confirm before you print that the supplier understands it is a trademark and not merely a decorative graphic. The second, and more operational half, is procedural: define in the purchase specification how the mark is reproduced — the exact Pantone or colour values, the permitted reproduction tolerance, the minimum size at which the mark may appear, the placement, the number of repeats, the clear space, and the prohibition on modifying, mirroring, cropping or re-proportioning the artwork. Then hold the factory to that specification with a pre-production proof and an in-line inspection. A trademark that is reproduced at the wrong scale or the wrong colour on 20,000 metres is a trademark that has been diluted, and the damage to its distinctiveness is permanent and very difficult to unwind.

3. What happens when a licensed character or brand design reaches the end of its licence term?

This is the most common and most expensive trap in the decorated trim category, and it is entirely a planning failure rather than a factory failure. Licences expire, get terminated, or move to a new licensee, and the factory's production slot does not stop on that date. A buyer who ordered a licensed motif in good faith and then discovers the licence ended while 40,000 metres of the trim was in transit has no contractual recourse against the factory, who produced exactly what was ordered, and faces the cost of destroying or reworking the finished goods. The remedy is scheduling discipline: record the licence expiry date, the sell-through window, and the time required to consume the existing stock plus the time required to place and deliver a replacement order without a licence. Build that timeline backwards from the expiry date and book the reorder early enough to clear the pipe before the licence ends.

4. Should I require a confidentiality or NDA agreement before sending artwork?

Yes, for anything beyond your most generic, publicly visible decoration, and you should require it as a signed document rather than a line in an email. A workable confidentiality undertaking covers three things: the artwork and files you supply, the files and production assets the factory generates from them, and the commercial terms of the programme including prices and volumes. It should specify how long the obligation lasts, that the obligation survives the end of the commercial relationship, that the factory will restrict access to named personnel who need it to do the work, and what happens on termination — return or certified destruction. One practical addition worth asking for: a clause confirming that the factory has not and will not offer your design, or any substantially similar design, to another customer. The enforcement value of such a clause is modest, but its deterrent effect on a stray sample reaching the wrong trade show is real.

5. How do I stop my artwork appearing on another customer's ribbon?

Three mechanisms work, in ascending order of strength. First, control: put the artwork behind an NDA before it leaves your organisation, require that the factory's samples are not shown publicly, and ask for a warranty that it has not offered the design to others. Second, detect: inspect factory showrooms and trade fair samples for your design, request a written statement on each visit, and periodically audit. Third, contract: write into the agreement a non-circumvention and exclusivity clause with a meaningful remedy, and — if the design is commercially significant enough to justify the cost — a right of first refusal on the base construction if the factory wants to sell it to a competitor. None of these is bulletproof. The combination of an NDA, a written warranty and a contractual remedy is what makes the risk acceptable rather than what makes it zero.

6. What should the intellectual property clause in my ribbon supplier agreement actually say?

Cover six things. First, ownership of the brand artwork you supply and a warranty that you have the right to license it for this use, including where the design is a licensed character or a third-party graphic. Second, ownership of the production assets the factory creates for you — separations, screens, plates, dies, digital files and the physical samples — and the date on which that ownership transfers, usually on payment. Third, an undertaking that the factory will not reproduce your artwork for other customers and will not offer it as its own stock design. Fourth, confidentiality and its survival on termination of the relationship. Fifth, a trademark handling clause covering reproduction tolerances, minimum size, placement, and the prohibition on modification, with a pre-production proof and in-line inspection as the verification mechanism. Sixth, what happens to the assets when the relationship ends: return, certified destruction, or a continuing licence for finished stock only. Write the clauses in plain language and give each one a verification step, because a clause with no evidence attached to it is difficult to enforce and impossible to audit.

7. Does artwork ownership affect cost or lead time?

The direct effect is small, but the indirect effects are larger than buyers expect. A clearly drafted IP position usually shortens the approval loop, because the factory is not waiting to understand what it may and may not do with your files. It reduces the risk of a production hold after the fact, which is where schedule damage usually comes from. It can also affect tooling cost: where you take ownership of the screens or plates, the factory may charge more for the setup, or may decline to amortise the tooling over repeat runs. In practice the cost differences are minor, and the important economic effect is elsewhere — it is the cost of the finished goods you cannot sell because a licence expired, a mark was reproduced incorrectly, or a competitor is trading on your design. That cost dwarfs every administration charge in the supply agreement.
