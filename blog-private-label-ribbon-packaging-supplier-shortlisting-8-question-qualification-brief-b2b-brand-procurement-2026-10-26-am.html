Why Asking for a Price First Is the Most Expensive Mistake

The sequence almost always begins the same way. A brand owner has a product in mind, finds three or four ribbon suppliers, and sends the same brief to each with a request for pricing and minimum order quantity. Within a week the buyer has four quotations, picks the lowest, and places the order. The failure here is not that the buyer chose badly; it is that the buyer chose at all, because at that point the four quotations are not comparable and the lowest number is measuring almost nothing. A price is an output of a specification. If the specification is one line saying "printed grosgrain, 16mm, 2 colours", two mills are not quoting the same thing. One may be quoting a woven construction with a printed surface treatment and a polyester yarn, the other a true printed satin with a different face and a different hand. One may be quoting a single-colour print with a standard edge, the other a double-side print with a wired edge and a heat-sealed cut. One may be pricing at a volume break that applies at thirty thousand metres, the other at five. The quotations look like they are answering the same question, and they are not. The cost of finding this out is paid in the wrong sample, the wrong price, the wrong lead time and the wrong decision, and it is paid at a stage where the costs of changing course are still low if you catch it early and expensive if you catch it late. The practical sequence that works is the reverse of the intuitive one: qualify the mill on what it can do, agree the specification with them, and only then ask for a price against that specification. The quotations that arrive at the end of that sequence are comparable by construction, because they are all quotations of the same article. This is a 2026 guide to running that sequence for private-label ribbon and decorative trim programmes, written for brand owners, procurement leads and category managers who are qualifying a supply base rather than buying a commodity.

The Eight Qualification Questions, and Why Each One Matters

Eight questions, asked of every shortlisted supplier in the same order and with the same wording, will tell you more about whether a mill can run your programme than a day of factory inspection. They fall into four groups: what the mill is and what it makes, whether it can hold capacity and how deep its process is, the commercial terms that will determine whether your programme is viable, and the behaviour under stress, which is the hardest to observe and the most predictive. The reason to ask the same questions of everyone, in the same order, is that it makes the answers comparable. Suppliers talk about their strengths. A supplier whose genuine strength is not in a given area will often volunteer it if asked in a structured sequence and a neutral register, because the question has a shape that permits an honest answer. The reason to ask them before requesting a price is that the answers frequently change what you should be pricing. If the mill tells you it does not print woven ribbon and subcontracts it, the price you are about to request should be a price for a different supply model, and the risk allocation should be different too. If the mill tells you its minimum for printed construction is driven by the screen charge rather than by the metreage, your order-splitting strategy should be designed around colour count. If the mill cannot state its component lead times separately from its total, its total lead time is not a commitment. Eight questions, asked in sequence, replace an assumption with information, and the information changes the commercial structure of the programme before any money is committed.

Question One: Are You the Mill, and What Do You Actually Make?

The first question is not rude and it is not an accusation. It is the question that determines the shape of everything that follows, and it is best asked in a way that makes an honest answer easy: ask what the plant makes, on what equipment, at what widths, and whether the specific process your programme needs is in-house or subcontracted. You will get one of three answers. The first is that they are the mill, they make the construction, and they can describe the equipment accurately and specifically. The second is that they are the mill for part of the chain and subcontract another step, which is extremely common and entirely workable, provided you know which step and what it means for your control over timing and quality. The third is that they are a trading company or a sourcing office, in which case the programme will be executed by a mill they select and contract with, and your working relationship is with an intermediary rather than with a production facility. The third answer is not disqualifying. Plenty of buyers work successfully with intermediaries, and some of them have no other option. What matters is that you make the decision consciously, with the specific consequences in view, rather than discovering the arrangement when a quality dispute arises and there is no escalation path. The consequences of a trading-company supply model are concrete. The intermediary typically adds a margin between your price and the mill's price, which means less of your negotiation reaches the production cost. The lead time often carries an extra buffer for coordination, and that buffer shows up on your critical path. When something goes wrong, the intermediary may or may not have contractual leverage over its own mill. And the traceability you expect, the lot-level genealogy and the direct technical discussion you will eventually need, are one step further away. Ask the question early, ask it plainly, and write down the answer in your qualification record. Then the rest of the eight questions can be interpreted correctly, because you now know who is answering them.

Questions Two and Three: Capacity and the Depth of Your Process

The second and third questions together establish whether the mill can actually deliver your volume when you need it, and whether it can make the specific construction you have specified. On capacity, the useful question is not "can you make this quantity" but "what does your production schedule look like in the months my programme runs, and how much of that schedule is already committed". A mill that is running at eighty-five per cent in your delivery window cannot absorb your volume in that window regardless of its total annual capacity, and the most useful single disclosure is a stated current utilisation figure for the relevant period. The second useful disclosure is how capacity is reserved and whether a reservation carries a cost, because a supplier who reserves loom time for a year and then loses the order has behaved reasonably while costing you. On process depth, the question is whether the specific construction you need is made in-house. If your programme requires printed grosgrain with a heat-sealed edge and a wired edge, the mill needs weaving, printing, edge sealing and wiring. If it subcontracts the sealing to a trim house, the seal is now outside your quality system and outside the mill's direct control, and the mill's own incoming inspection will not necessarily catch a seal problem before you do. Process depth also determines how much of your development timeline the mill controls. The deeper the process, the more of the calendar time sits inside the mill's control rather than in transit between process owners, which is one of the strongest arguments for a deeper mill even when its price is not the lowest. Ask specifically which steps are in-house and which are not, and ask what incoming inspection the mill applies to the subcontracted steps. The answer to that second question tells you whether the subcontracted output is verified or merely accepted.

Question Four: Minimum Order Quantity and What Actually Drives It

The minimum order quantity is one of the most-quoted and least-understood numbers in the category, and the reason it is misunderstood is that it is a policy figure rather than a cost figure. A supplier quoting "one thousand metres" as an MOQ across a catalogue of forty constructions is quoting a commercial threshold, and a threshold tells you nothing about whether your order will be efficient. What you need is the MOQ for each construction separately, and the driver behind each one. The driver for a woven ribbon is usually width and dye batch, because a narrow width ties up loom capacity inefficiently and a small dye batch ties up a dye vessel that has a fixed minimum fill. The driver for a printed ribbon is usually the cylinder or screen charge, because the setup cost is recovered over the run length and the supplier will not run a print for a short run against the setup cost. The driver for a constructed item such as a bow or a ribbon flower is usually the component count, because every component adds an assembly operation and each one carries its own minimum. Once you have the driver, you can negotiate against the economics rather than against the policy, and there are three specific moves available. The first is to concentrate rather than spread: raising the order quantity on fewer colours is nearly always cheaper than holding the same total quantity across more colours, because the dye batch and the print setup are per colour. The second is to ask whether the MOQ applies per colour, per width or per order, because the difference between these can be a factor of several on a multi-colour programme and it determines how you design your range. The third is to ask whether a split delivery is permitted against a single order, which lets you launch with one colourway and add the rest without re-triggering the minimum. Smith Ribbon works to a 1,000-metre standard minimum order quantity with 500-metre availability for smaller batch programmes, and quotes the driver behind each figure so the order-splitting decision is made on cost rather than on a published threshold.

Questions Five and Six: Colour Development and Tooling

The fifth and sixth questions deal with the two costs that sit outside the per-metre price and that quietly consume the budget of a first programme: getting the colour right, and paying for the tooling that puts it there. On colour, the question is what happens when your approved colour cannot be repeated. Specifically: how is a colour recipe versioned, how is the approved reference stored, and how is a new lot checked against it before the lot ships. A supplier who describes dye lots, a stored recipe and a measured comparison against a retained reference has a process that will produce your colour again in six months. A supplier who describes "the same dye house" and "we can always do that shade" has a memory and a hope. The related question is what the tolerance is and how it is stated, because a colour tolerance without a measurement method is not a specification at all. Ask whether the measurement is by instrument or by eye, and if by instrument, ask what the acceptance threshold is. The value of this question is that it converts colour from a negotiation into a specification, and specifications are enforceable while preferences are not. On tooling, the question is what the tooling costs, what the lead time for the tooling is, and who owns it when the programme ends. These three sub-questions belong together because they define whether the programme is portable. If the tool is owned by you, you can move the programme to another mill at the end of the relationship or when something goes wrong, and you can take the artwork and the setup with you. If the tool is owned by the mill and is amortised into the unit price, moving the programme means re-paying. If the tool is a screen or a cylinder with a physical lead time of two to three weeks, that lead time belongs in your critical path and must be scheduled before the production slot, not alongside it. Ask also whether the tooling can be modified rather than replaced when the programme evolves, because a minor artwork revision should not trigger a full tooling charge.

Question Seven: Documentation and Compliance

The seventh question asks what documentation the mill will provide for your programme and how current it is. This is the question that most often distinguishes a supplier that can support a serious brand programme from one that can only supply a commodity. Ask for the certification scope with expiry dates, and read the scope rather than the certificate. A certificate whose scope covers the company rather than the specific product and process tells you that the company has a quality system; it does not tell you that the ribbon you are buying was made under it. A scope that names the site, the process and the product category tells you considerably more. Ask which certification body issued it and whether you can verify the certificate number directly, because a certificate that has lapsed and is still presented in a sales pack is not a rare occurrence. Then ask about the documentation the mill produces per production lot: the finished-goods inspection record with measured values, the lot traceability, the material or component genealogy, and the test results where performance requirements apply. The reason this question sits in the qualification stage rather than at launch is that a mill which cannot produce these documents will not produce them later under schedule pressure, because building the documentation habit takes months and starting it late means the first three shipments are undocumented. A final part of this question concerns the specific claims your brand will make. If your packaging will carry a recycled content claim, a certification mark, an origin statement or a fibre content declaration, ask what substantiation the mill can provide, and ask for it at the qualification stage rather than after the artwork is locked, because the substantiation requirement can change the material and therefore the price.

Question Eight: How You Will Handle My Programme When Something Goes Wrong

The eighth question is the one buyers most often skip and it is the one that matters most in year two. Ask how the mill handles a customer complaint, and ask for specifics: what is the escalation path, who holds which role, what is the defined response time, and what happens to the lot while the issue is investigated. The answer you are listening for is a process, not a personality. A good answer describes a containment step, a root-cause analysis, a corrective action with a verification and a stated closure, and it identifies the roles by name rather than by function in the abstract. A weaker answer describes someone senior taking a personal interest, which can work well and does not scale. A poor answer describes a general undertaking to communicate, without a containment step and without a defined response time, which is what "we will do our best" sounds like in a formal context. The reason this question predicts year-two behaviour better than any other is that the first twenty-four hours after a defect discovery are what determine whether a problem stays a contained issue or becomes a supply failure. A mill that quarantines the affected stock, stops the run and tells you the same day protects your retail calendar. A mill that discovers the issue at your customer's warehouse has already lost the calendar. Ask this question in the tone of planning a normal working relationship rather than an interrogation, and note the specificity of the answer. You can also test it: during the trial order, report a minor discrepancy and see what happens. That test costs you one email and it tells you more than three factory visits.

The Document Set That Should Accompany a Shortlist

Before a comparison can be meaningful, the shortlisted suppliers should be working from the same set of documents, and six of them carry most of the weight. The approved or draft specification, which defines construction, width, tolerance, colour, print method, edge treatment, packaging and the acceptance criteria, and which every supplier should be quoting against so that the quotations describe the same article. The bill of materials, with the construction broken out into yarn, weave, dye, print, finish and edge so that you can see what is included in a quoted metre and what is not. The certification scope with expiry dates, confirming that the credentials cover your product and process. The minimum order quantity schedule by construction, with the drivers stated. The lead-time breakdown, separating yarn and dye, print or construction, finishing, inspection, packaging and transit, so that the long pole is visible. And the documentation schedule, listing what the mill will produce and issue per production lot. Six documents, and the practical test of whether a supplier is serious is simple: a mill that runs a mature operation will already have most of these and will send them with minimal prompting, while a mill that does not will need to create them, and that creation effort is itself information about how the programme will run. One practical note on sequence. Send the specification to all shortlisted suppliers at the same time, with the same revision number, and ask each of them to quote against it. If you send an updated specification to one supplier first, you have told the others that you are negotiating, and you will receive quotations that are less sharp on the second round than they should be.

Building an Apples-to-Apples Comparison of the Quotations You Receive

When the quotations arrive, the comparison itself is where most programmes lose money. Nine columns make the comparison meaningful, and the sequence matters more than the format. Start by normalising the commercial basis: convert every quotation to the same Incoterm at the same named place, so that you are comparing the point at which risk and cost transfer rather than mixing ex-works, FOB and delivered figures. Then confirm that the constructions are genuinely identical, because a lower price against a lighter construction is not a lower price and it will consume the margin in the market rather than in the factory. Then rebuild the number as a total landed cost by adding what the supplier did not quote: freight, duty, third-party inspection, packaging, the financing cost of the deposit and the pre-booking, and the labour and freight for any components you are buying separately and attaching yourself. This step routinely reveals that the lowest quoted price is not the lowest landed cost, particularly when one supplier quotes on an ex-works basis and another on a delivered basis into the same port. Then check the order split, and treat a quotation valid at five thousand metres and one valid at two thousand as two different quotations for the same article, since the price difference between them is frequently larger than the gap between suppliers. Finally, apply the qualitative columns, which do not belong in a spreadsheet of numbers but decide the outcome: the process depth in-house, the capacity position in your delivery window, the quality documentation actually produced, the escalation behaviour under stress, and the overall ease of doing business. In practice the numbers narrow the field to two and the qualitative assessment decides between them, and a buyer who stops at the numbers has done the easy half of the work.

How to Run the Site Visit Once the Shortlist Is Short

By the time you visit, you should have a shortlist of two or three and a written document set from each, which is what makes the visit efficient rather than a day of general impressions. Send the agenda in advance, naming four things you want to see: the weaving area, the dyeing and colour laboratory, the printing or finishing area, and the inspection and packing area. An unplanned visit shows you the parts of the plant someone has chosen to show you, and in a well-run mill the difference between the two is not large. In the colour laboratory, the interesting thing is not the equipment but the process, so ask to see the retained approved reference for a current customer programme and to see how a lot is checked against it. On the floor, watch a changeover if the timing allows, because a changeover is where process discipline either shows or does not. Ask to see the inspection records for the last three production lots of a programme similar to yours, and ask what happens when a measurement fails. Ask what the plant does when a delivery is late, and who decides. Then verify the documentation against the plant, which is the point of the visit: the documents you received in the qualification stage should describe processes you can actually see, and where they do not, that gap is the most valuable thing you will learn from the day. Keep the record of the visit against the qualification scorecard you built from the eight questions, because the two together are the basis on which you will make the award and the basis you will use to justify it internally.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many ribbon suppliers should I shortlist for a private-label programme?

Three to five is the working range. Fewer than three leaves you without a benchmark and without a fallback if the first supplier cannot hold capacity in your peak season. More than five creates an administrative load that rarely pays for itself, because the qualification questions take real effort on both sides and a supplier who feels over-solicited often becomes slow and less forthcoming in the answers. The number that matters less than the count is the depth: three suppliers answered properly are more useful than eight answered thinly. If your programme is small and single-SKU, three is enough. If you are running a multi-market rollout with peak-season deadlines, five gives you a genuine second source for at least the highest-volume articles.

What is the single most important question to ask a ribbon supplier during shortlisting?

The one that establishes whether you are talking to the mill or to an intermediary, because it determines every other answer. Ask what they make, on what equipment, at what widths, in what volume, and whether the specific process your programme needs is in-house or subcontracted. A supplier who is a trading company will often answer honestly and completely, and the answer will simply be that they do not weave or dye and source those steps from a mill they work with. That is a workable arrangement for some buyers, but it must be a conscious decision rather than a discovery made after the first quality dispute. The follow-on question is what happens when the subcontracted step fails: if the answer is that the intermediary has no contractual leverage over its own mill, the escalation path you would rely on does not exist.

Should I shortlist a ribbon mill in my own country, or always source from Asia?

Decide on the basis of landed cost, lead time and programme complexity rather than on geography as a principle. For a single article at modest volume, a domestic or near-domestic mill may deliver a lower total cost once you account for the smaller order quantities, shorter transit and lower administrative cost, even at a higher unit price. For multi-article programmes with printed or constructed components, an Asian mill typically holds a cost advantage that is large enough to absorb the freight and duty, and also typically holds the process depth and the capacity to support a peak-season schedule. The decisive variables are the number of SKUs, the print or construction complexity, the size of the first order and how hard your deadline is. A buyer who evaluates on the quoted unit price alone will systematically choose wrongly in both directions.

What should I ask about minimum order quantity during shortlisting?

Ask for the MOQ for each construction separately, and ask what drives it. The answer for a woven ribbon is usually the width and the dye batch, because a narrow width ties up the loom and a small dye batch ties up the dye house. The answer for printed ribbon is usually the print cylinder or screen charge, because the setup cost is amortised across the run length. The answer for a constructed product such as a bow or a flower is usually the component count and the assembly labour. A supplier quoting a single MOQ figure across a catalogue is quoting a policy rather than a cost structure, and you cannot negotiate intelligently against a policy. Ask also whether the MOQ applies per colour, per width or per order, because the difference between these can be a factor of several on a multi-colour programme and it determines how you design your colour range.

How do I compare quotations from ribbon suppliers that look nothing like each other?

Normalise them onto a single basis before comparing any of them. Convert every quotation to the same Incoterm at the same named place, so you are comparing the point at which risk and cost transfer rather than a mixture of ex-works and delivered prices. Confirm that each quotation includes the same construction and the same specification, because a lower price against a lighter construction is not a lower price. Then rebuild the number as a total landed cost by adding the elements the supplier did not quote: freight, duty, inspection, packaging, financing on the deposit and the pre-booking, and the labour and freight for the components you are buying separately. Finally, check the order split, because a quotation valid at five thousand metres and one valid at two thousand metres are two different quotations for the same article.

What documentation should a shortlisted ribbon mill send with its quotation?

Six items. The approved or draft specification, so you can see whether the mill is quoting against your requirement or against its own standard article. The bill of materials with the construction broken out, so you can see what is actually included in a quoted metre and what is not. The certification scope with expiry dates, so you can confirm the certifications cover the products and processes in your programme rather than the company generally. A statement of minimum order quantity by construction and the drivers of that minimum. A stated lead time broken into its components, so you can see where the calendar time goes. And a reference or a case study for a comparable programme, so you can judge whether the mill has run something of similar complexity. A factory that sends all six in the first exchange is telling you it has a system. One that sends only a price sheet is telling you the system is you.

How do I tell the difference between a well-run mill and one that is simply larger?

Look at whether the mill is running to a written system rather than to the founder. The observable differences are specific. Ask to see the approved article retention and the first-article process: a plant with a documented first-article check at every changeover has quality controls that operate without the founder in the building. Ask how a colour is controlled across lots: an answer involving a dye recipe version, a measured reference and a colour tolerance says the mill has a system; an answer involving the dye supervisor's judgement says it does not. Ask how the mill handles a customer complaint, and listen for whether there is a defined escalation path with named roles. Scale without process is the most common failure mode among fast-growing mills, and it is the failure mode that appears after the programme has launched rather than during the trial.

Should I visit the factory before placing a first order?

Yes, for any programme that will run more than one production run, and you can do it well in a single day if you plan it in advance. Send the visit request with a written agenda naming the four things you want to see, because an unplanned factory visit shows you the parts of the plant someone has chosen to show you. Ask to see the weaving area, the dyeing and colour laboratory, the printing or finishing area and the inspection and packing area. Ask to see a roll of a current customer programme, and ask whether you can see the retained approved article for it. Watch a changeover if the timing allows, because a changeover is where process discipline is either visible or absent. Treat the visit as a verification of the documentation set rather than as a separate exercise, since the documents you asked for in the shortlisting stage tell you what to look for.
