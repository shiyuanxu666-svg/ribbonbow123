The core principle: a supplier declaration of REACH compliance tells you what the supplier believes. A chemical test report tells you what was measured, in what lot, by which method, to which limit. For a brand that will be asked by a retailer, a customs authority or a consumer why a trim on a finished product is safe, only the second category is evidence. Know which one you are holding before you rely on it.

What REACH Actually Regulates in a Ribbon Programme

The EU REACH Regulation governs chemical substances and mixtures placed on the EU market. For a ribbon and trim buyer it operates through three separate mechanisms, and most confusion in the industry comes from treating them as one thing called "REACH compliance".

1. Restriction (Annex XVII)

Annex XVII lists substances that are restricted in manufacture, placing on the market, or use, under defined conditions. For textile trims the ones that bite in practice are the azodyes (restricted under entries 43 and 44, with limits on certain listed aromatic amines), certain phthalates in articles intended for prolonged contact with skin, PAHs in articles intended for direct contact with the skin for extended periods, PFAS and PFOS-related restrictions under entry 68, and nickel in certain metal components. Restriction entries specify the substance, the threshold, and the application — a substance can be unrestricted in one application and tightly limited in another. "The same" is never a safe assumption about scope.

2. Candidate List (SVHC)

The Candidate List is not a ban. It is a list of substances of high concern, and listing triggers a specific obligation: if an article contains more than 0.1% weight of a listed substance, the producer or importer must provide the safety information described in Article 33 to downstream actors, including the brand that will use the trim in a finished product. The Candidate List grows continuously, and a substance that was not listed when your supplier last ran a test may be listed now. A test report has a date, and the Candidate List has a moving threshold.

3. Registration (Annex VI onward)

For substances manufactured or imported in quantities of one tonne per year or more, the manufacturer must register the substance with the European Chemicals Agency and provide safety data. This is upstream of your ribbon and generally outside your contractual control, but it is the mechanism by which restricted substances enter the market in the first place, and it is worth knowing that a well-run supply chain is one where registration is already handled upstream.

What Chemical Testing Proves — and What It Does Not

This is the distinction that matters most commercially, because ribbon suppliers offer "REACH testing" as a line item and buyers frequently do not know which of several different things they are buying.

Evidence typeWhat it establishesWhat it does not establish
Supplier declaration letterThat the supplier states the product complies, at the date of signingThat any measurement was performed, on which lot, by which method, or to which limit
Declaration plus material disclosureThe composition the supplier believes it is usingAnything about what is actually in the goods you received
Finished-article chemical test report (lab-based)Measured concentrations of specified substances in a specific submitted sample, by a named method, against a stated limitThat a different lot, a different dye lot or a different supplier's product would give the same result
Component-level test on raw materialsThat the yarn, dye, finishing chemistry or print ink meets its restricted-substance limitsThat the conversion process, the print curing or the finishing did not introduce a substance
The rule of evidence: chemical compliance is a property of a specific lot of product tested by a specific method against a specific limit on a specific date. Every one of those four qualifiers is load-bearing. A report with no sample identity, no method reference, no limit and no date is not a test report; it is a document.

The four identifiers that make a report usable

  1. Sample identity — the product code, colour, width, construction, decoration method and batch or lot the sample was taken from. A report that cannot be traced to a specification is not transferable to your programme.
  2. Method — the analytical standard used (for example EN ISO 14399 for azo colourants, or the relevant REACH Annex XVII testing method). A result with no method is not comparable to anything.
  3. Limit — the threshold the result was assessed against, and its source: the Annex XVII entry, the brand's own internal limit, or the Candidate List 0.1% threshold.
  4. Date — because the Candidate List and the Annex XVII both change, and a report predating a relevant amendment no longer demonstrates compliance with the current regime.

Article 33 Declarations: Who Needs Them and How to Handle Them

Article 33 is the obligation most ribbon buyers meet without ever knowing it exists, because it is usually the ribbon supplier's obligation and it arrives as a clause in a purchase order rather than as a request.

The mechanism: if a ribbon contains a Candidate List substance above 0.1% by weight, the supplier, as a producer of an article, must provide you with the information so that you can pass it on to your own downstream actors. In practice this means a safety information sheet stating the substance, the CAS number, the concentration range and the reason for its presence. If no listed substance exceeds the threshold, the supplier provides a statement to that effect — which is why most declarations are negative and why buyers skim them.

Three practical consequences for a brand buyer:

  • Ask for the Article 33 declaration on every purchase order, for every SKU, not once per supplier. A supplier-level declaration covering "all products" is not Article 33 compliant, because the threshold is evaluated per article.
  • Keep the declarations, don't just check them. When a retailer asks which Candidate List substances are present in your finished product, you need a retrievable file, not a memory of which supplier said what.
  • Re-request on a cycle tied to the Candidate List, not to your purchase order cycle. A supplier's declaration reflects their knowledge at the date of issue. If the Candidate List gains an entry that affects your yarns or inks, every open declaration behind it is stale.

Building a Chemical Compliance Evidence File Per Lot

The practical structure that survives an audit is a file, not a folder of unrelated PDFs. For each active programme, the file should contain a defined set of documents with a defined owner and a defined refresh trigger.

The six documents to hold per programme

  1. Fibre and raw material composition — the complete yarn and fibre breakdown for each construction in the programme, with supplier references. This is the input to every other judgement: without it you cannot scope the testing or evaluate a declaration.
  2. Component-level chemical test reports — reports covering the yarn, the dye, the finishing chemistry and the print ink, each with sample identity, method, limit and date. These are the tests that actually generate data.
  3. Finished-article test report — a test performed on the finished ribbon as it would be shipped, covering the restricted substances relevant to that product's use. This is what closes the gap between components and product.
  4. Article 33 declaration — the current declaration for each SKU, either listing the substances present above threshold or confirming that none are. Re-dated on the Candidate List cycle.
  5. Supplier scope statement — a short document that states which steps the supplier tests and which it passes through, and who holds evidence for the rest. This document is what tells you where to point next.
  6. Restricted substance list version record — the version and date of the Candidate List and the Annex XVII against which your programme was last assessed. This is the document that makes your file defensible when the list moves.

Why the finished-article test matters

Component tests tell you what came in. They do not tell you what happened during conversion. Dyeing can involve chemistry beyond the dye itself, heat-transfer and screen-print decoration involves inks and curing, and some finishing routes introduce substances that are not present in any incoming component. This is why a programme that tests only its yarn and its dye can still fail a finished-article limit, and why a supplier's component-level evidence, however complete, is not the same as a finished-product result.

The reverse is also true: a finished-article test on one product does not transfer to a different colourway, a different print ink or a different finishing route in the same programme. The evidence unit is a specification, not a brand.

The Supplier Questions That Reveal Real Capability

These are the questions worth asking in an RFQ or a supplier review, because they ask for scope and evidence rather than for reassurance.

  1. Which substances do you test in-house, which do you subcontract, and which do you not cover at all?
  2. Which legal entity holds the chemical test reports, and can I receive them directly rather than as a summary?
  3. Does your Article 33 declaration cover each SKU individually, or does it cover a product family?
  4. What is the date of the most recent Candidate List assessment against your yarn, dye, finish and ink, and against the finished article?
  5. Which Annex XVII entries do you assess for this product, and which do you consider not applicable — and why?
  6. What sample identification appears on your test reports: product code, colour, lot and date?
  7. Which analytical methods do you use for each substance, and do you hold accreditation for them?
  8. What happens to the evidence file when I change a colour, a width, a print ink or a finishing route?
  9. Have you had a REACH-related shipment held, re-labelled or rejected at a border in the last 24 months, and what was the cause?
  10. Will you flow chemical compliance requirements down to your own sub-suppliers, and can I see how?
Question 8 is the one buyers skip and the one that matters most in practice. A ribbon programme is not one specification — it is thirty. If a supplier cannot say what happens to the evidence file when the colour changes or the ink changes, then every colourway and every print variant is quietly running on evidence that belongs to a different product. That is the most common way a compliant programme becomes a non-compliant one, and it happens without anyone deciding anything.

How REACH Intersects With Other Compliance Work You Already Do

REACH is one of several regimes that a ribbon brand answers for, and much of the diligence is shared. An OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate covers certain restricted substances in a tested article, but it is a specific scope of substances tested by a specific method — it is not a REACH SVHC screen and should not be presented as one. BSCI, SEDEX and SMETA audits cover social and ethical conduct including some chemical handling and worker protection practice, but they are not chemical content verification. An FSC or GRS certificate covers the chain of custody of fibre or recycled content, not the chemistry of the dyeing.

The practical rule is that one certificate rarely discharges two obligations. The buyer who assumes an OEKO-TEX certificate answers the REACH question will discover the gap during a retailer audit, at the point where a rushed request is most expensive. Mapping each regime to the specific documents it produces, and treating the overlaps honestly, is what prevents a compliance file from being an expensive stack of certificates that each cover a fraction of the question.

Cost, Timing and the Realistic Cadence

Chemical testing is not free, and buyers who treat it as a cost will either over-test or avoid it. A practical approach: test by risk, not by SKU. Group your programme into specification families that share fibre, dye, finish and decoration route, test one representative per family plus the extremes, and re-test when the specification changes rather than on a fixed calendar.

The two situations that justify a broader test scope are a first-time entry into a regulated market with a retailer that will audit, and any programme where the decoration chemistry is unusual — for example soft-touch finishes, glow or reflective treatments, or metallic foils with a binder system. In those cases the finished-article test carries more weight than the component tests, because the chemistry of interest was introduced at conversion.

On timing, the release date of the test report matters more than the cost. A report that arrives after the shipment has cleared customs and entered your distribution centre is still useful — it is simply too late to prevent a hold, and the cost of a hold is materially higher than the cost of the test.

1. Is polyester ribbon subject to REACH restrictions?

REACH applies to chemical substances regardless of the base fibre, so the relevance to your ribbon depends on the substances used rather than the fibre type. Polyester ribbon is not outside the scope of chemical compliance in the way it is outside the scope of deforestation rules, because dye chemistry, finishing chemistry and print inks all introduce substances that may be restricted. The common restricted-substance tests for dyed ribbon are azo colourants, heavy metals and, where relevant, phthalates and PFAS. The correct question is which substances and which applications are covered by the restrictions you must satisfy, not which fibre you use.

2. What is an Article 33 declaration and who has to provide one?

Article 33 of the EU REACH Regulation requires that if an article contains a substance on the Candidate List of substances of high concern above 0.1% by weight, the producer or importer provides safety information to downstream actors. In a ribbon supply chain that normally makes the supplier the producer of the article, and your brand the downstream actor who receives it. Where no listed substance exceeds the threshold, the supplier provides a negative declaration to that effect. In practice the declaration should be issued per SKU, not per supplier, because the threshold is evaluated article by article.

3. Is a supplier declaration of REACH compliance sufficient evidence?

No. A declaration letter states what the supplier believes at the date of signing; it establishes that no measurement was performed, on which lot, by which method or against which limit. It is useful as a supporting document and it is what many purchase orders ask for, but it cannot answer an auditor's question about a specific lot, and it cannot be carried forward to a new colourway or a changed print ink. If you need to demonstrate compliance for a particular shipment, you need a laboratory test report with sample identity, method, limit and date.

4. What four things must appear on a chemical test report for it to be usable?

Sample identity, so the report can be traced to a product code, colour, construction, decoration method and lot; the analytical method and standard used; the limit the result was assessed against and the source of that limit; and the date of the report. Each of the four is load-bearing. Without sample identity the report cannot be transferred to your programme, without the method the number is not comparable, without the limit there is nothing to judge the number against, and without the date you cannot tell whether the result was assessed against the current Candidate List and Annex XVII.

5. Does an OEKO-TEX certificate satisfy REACH chemical compliance?

No, and this is a common and costly assumption. OEKO-TEX Standard 100 tests a defined set of restricted substances against a defined scope, using defined methods. It is not a Candidate List SVHC screen, it does not automatically cover every Annex XVII entry relevant to your product's application, and it does not generate an Article 33 declaration. The certifications are complementary: OEKO-TEX evidences a specific substance list under a specific standard, while REACH compliance requires restricted-substance testing, a Candidate List screen and an Article 33 declaration for each article.

6. What chemical tests should I require for ribbon used next to skin?

The scope depends on end use and target market. For trims in prolonged skin contact the relevant restricted-substance tests typically include azo colourants under the EN ISO 14399 series, PAHs by GC-MS or equivalent, and phthalates, with heavy metals where the trim includes metallic thread, foil or hardware. A finished-article test on the product as shipped closes the gap that component-level testing leaves open, because dye, finishing and print-curing chemistry all act after the incoming components are tested.

7. What should happen to the chemical evidence file when I change a colour or print ink?

The affected test reports should be invalidated and re-issued, and the file should record which reports cover which specification version. This is the most common way a compliant programme silently becomes non-compliant: the specification changes, the evidence does not follow, and every colourway or print variant continues to run on a report that belongs to a different product. A supplier that cannot explain what happens to the evidence file when the specification changes is telling you the evidence is not being maintained.
