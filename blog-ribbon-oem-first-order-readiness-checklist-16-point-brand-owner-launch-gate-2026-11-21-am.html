A first order is the one order in a programme where every unknown becomes a schedule risk at the same time. A repeat order inherits a specification, an approved sample, a known price and a proven packing configuration. A first order inherits none of those, and the brand has usually committed a launch date, a marketing budget and a retail shelf date around it. The readiness work described in this guide takes two to three weeks. Discovering the same gaps during production takes four to six weeks and costs the launch slot.

Most first-order delays are not manufacturing failures. The factory is ready, the looms are running, the yarn is in stock. What stops production is a piece of information that never arrived: an artwork file that was still on a designer's laptop, a fibre composition nobody wrote down, a packing list without a barcode, a price that was agreed verbally. Each of these is individually small. Together they form a critical path that a factory cannot shortcut, because every shortcut at this stage creates a cost that reappears later - a printed batch with the wrong care label, a carton the distribution centre cannot receive, a colour that does not match the approved sample.

This guide is organised as a checklist rather than an essay on principles, because readiness is a state you either reach or do not. Each item below states what must be locked, who locks it, and what happens if it is still open when the purchase order is released.

The Order That Was Placed but Could Not Start

It is useful to describe the typical failure precisely, because the pattern repeats and it is almost never what the buyer expected it to be.

A brand owner has a seasonal launch, say a spring gifting range. Artwork is signed off by the design team in March. The buyer requests quotes in April, samples arrive in April, and a sample is approved in late April. The purchase order is placed in early May for delivery in early July, giving eleven weeks. Everyone believes there are four weeks of buffer.

Production starts in mid-May and stops three days later. The specification sheet, which the buyer believed was attached to the purchase order, lists the width and the material but not the fibre content, and the mill cannot determine whether the approved polyester sample referred to a 100 percent polyester filament or a polyester-viscose blend - two different yarns with two different dyeing behaviours. The dye house prepared a recipe from the sample, and the lot produced a shade that reads slightly warmer than the approved standard. The buyer rejects it. The lot is scrapped. A new lot runs. The date has moved, the launch has moved with it, and the retailer's shelf plan has moved with both.

None of this was the mill's fault. The information existed somewhere - in a message thread, in a spreadsheet, in the head of the person who approved the sample. It had simply never been written down in a form that production could consume. That distinction matters: the fix for this class of delay is not a better factory or a longer lead time. It is a readiness gate.

What First-Order Readiness Actually Means

Readiness for a first ribbon order means that every input the factory needs in order to start production on the confirmed date has been received, understood and locked in a form that cannot be misread. Not "known" - known in someone's email is not locked. Locked means: written down, in the agreed format, acknowledged by the party who will execute it, and not open to reinterpretation.

That definition has three corollaries that are worth stating explicitly, because buyers frequently get them wrong.

Readiness is a manufacturing input, not a procurement formality. A purchase order that describes what to buy does not describe how to make it. The document that makes production possible is the specification - material, construction, width, finish, decoration method, colour reference, tolerance, packing and labelling. The purchase order is a commercial instrument that refers to it. Many delays trace back to buyers who treated the two as the same document.

Readiness has an owner per item, not an owner overall. "The buyer owns readiness" is how readiness fails. Readiness is a shared obligation with named parties: the brand's merchandising or packaging designer owns artwork; the brand's product manager owns the specification; the buyer's procurement colleague owns price and terms; the factory's account manager owns confirmation that the specification is buildable. Each of those people holds a piece that nobody else can supply.

Readiness is confirmed, not assumed. The factory should return a written acknowledgement of the specification, stating explicitly that the item is buildable as written and listing any deviation it is proposing. A silent factory is a risk, not an acceptance. Silence usually means the sales contact assumed the account manager would read the file, and the account manager assumed the sales contact had checked it.

The Single Gate That Protects Your Launch Date

Rather than sixteen separate approval emails, experienced programmes put a single gate in front of the purchase order. Everything below hangs off that gate. It is a short document - one page is enough - confirming that the items are complete.

The gate has three rules.

  1. Nothing enters production from a document that has not passed the gate. If a change arrives after the gate - a colour substitution, a width change, a revised artwork file - it is a change request, handled under change control, not a silent amendment. This single rule prevents most of the "we thought you meant the other one" conversations that consume the final week before a launch.
  2. The gate has a date, and it is set backwards from the production start date. Typically four to six weeks before production for a first order with new artwork or new construction, less if the construction is standard and the artwork is a repeat. A gate without a date is a gate that will slip.
  3. Every item on the gate has a named owner and a status. Locked, in progress, or not applicable. "In progress" on the gate date is treated as failed, because the schedule no longer has room for it.

The gate should be a single conversation rather than a document exchange. A short call with the factory's account manager and the mill-side production planner, at which the items are read out and any deviation proposed on the spot, catches more problems than any amount of document circulation - because the planner can say, in thirty seconds, that the specified width will run on a different loom and shift the date, and that the alternative width is available on the original one.

Specification and Bill of Materials Lock

The specification is the foundation of everything else. For ribbon, it must be complete enough that a mill with no contact with the buyer could build the item correctly. The following fields are the minimum, and each of them has caused a real delay when left open.

FieldWhy it must be locked
Fibre content and constructionDetermines which yarn the mill must hold and how it will dye. This is the single most common cause of a shade or hand-feel mismatch on an approved sample.
Material familyPolyester satin, grosgrain, organza, velvet, crape and woven-edge tape behave differently in the same dye bath. Specify the construction, not only the fibre.
Width and toleranceWidth affects both the loom selection and the trim consumption per unit of finished goods. A width that is nominally available may not be the width the mill runs efficiently.
Finish and edge treatmentHeat-sealed, raw cut, stitched selvedge or wired edge. Determines whether the ribbon can be cut and sewn without fraying, and whether it suits the customer's assembly method.
Decoration methodScreen print, heat transfer, foil, jacquard, sublimation or none. Decoration method constrains the colour count, the minimum order quantity and the artwork format.
Colour reference and toleranceThe approved standard with its reference number and the agreed shade tolerance, plus the measurement method. Without the method, the tolerance cannot be enforced.
Performance requirementsWash fastness, crocking, light fastness, abrasion, stretch recovery, flammability, or a specific test standard where the end use demands it.
Care and composition labellingThe exact label content and language per destination market, agreed before printing rather than after.
Packing formatRoll format, core size, length per roll, polybag and liner, master carton configuration, and the barcode or label format required by the receiving site.
Delivery terms and destinationThe Incoterm, the delivery address and the consignee, and any specific appointment or documentation requirements at the destination.

Two of these deserve emphasis because they are routinely omitted by buyers who have never had to receive retail packaging.

Width tolerance. "38mm" is a nominal. State the acceptable band - typically plus or minus a fraction of a millimetre - and whether the tolerance applies to the woven width or the finished cut width. Ribbon that is nominally correct but half a millimetre wide on the low side will consume more per gift box than the costing assumed, and the discrepancy appears as a margin erosion on the first order rather than as a quality claim.

Packing format. This is where first orders fail most often, and the failure is expensive because it happens after production. A ribbon delivered on a 500-metre roll when the customer's assembly line expects a 100-metre roll, or delivered on a core that does not fit the customer's mandrel, cannot be used and must be re-wound, returned or scrapped - and re-winding is a manual operation that lands on someone's dock as an unexpected inbound.

Sample Approval and the Sign-Off Chain

Sample approval is where most buyers assume they are covered, because a sample did arrive and somebody did say it looked right. The gap between "somebody looked at it" and "the approval is valid" is where first-order delays live.

Four questions determine whether an approval will hold up. A competent supplier will answer all four; a supplier who cannot is telling you something important about how the programme will run.

  1. What does this sample actually prove? A marketing swatch proves nothing. A hand-made strike-off proves the colour direction and the hand-feel but not the production behaviour. A sample cut from production material of the correct construction, with the real decoration method applied, proves substantially more. Knowing which type you are holding determines how much risk remains.
  2. Who approved it, for what? A colour approval from a designer is a colour approval. It is not a specification approval, not an artwork approval and not a performance approval. Each of those needs a named signatory with authority over that dimension, and the approver must be the person who will own the result at retail.
  3. Is the approval recorded in a form production can consume? Written, dated, referencing the sample identifier and the specification revision. An approval by message thread is recoverable for months and unusable after that.
  4. What triggers re-approval? A change of base material, a new dye lot on a re-order, a change of decoration method, a revised artwork file. These must be named in advance so that a later decision to proceed is a decision, not an oversight.

For a first order specifically, one further point applies. The approved sample should be a sample that the factory retains a portion of, and that both sides label identically. When the first production lot runs and the buyer inspects it, the inspection is against a reference. Without a retained reference the inspection becomes an argument about what was approved, which is a different and much more expensive conversation.

Artwork Ownership and File Handover

Artwork is the most common single cause of a first-order delay and the easiest to fix in advance, because the fix is a file transfer and a written ownership statement rather than a negotiation.

Two questions must be answered before production begins.

Who owns the artwork? If the brand commissioned the design, the brand owns it and should say so in writing to the factory. If the factory created the design, the brand will usually want to own or licence it, and that must be agreed at the point of creation, not retrospectively. Unclear artwork ownership on a private label ribbon programme creates two problems: the factory may be cautious about printing a design it does not own, and the brand may find that the artwork it has been using to sell the product is not actually its property.

What format is the production file in? A print-ready file for ribbon decoration is not the same file as the artwork used in a marketing deck. The factory needs: the artwork at the repeat length the ribbon will actually use, with the repeat set so the pattern runs continuously along the length; the colour separations or the print specification; the physical size and the unit stated; and any overprint or trapping notes. Most factories will prepare a production file themselves from a brand-supplied file and send it back for approval - which is the correct workflow, but it consumes several days and must be scheduled, not assumed to be instant.

A practical rule that removes this risk entirely: agree the artwork handover as a scheduled event with a named deadline, and treat a production file that has not been approved by that deadline as a schedule risk rather than a routine delay. On a launch-critical programme, the artwork production file is usually the longest-lead item in the entire chain after a new mould or a new dye lot, and it is routinely treated as though it takes an afternoon.

Commercial Lock: Price, Terms and Capacity

Three commercial items must be locked at the same time as the specification, because each one can stop production even when the material is ready.

Price, in the agreed currency and with the agreed Incoterm. A price quoted for a different quantity, a different packing format or a different delivery term is not a price. The most common error is quoting on the mill's standard packing while the brand's costing assumes a specific retail-ready pack, and discovering the difference after the order is released. State the quantity, the packing, the destination and the term in the same sentence as the price.

Payment terms and the first-order payment structure. A first order with a new supplier is typically paid in advance or by irrevocable letter of credit, with the balance arrangement agreed for subsequent orders. Decide this before the order is placed, not during the negotiation of the second order. Suppliers that ask for 30 percent deposit and 70 percent against a bill of lading are not unusual; what is unusual and risky is discovering the structure late, because it changes the buyer's cash flow plan and sometimes the approval path.

Capacity confirmation with a date, not an assurance. "We can do it" is not a commitment. The commitment is: the factory confirms that it can allocate loom and dyeing capacity for this specification within the stated window, and names the production start date. Where a launch date is fixed, this is the point at which the buyer learns whether the date is actually achievable - which is far better learned eight weeks out than four days out.

Logistics and Packing Data Readiness

Logistics readiness is where the last surprises live, because it is usually owned by a different department from the one that placed the order.

Confirm the following before the goods are made, not after.

  • Barcode and label format - symbology, character set, placement on the carton, and whether the barcode is generated by the factory or supplied by the brand.
  • Carton configuration - pieces per carton, carton dimensions, gross weight, and the pallet configuration with the pallet type and the maximum stacking height the receiving site can handle.
  • Label content - the exact legal and commercial copy on the carton: composition, care instructions, country of origin, importer or responsible-party details, and your own SKU and lot identification.
  • Documents required on arrival - commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and any test report or compliance statement the destination market expects.
  • The receiving site's actual capability - a retailer's distribution centre may reject a pallet that exceeds its dock height or a carton weight above its handling limit, and the rejection happens after the goods have travelled.

Lot and batch identification deserves a specific mention. Agree before production that every carton carries a lot identifier that ties back to the production batch, and that the supplier retains a reference sample from that batch. When a retailer raises a quality query six months later, that identifier is the only thing that makes the query answerable, and without it the response is an argument about whether the material in the complaint came from your order.

Planning the Reorder Before You Need It

The last item on a first-order readiness list is forward-looking, and it is the one most often skipped: agree the reorder mechanism while the first order is still in production.

At the point of the first order, both parties know things they will not know later - the actual dye lot behaviour, the real yield, the true packing configuration, the honest lead time once the pipeline is full. This is the cheapest moment to agree the reorder terms, because the conversation is about a programme rather than about a dispute.

Three things are worth recording at this point, in the purchase order or its accompanying note.

The reorder price basis - whether it is fixed for a defined period, indexed to a published raw material reference, or simply quoted at the time, and over what quantity range it holds.

The reorder lead time - the real figure, measured from the date a confirmed order is received to the date goods are ready to ship, and whether the factory holds capacity for repeat business of the same specification.

The artwork and colour retention - how long the factory holds the production file and the dye recipe, and at what cost they are re-issued. A recipe retired at the end of a peak season and re-developed at the next one costs both money and an entire approval cycle.

None of this is glamorous. But the pattern across every first-order delay I have reviewed from the buyer side is the same: a small piece of information that was available, understood and agreed, but never written down where the person executing the work would find it. The readiness gate is simply the discipline of making sure that nothing important is still in someone's head when the order is released.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How long before the launch date should the first-order readiness gate be held?

For a first order with new artwork or a new construction, four to six weeks before the planned production start date. Standard constructions with repeat artwork can often be gated three weeks out. The date is set backwards from production start, not from the delivery date, because production start is where the unknown inputs have to arrive.

2. What is the single most commonly missed item in a ribbon OEM first order?

In our experience it is the fibre content and construction in the specification. Buyers routinely approve a sample without confirming whether it is a 100 percent polyester filament, a polyester-viscose blend or a different construction entirely. Because these yarns dye differently, the production lot can land on a different shade from the approved sample, and the resulting scrap is discovered only after the lot is dyed.

3. Can we release a purchase order before the artwork production file is approved?

It is possible but it is rarely wise. The artwork production file is usually the longest-lead item in the chain once the print method is fixed, and it depends on inputs - final colour separations, physical repeat length, print specification - that themselves require brand-side decisions. Releasing the order early does not shorten that path, it only removes the schedule buffer underneath it.

4. Does a readiness gate slow the programme down?

It moves work earlier rather than adding work. The gate is a short meeting and a one-page confirmation, and most of the items on it are decisions the brand has to make anyway. Its effect on the programme is that problems surface in week three instead of in the week before delivery, when there is no longer any option to do anything about them.

5. Who should own the readiness gate - the brand or the factory?

The brand initiates it and the factory co-signs it. The brand owns the inputs it controls, which are artwork, the specification, the colour decision and the commercial terms. The factory owns the inputs it controls, which are buildability, capacity, the lead time and the packing configuration. A gate with a single owner produces a document where one party's obligations are silently assumed rather than confirmed.

6. We are a small brand and cannot commit to large quantities on a first order. Does the checklist still apply?

Yes, and most of it applies more strongly. Small first orders are more exposed to cost-per-unit distortion, because setup and development charges spread across a smaller quantity, and more exposed to shade risk, because a smaller run is less likely to be produced in a single dye lot. In practice the readiness work at low volume is a larger proportion of the total effort, not a smaller one. Minimum order quantities from 500 metres allow a first order to be sized for real consumption rather than for a speculative bulk buy.

7. What happens if something changes after the readiness gate has been passed?

It should be handled as a change request, not as a quiet amendment. The gate rules normally require that any change after approval is raised in writing, assessed for its effect on cost, lead time and specification, and approved by the same authority that signed the original. A change made informally - a substituted yarn, an adjusted width, a revised artwork file - is the mechanism by which an approved programme silently stops being the approved programme.
