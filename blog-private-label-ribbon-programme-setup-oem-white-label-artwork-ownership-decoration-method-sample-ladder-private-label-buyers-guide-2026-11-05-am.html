The Difference Between Buying Ribbon and Buying a Private Label Programme

Most brand owners arrive at ribbon sourcing with a product question: what width, what material, what price per metre. That is the right question for a merchant, and it is the wrong question for a brand that intends to put its own name on the product.

A private label ribbon programme is not a purchase. It is a small manufacturing relationship, with tooling, artwork, colour standards, documentation, quality records and a supply rhythm attached to it. The unit price is one line in that relationship. The programme is what you are actually buying, and the cost of getting the programme structure right is worth more attention than another two percent on the metre price.

At Smith Ribbon in Xiamen, we have been running OEM programmes since 2004 for brands, retailers, gift companies and promotional suppliers across more than 50 countries. What follows is the structure we have seen hold up, written for the people who have to sign for it: brand managers who need speed to shelf, category buyers who need repeatability, and procurement managers who need a supplier they can explain to a colleague.

Six Decisions You Make Before the First Metre Is Woven

Every private label programme starts with decisions that look administrative and turn out to be commercial. These are the six that matter most, and the order in which they are best taken.

1. Who owns the design?

Before anything is quoted, establish in writing whether the brand owns the artwork outright, whether the factory produced it under contract, or whether it sits somewhere in between. This sounds like a formality until a programme is discontinued and the brand discovers that re-ordering requires a new tooling charge because the original artwork belonged to the supplier. We ask every client to confirm ownership at the quotation stage, in one line, before samples are made. It costs nothing then and it costs a great deal later.

2. Is the artwork your design or a supplier design?

There are three quite different situations, and they lead to different pricing and different risks:

  • Brand-supplied artwork — your designer creates the pattern, you own it, the factory converts it. Lowest design cost, highest specification control.
  • Factory-supplied artwork adapted for you — you modify an existing pattern. Faster and cheaper, but you should confirm in writing that your modified version is protected and that no other client receives the identical file.
  • Factory-supplied artwork with a minor change — you select from a stock pattern with a colour or width adjustment. Lowest cost, least differentiation, and generally the weakest commercial position if the ribbon becomes a visible brand asset.

For a brand that wants the ribbon to be recognisable on shelf, the second and third options are worth avoiding. A distinct pattern does more for shelf presence than a heavier substrate or a more complex print finish.

3. What is the decoration method?

The method constrains everything downstream: the colour range achievable, the tactile feel, the durability, the setup cost and the per-metre cost at volume. Screen printing gives clean separation and good durability at mid and high volumes. Foil stamping gives a metallic result with high shelf impact and a higher setup cost. Heat transfer allows complex imagery and full colour with a soft hand, at a premium. Jacquard weaves the pattern into the fabric itself, so there is no print layer at all and no risk of surface abrasion. Digital printing gives unlimited colour and short runs at a premium per metre.

Choose the method that fits the product's use and its expected volume, not the one that looks best in a swatch book. A foil-stamped ribbon on a gift box that sells in volume may cost more per metre than a printed polyester ribbon and still be the correct commercial answer for the brand.

4. How many SKUs are in the programme?

SKU count is the single biggest driver of programme cost that buyers most often underestimate. Each colour, each width and each decoration variant is a separate setup on the factory floor: a separate dye lot where the colour is custom, a separate screen or plate, a separate changeover. A programme with one ribbon in three colours is a different commercial animal from a programme with one ribbon in three colours across three widths and two print finishes, even though both are "nine SKUs" on a spreadsheet.

The practical advice is to sequence. Launch with the colours that drive volume, prove the demand, and add the remaining colours once the reorder rhythm is established. Factories that ask for a commitment across the full range before the first order are asking you to fund complexity before you have learned anything about the product.

5. What is the minimum order quantity, per SKU and in total?

MOQ is a negotiation, and it has two components that are usually quoted as one number. The first is the factory's economic minimum on a single run — the volume below which the setup cannot be amortised. The second is the total programme minimum, which exists so the factory can schedule the order sensibly. These are not the same constraint and they behave differently.

At our own operation the standard entry point for OEM customisation is 1,000 metres, with small-batch runs possible from 500 metres. What matters more than the number is understanding what happens when you need less. Ask what the surcharge structure is for a sub-MOQ run, and whether the surcharge applies to the whole order or only to the shortfall. A supplier who will state both clearly is telling you something useful about how they will behave when your forecast goes wrong.

6. What happens at reorder?

The reorder is where private label programmes reveal their true quality. The question to ask is not "can we reorder?" but "what has to happen again?" If the answer is "nothing, we have your file, your dye recipe and your colour standard on record", the programme is well set up. If the answer involves re-confirming artwork, re-running a lab dip or re-negotiating a price, the programme has a gap in it.

A well-run programme stores the dye recipe, the approved colour standard, the artwork file with bleed and trap specifications, the finishing parameters and the packaging specification against your account. That storage is a cost the factory carries and a value it delivers. It is worth paying attention to during supplier selection, because it is one of the clearest signals of whether a supplier intends to run a programme or simply to complete orders.

Building the Specification So It Can Be Ordered Again

A programme is only durable if the specification is durable. The most common failure is a specification written well enough to quote against and not well enough to reproduce in two years.

A workable ribbon specification covers material and construction, width and tolerance, colour standard with an agreed tolerance, decoration method with details of registration and coverage, edge and selvedge condition, finishing, roll format and core size, and packaging. Each of these should be expressed as a value that can be checked rather than a value that can only be admired. "Soft hand" is not checkable. "Shelf and hand feel consistent with the approved reference sample retained at the factory" is checkable, because the reference sample still exists.

Two specific disciplines pay for themselves repeatedly:

  • Colour standard approval against a physical reference. A Pantone number printed on paper is a starting point, not a standard. The standard is a swatch or a physical length of ribbon that has been through your approval process, retained by both sides, and reviewed for drift over time.
  • Roll format and core size specified precisely. Core size, winding direction, maximum roll diameter and the permitted defect or splice allowance all affect how the ribbon runs on the buyer's own equipment. Getting this wrong generates returns that look like quality failures and are not.

Packaging and Presentation as a Programme Decision

Secondary packaging is frequently the last thing decided and frequently the reason a programme is rejected at the buyer's receiving dock. It deserves to be an early decision, because it changes the economics.

Rolled ribbon shipped on a core does not need a box, but it needs protection against flattening and contamination, and it needs a carton that will survive the freight route. Ribbon supplied on a spool or a card needs a different presentation but arrives ready for a fulfilment operation. Ribbon supplied as pre-cut lengths or as assembled bows and trim sets needs a bill of materials that defines the kit precisely, because a trim set that arrives one bow short is a line-stopping problem for a fulfilment centre.

When a brand is building a retail programme, think about the packaging from the receiving dock backwards. A carton that arrives damaged costs more than the carton. A carton that cannot be opened quickly costs more than the carton. A carton that cannot be stacked on a full pallet costs considerably more, because it determines how the receiving warehouse has to build the load. These are ordinary packaging decisions with measurable consequences.

How the Sample Ladder Works

Getting from a concept to an approved article takes a defined sequence of samples, and each one proves something specific. Approving the wrong sample is the most common avoidable error in a private label programme, so it is worth being precise about what each stage can and cannot tell you.

  1. The material swatch — a length of the base substrate in your chosen colour. It proves the substrate hand, the weight and the drape. It proves nothing about print or finish.
  2. The lab dip or colour strike-off — the base fabric with your colour applied. This is where colour standard approval happens. A lab dip is about colour and shade; it is not a demonstration of print quality.
  3. The pre-production sample — the full specification, decorated, finished and wound, but before the production lot. This proves the artwork, registration, coverage and handle. It is the sample that should be signed against the specification.
  4. The pre-shipment sample — drawn from the actual production lot. This is the sample that proves the production run matches what was approved, and it is the one that carries the most weight when a claim is later made about lot consistency.

Two rules make the ladder work. First, never approve a specification on a pre-production sample alone — production changes hand and sometimes shade, and discovering that after a 10,000 metre run is expensive. Second, keep the approved sample. A retained reference, held by both buyer and factory, is what makes a quality conversation a comparison of two objects rather than two recollections.

What a Good Private Label Agreement Should Contain

Not every programme needs a formal agreement, but every programme benefits from a written record of the terms that matter. The clauses that carry the most weight in practice are these:

  • Artwork and design ownership, stated explicitly and in both directions.
  • Tooling, screens, plates, dies and physical samples — who pays for them, who owns them, and what happens to them if the programme ends.
  • Colour and quality standards, with the reference sample identified and the acceptance criteria written down.
  • Price and its basis, including how the price responds to a change in material cost or volume.
  • Lead time and the definition of when the clock starts, which is typically not the day the order is placed.
  • Defect remedy, expressed as a sequence: replacement, credit, rework, then price concession.
  • Documentation obligations — which certificates, test reports and declarations the factory must supply, and how often.
  • Termination and exit, including the notice period and what happens to work in progress.

The purpose of this document is not to be adversarial. It is to let both sides answer the ordinary questions without escalating. Most private label disputes are not about anybody being dishonest. They are about a term that was never written down, remembered differently by two people who were both being reasonable.

How Private Label Programmes Fail

The failures repeat, and they are worth naming because most are avoidable at the specification stage.

The programme that was never really specified. The order was placed against a photograph and a price. When a defect appeared, neither side could point to a document. The remedy becomes an argument about intent rather than a comparison against a standard.

Artwork supplied in the wrong form. A logo drawn for print on a white background will not separate cleanly onto a woven ground. Artwork needs bleed, trap, and a specification of the weave it will sit on. Files supplied without this produce a print that looks wrong in a way that is hard to describe and impossible to dispute clearly.

Colour approved from a screen. Screens are not calibrated, print conditions differ, and a shade approved on a monitor can be rejected at the factory as visibly wrong. Physical approval is the only reliable method.

The reorder that reopens everything. The programme was delivered to a standard the first time, but the factory has since changed a machine, a shift pattern or an operator, and the reorder is subtly different. This is why the retained reference sample and the stored dye recipe matter.

The supplier who was reasonable at quotation and obstructive at claim time. The quote stage reveals willingness and commercial judgement. The claim stage reveals structure. Both matter, and they are not the same evidence.

Building a Programme That Scales

The strongest programmes we see are not the largest. They are the ones where the buyer has kept the specification stable, the reorder is genuinely cheap, and the relationship has moved past the point of renegotiating every order. Three things tend to produce that outcome.

First, fix the specification and change it rarely. Every change costs artwork, a sample, an approval and a risk of inconsistency across the installed base. A small, controlled change process protects more value than it costs.

Second, run a rolling reorder rhythm rather than placing large orders infrequently. A predictable weekly or monthly reorder at a moderate size lowers the cost of working capital and reduces the risk of a stockout on a best-selling colour. It also gives the factory a steady signal about the programme's real importance.

Third, review the programme quarterly, even when nothing is wrong. Colour drift accumulates gradually, competition moves, and a specification that was right two years ago may no longer be cost-effective. A short, regular review keeps the programme current without the disruption of a change.

What Good Looks Like in Practice

A healthy private label ribbon programme has a few recognisable properties. The buyer can place a reorder in an afternoon, because everything the factory needs is on record. The factory can answer a question about the programme from memory, because the programme is theirs too. Disputes are resolved by comparison rather than argument, because both sides hold the same reference. The brand can add a colour or a width without renegotiating the whole agreement, because the structure was built to absorb that. And the ribbon on shelf still looks like the ribbon the brand designed, four reorder cycles later, because the specification was written to be reproduced rather than merely to be ordered once.

That is the standard worth aiming for. A programme that reaches it is worth considerably more than the difference between two quotes, because it removes a category of operational risk that does not appear on any spreadsheet until the day it fails.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand managers, category buyers and procurement teams most often ask us about setting up a private label ribbon programme, from artwork ownership to sample approval and reorder consistency. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the minimum order quantity for custom private label ribbon?

At Smith Ribbon the standard entry point for OEM customisation is 1,000 metres per design, with small-batch runs possible from 500 metres where the design is simple. MOQ has two components that are often quoted as one number: the factory's economic minimum on a single run, and a total programme minimum that allows sensible scheduling. Ask for both separately, and ask what a sub-MOQ run costs, because that surcharge is what you will actually pay if your forecast is wrong.

2. Who owns the artwork and tooling in a private label ribbon programme?

It should be stated in writing at the quotation stage, before samples are made. Typically the brand owns the artwork outright, while physical tooling such as screens, plates, dies and retained reference samples may be owned by the factory but recorded on the brand's account, with their status addressed in the agreement so that they are available if the programme is discontinued or transferred. This single line of writing costs nothing to agree early and is one of the most expensive things to sort out late.

3. Should I use printed, woven or foil-stamped ribbon for a branded programme?

Printed ribbons (screen print or digital) suit most branded programmes because they give clean colour separation, good durability and reasonable cost at mid and high volume. Foil stamping gives strong shelf impact with a metallic finish at a higher setup cost. Jacquard weaves the pattern into the fabric, so there is no surface print to abrade and nothing to crack, which suits premium programmes and durable applications. The right answer depends on your volume, your target retail price and how the ribbon is handled after it reaches the shelf, not on appearance alone.

4. How long does it take to get a custom ribbon programme from artwork to shipment?

For a standard custom programme the usual sequence is around 25 to 35 days from approved artwork and colour standard to shipment for a production run of common specifications, with sample development adding 7 to 15 days ahead of that. The variable that most often extends the timeline is artwork readiness, not production capacity. Programmes with jacquard weaving, multi-colour printing, special finishes or large seasonal peaks should be planned with more lead time, and holiday and year-end windows should be reserved well ahead.

5. What documentation should I get with a private label ribbon order?

You should receive a commercial invoice and packing list as a minimum, plus any applicable test reports and certificates held on file for your programme, and a batch or lot reference that links the delivered goods to the production lot. For the supply of goods into the EU and UK, documentation should support customs declaration and, where relevant, product traceability under the current digital product passport requirements. Ask early which documents your factory actually holds for your specific material and programme rather than assuming, because the answer varies with substrate, dye and decoration method.

6. How do I make sure the colour on the reorder matches the colour on the first order?

Approve colour against a physical reference, not a screen and not a Pantone number alone. The reference should be a retained physical length of ribbon from the approved lot, held by both the factory and the buyer, reviewed under consistent light. The factory should hold the dye recipe and the tolerance agreed against it, and any reorder should be checked against the same reference before shipment. This is the single most effective control on lot-to-lot consistency and the one most often skipped when a programme moves from a development order to a routine reorder.

7. How many colours and widths should a first private label ribbon order include?

Fewer than you think, for the first order. Each additional colour, width or decoration variant is a separate setup on the factory floor, a separate dye lot where colour is custom, and a separate changeover, so the true cost of a nine-SKU programme is not comparable to three colours of one ribbon. Launch with the colours that drive the majority of demand, establish the reorder rhythm, then add the remaining colours once you understand real sell-through. Suppliers who press for a full-range commitment before the first order are asking you to fund complexity before you have learned anything about the product.
