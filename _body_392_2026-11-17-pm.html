Buyers who have worked with ribbon for a few years develop a suspicion about how much of the specification is really being controlled. They ask for a 25-metre roll in a specific colour, and the goods arrive — but the rolls are heavier than expected, the spool is a different size to last time, and the label carries a reference nobody in the warehouse can match to a purchase order line. Nothing is wrong. Nothing is late. The roll simply is not the roll that was ordered, because the roll was never specified.

This guide covers what a complete ribbon roll specification contains and how to write one that a mill can quote against and a warehouse can receive. It covers the physical elements — core, spool, width, winding, length, weight — and the identification and packaging that sit around them. It is written for brand-side procurement and packaging engineers who order ribbon for gift sets, apparel trim, cosmetic packaging and retail programmes, and for the sourcing managers who need to specify it precisely enough to be comparable between suppliers.

Why the Roll Specification Is a Separate Decision

Ribbon is normally described by material, width, construction and colour. Those four attributes determine what the ribbon is. The roll specification determines what the ribbon arrives as — how it is packaged, what it is weighed against, how it is identified, and how your line has to handle it. These are different decisions with different consequences, and they are frequently left to the mill's default because they feel like packaging details rather than specification items.

That assumption is expensive in three ways that are visible in a cost sheet long before anyone argues about quality.

Element of the roll specificationWho it actually affectsWhat goes wrong when it is unspecified
Core or spool typeYour production line, your warehouse, your freightRolls that will not fit your equipment, or that jam when the operator tries to adapt them
Net weight vs metre basisYour purchasing, your costing, your customs declarationQuantity disputes at invoice, and a true cost per metre that differs from the quoted cost per metre
Length per rollYour cutting room, your operator time, your changeover frequencyRolls that are too short to complete a set, so the operator joins ribbon mid-piece

The general principle is straightforward. If a characteristic of the delivered roll affects your cost, your line, your labour or your ability to trace a problem, it belongs in the specification. If it only affects the mill's convenience, leave it to the mill.

Core and Spool: What to Specify and Why

The core is the rigid tube around which the ribbon is wound. It is the single most consequential element of the roll specification, because it determines the roll's geometry, your packaging footprint and your line compatibility.

The common core sizes

Core diameterTypical useTrade-off
25 mm / 1 inchThe default for narrow ribbon, trim, elastic, most decorative ribbon. Used across most gift and apparel trim work.Smallest roll diameter, so the most rolls per carton and the most frequent re-threading on a long-running line.
38 mm / 1.5 inchWider ribbon, heavier constructions, wound ribbon for gift bows.A reasonable middle ground. Used where 25 mm rolls are too short for the required run length.
76 mm / 3 inchWide ribbon, heavy rolls, retail spools, wound trim for hair accessories and home textiles.Largest roll diameter. Fewer rolls per carton, higher freight per metre, but far fewer thread changes on the line.
Conical / tapered coreSpecific winding configurations for certain automated equipment.Machine-specific. Specify only where the receiving equipment requires it, and give the mill the machine reference.
Cardboard tube vs plastic spoolBoth are used on 25 mm and 38 mm cores. Cardboard is standard for most decorative ribbon; plastic spools are used where the roll will be dispensed repeatedly or where the roll must survive handling.Plastic adds cost per roll and usually needs a wider flange. Cardboard is cheaper and is the default unless there is a reason.

The three numbers that define a spool

When specifying a spool, state all three of the following, because suppliers use the terms loosely in conversation and precisely in the order form.

  1. Inner diameter — the bore the shaft of the machine fits onto. This is a hard machine requirement and should never be left open.
  2. Overall width — how wide the flange is. This must exceed the ribbon width, usually by 5 to 10 mm, or the ribbon will overhang the flange and foul the unwinding guide.
  3. Flange style — flat flanges, flanged on one side only, or with a notched or slotted flange for locating pins. This is where a mismatch causes a line to stop immediately rather than after a thousand metres.

A buyer's specification sheet that says "ribbon on spool" without these three numbers is not a specification, and the mill will supply whatever its default is. When you receive a consignment whose spools will not mount, the cost falls on the freight, not the mill, because the order did not say what you needed.

Width, Slitting and the Sliver Problem

Ribbon is woven at a nominal width and then often slit narrower. Slitting is where a wide ribbon is cut into narrower ribbons by a series of blades, and it is a process worth understanding because it explains a set of defects buyers otherwise find mysterious.

Why slitting creates problems

Slitting is a cutting operation, and cutting produces two edges. Those edges are raw — they are not heat-sealed, not bound and not woven — so they will fray under handling, abrasion and repeated flexing. This is why slit ribbon is usually supplied with an edge treatment, and why the edge treatment has to be specified separately from the width.

Edge treatmentHow it is producedWhat it protects against
Woven selvedgeThe edge is woven as part of the fabric, so there is no cut edge at all.Maximum fray resistance. The default for high-quality narrow ribbon and the only true edge for goods that will be heavily flexed or cut.
Heat-sealed edgeThe cut edge is passed over a heated blade or a sealing roller that melts the fibre at the edge.Good fray resistance on thermoplastic fibres such as polyester and nylon. Less effective on rayon, cotton or paper-backed constructions, where the seal does not adhere.
Overlocked / stitched edgeAn overlock thread or a binding stitch is run along the edge.Very good fray resistance on any fibre, including natural and paper-based. Adds a visible thread line and a small cost.
Raw cut edgeNo treatment.Nothing. Acceptable only where the edge will be bound, stitched or hidden in the finished product.

The second slitting issue is tolerance. A slitting operation has a natural width variation, and the buyer must decide how much of it to accept. Typical practice is a width tolerance of plus or minus 1 mm on a nominal width, with tighter tolerances available for narrow ribbon where the percentage matters more. The more important point is to specify the tolerance as a maximum deviation on the measured width rather than as a nominal, and to state that it applies to every roll rather than to the batch average. Batch-average tolerance can be satisfied by a consignment containing some rolls that are unusable.

Edge cuts and slivers

A sliver is a loose thread or a small fragment of ribbon detached during slitting, usually from a blade that has become dull or from a fibre that has not been anchored properly. Slivers are not a cosmetic issue — in a gift set or a cosmetic package, a single sliver on the ribbon is visible and is a complaint. The preventive measure is a defined sliver control in the mill, which for a serious buyer means asking about blade change frequency and asking to see slivers pulled from goods rather than only slivers found on the surface of the wound roll.

Length and Net Weight: The Two Bases of Order Quantity

This is the part of the roll specification that most often causes a dispute, and it is worth being precise about because the distinction is straightforward once stated.

Net weight and metreage are not interchangeable

Ribbon is sold commercially by weight and consumed physically by length. The relationship between the two depends on the ribbon itself: its width, its construction, its fibre content and any applied finish. A given kilogram of 38 mm grosgrain yields a very different number of metres from the same kilogram of 10 mm organza.

This means metreage is a derived figure for any ribbon whose specification you have already fixed. If the material, width, construction and finish are all specified, then the metres per kilogram follow from the construction — and a supplier who is asked to deliver a specified metreage on a specified construction is being asked to hit a weight tolerance by proxy, which is harder than hitting a weight tolerance directly.

So the recommendation is: specify net weight as the controlling quantity, and state the expected metreage as a derived reference, with an agreed tolerance on both. That way the mill controls the variable it can actually control, and you still have the metreage figure you need for costing and for your own production planning.

Order basisWhen it is rightWhat to watch
Net weight (kg)Almost all bulk ribbon procurement. The production unit. Drives the invoice, the customs declaration and the freight calculation.The metreage you receive varies with construction tolerance. If your costing is per metre, ask the mill to state the expected yield and confirm it against your own consumption records.
Length (m or yd)Where the finished good has a defined run length: retail spools, cut-length trim, ribbon wound to a set length for a set.The net weight becomes derived and can swing if construction tolerance is wide. Specify both, and make net weight the tolerance-bearing number.
Piece countPre-cut lengths or specific cut pieces such as bows and tiebacks.Does not capture the material consumed in the cutting process unless a cutting allowance is stated.
Yards per pound / metres per kiloLegacy industry units, still common in US conversations.Ambiguous unless the width is stated with it. Always convert to a single basis before comparing quotations.

Net weight versus gross weight

Gross weight includes the spool and the packaging; net weight is the ribbon alone. Both need to be specified because they are used for different purposes, and a packing list that quotes only one of them guarantees a reconciliation problem at the other end.

FigureWhere it is usedEffect of getting it wrong
Net weight per rollCommercial invoice, purchase order reconciliation, cost per metre, yield analysis, customs declaration as the basis of the quantity of goods.The consignment appears short against the order by the weight of every spool and carton in it.
Gross weight per rollFreight calculation, carrier charges, warehouse handling, and comparison against shipment weight limits.Freight is charged on a different basis than the invoice, and the two no longer reconcile.
Gross weight per cartonAir freight, courier and parcel shipping, and container weight calculation for road and sea freight.Air freight charges on actual or volumetric weight whichever is greater, and a consignment can exceed a courier's per-parcel limit.
Number of rolls per cartonWarehouse and receiving operations, carton markings, and the picking sequence.Cartons that are too heavy to lift safely, or so light that the receiving labour cost exceeds the value of the goods.

A worked example of the weight basis

A purchase order calls for 5,000 kg of 20 mm grosgrain in a specified colour, on 25 mm cores, at plus or minus 5 per cent net weight tolerance per roll, packed 20 rolls per carton. The invoice should show net weight of 5,000 kg, gross weight including spools and cartons, and the computed metreage. The carton count is derived, not stated. If the mill delivers 4,850 kg net and 5,120 kg gross, that is within tolerance and the reconciliation is correct — provided the buyer was comparing against net weight, which they should have been.

The failure mode is always the same: the purchase order says "5,000 kg" without saying net, the supplier delivers 4,850 kg net and 5,120 kg gross, the buyer checks the gross figure against the order, sees 2.4 per cent over, and files a claim for short delivery on a consignment that is actually compliant. Specifying the basis in one line on the order prevents this entirely.

Winding Quality Defects and How to Test for Them

Winding quality determines how the ribbon behaves on the receiving line, and it is a fast thing to assess. The defects below are visible without instruments, and a buyer who checks them at goods-inward pays for them once rather than through finished-goods rejection.

DefectWhat it looks likeWhat it causesHow it is prevented
Uneven end faceThe end of the roll is not flat — one side of the core protrudes, or the face is stepped or conical rather than square.Constant rubbing on the unwind, snagging, and uneven tension as the roll diameter changes.Controlled winding tension and traverse alignment, with the end face checked during winding.
Over-wound / roll hardnessThe roll is unusually hard and shows a visible dent or depression along its length when pressed, or the layers bond slightly together.On a running line, the roll will not unwind smoothly — the ribbon takes a set, and printed or wired faces can mark against themselves.A specified winding tension window and a hardness limit, checked at the end of the roll.
Under-wound / loose rollThe roll is soft, the edges move, and layers shift when the roll is lifted or turned.Telescoping in transit, ribbon falling off the core, and a jam if the ribbon is drawn from a stationary roll.Minimum winding tension specification, and a roll that is stable when stood on its end without support.
Ribbon overhangRibbon extends beyond the flange of the spool.Immediate fouling of the unwind guide, and damaged ribbon edges.Flange width specified with adequate clearance, and a check at the start of each roll.
Wrong winding directionThe decorated, wired or printed face is wound on the inside, against the next layer.Face-to-face abrasion that dulls the print and dulls the metallic of wired ribbon. Often not visible until the roll is used.Winding direction stated explicitly in the order, even when the requirement is routine for that product.
Slivers and loose fibresSmall detached fragments or threads, either on the surface of the wound roll or inside the core when unwound.Visible contamination in a gift set or cosmetic package; a complaint and a rework cost.Blade change frequency on slitting, cleaning discipline at the winding station, and sliver checks drawn from goods rather than from the roll surface.
Core damage or displacementThe core is crushed, the wind is off-centre, or the core has slipped within the roll.Run-out at speed, uneven tension, and a roll that will not seat on the machine shaft.Core specification to a minimum wall thickness, and a check that the core is properly seated before winding begins.
Ribbon kink or setThe ribbon holds a sharp fold or a curved memory when unrolled.Obvious in the finished product, and an extra operation to remove.Handling and storage conditions during winding and packing, and correct roll tension. Also a check on how the ribbon was conditioned before winding.

The thirty-second roll check

A practical routine for incoming inspection that catches most of the above requires no equipment. Take a random roll from the consignment, stand it on its end on a flat surface and check that it stands without leaning. Run your thumb across the end face and feel for a flat, square profile. Press the side of the roll and look for a dent. Check that the ribbon does not overhang the flange. Unwind two metres and look at the face — is the print or wire on the outside as specified, and are there slivers. Unroll it and check for kinks. This takes half a minute and catches nearly everything in the table above.

Two further checks are worth adding. First, weigh three rolls at random against the stated net weight and check the tolerance yourself rather than accepting the mill's figures — a systematic overstatement of net weight is not uncommon and is invisible without a scale. Second, check the meterage on the same rolls against the yield figure the mill quoted, because a consistent relationship between the two confirms the construction is what was ordered.

Roll Labelling and Lot Identification

Labeling is where a buyer's traceability chain is either built or broken. It is also the element most often left to the mill's default, because it seems like a small administrative matter rather than a specification item.

Three rules that make labels useful

Label the spool, not only the carton. The carton is removed within minutes of arrival, usually on the receiving dock, and after that the roll is identified only by its own label. A lot code on the carton that does not travel with the roll is a lot code you will need and will not have.

Keep the label legible and durable. A label that fades in a warehouse with chemical handling, or is covered by the ribbon overhang, is not an identification system. Adhesive labels applied under tension on a wound roll tend to lift at the edges; the more robust approach for high-volume goods is a printed sleeve or a printed band around the roll rather than a small sticker on the core.

Agree the format with the receiving operation, not only with the purchasing system. The most useful format is one the warehouse can read at a glance from a metre away in a poorly lit goods-inward area, with the SKU in the largest type. A label optimised for a database and read by nobody on the floor is a label that gets transcribed by hand, and hand transcription is where the errors enter the system.

The lot code is the field that matters most

If a single element of the roll specification is going to be enforced, it should be the lot identifier. Every quality dispute involving ribbon eventually turns on the question of which production run a roll came from, and a mill that weaves on a shared order book will produce the same colourway in several dye lots over a period of weeks. Without a lot code, a shade variation found in week six cannot be traced back to the dye lot in week two, and the only available response is to quarantine everything.

For buyers with certification or social compliance obligations, the lot code is also the link between the delivered goods and the documentation: the test report, the batch record and the inspection report all use the same identifier. A buyer who specifies a lot code has, without any further effort, built the evidence file that a retailer audit will later ask for.

Carton and Pack Specification

The carton specification is partly a logistics decision and partly a labour-safety decision, and the second is where buyers most often under-specify.

ElementWhat to specifyReason
Rolls per cartonA number, not a maximum. Often 10, 20, 25 or 30 rolls depending on core and roll diameter.A stated number lets the mill confirm the carton weight and lets the warehouse plan a putaway location.
Maximum gross carton weightA limit, typically 18 to 25 kg depending on the destination market's manual handling norms and warehouse policy.This is a safety limit. Cartons above it should not be accepted, and it is far cheaper to specify than to re-handle.
Carton constructionCorrugated board grade, and whether it is a regular slotted container or a telescoping or tuck-end box for easy opening.Board grade affects stacking strength, which matters when cartons are stacked several high in a container.
Internal protectionRoll separation by dividers, tissue or a sleeve; whether rolls are individually sleeved or wrapped as a group.Rolls that rub against each other in transit pick up abrasion at the edges — the part of the ribbon most likely to be visible in a gift set.
Barcode or data matrixFormat, placement, symbology and whether it encodes SKU, lot, quantity or all three.A barcode that encodes the lot code removes the manual transcription step at goods-inward entirely.
Carton markingShipping marks: shipper, consignee, PO number, carton number in the format X of Y, and handling instructions.Carton numbering in X of Y format is what makes partial receipt and shortage claims tractable.
Pallet configurationWhether the consignment is palletised, at what height and with what treatment.An unpalletised consignment of small cartons is slow to handle and easy to damage; palletised is faster but adds a floor-space and handling requirement.
Moisture protectionWhether the carton must be lined or the consignment must be shrink-wrapped or desiccant added.Relevant to paper-backed and natural-fibre ribbon, and to any sea transit through a humid period.

Two practical points specific to the roll rather than the carton. First, roll protection inside the carton should be specified by the ribbon, not in general terms, because a heavy satin ribbon wound tightly and a light organza wound loosely have entirely different abrasion and compression behaviour in the same carton. Second, specify whether the rolls are to be individually sleeved; individual sleeving costs money per roll and adds a disposal step at the receiving end, but it protects decorated surfaces in a way no carton design can.

Five Mistakes That Cost Real Money

Specifying width but not core. The order arrives, the rolls do not fit the line, and the consignment is either returned or re-wound at a cost that routinely exceeds the value of the goods.

Ordering by metres on a construction with wide tolerance. The mill hits the metreage, the weight varies with the construction tolerance, and the cost per metre you budgeted is not the cost per metre you paid.

Omitting "net" from the weight basis. A reconciliation dispute on every consignment, with a claim raised against a supplier who has in fact delivered compliantly.

Accepting the mill's default labelling. A default label will not carry your SKU and will not carry a lot code, and both of those become urgent the first time you need to quarantine a partial lot.

Leaving winding direction and edge treatment implicit. Both surface as a complaint from the finished-goods stage, at which point the ribbon is already in a set, a carton or a gift box, and the scrap cost is multiplied by the value of the assembly.

Summary

A ribbon order is a specification of what the ribbon is and a specification of how it arrives, and the second half is where the money leaks. Specify the core and spool completely, state the quantity basis as net weight with metreage derived, set the tolerances as per-roll maxima, specify the edge treatment and winding direction explicitly, require lot identification on the spool itself, and set the carton standard with a manual handling weight limit. None of this is complicated, and every field takes a line on the order form. What it prevents is the class of problems that consume far more time than the specification would ever have cost.
