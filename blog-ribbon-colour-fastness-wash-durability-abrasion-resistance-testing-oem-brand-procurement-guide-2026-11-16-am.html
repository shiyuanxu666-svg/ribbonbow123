The core principle: colour fastness is a property of the finished ribbon, not of the dye batch. A dye house can pass every test on greige yarn and you can still receive ribbon that fades, bleeds or abrades at the fold, because the test surface, the weave and the decoration change the result. Any durability specification you write should state the test method, the exposure level and the surface — and a supplier who quotes only ‘good colour fastness’ without those three has given you a promise rather than a specification.

Decorative ribbon is cheap relative to the product it is sewn into, and it is often the element that determines whether a garment, a gift set or a home accessory still looks right after six months of use. It is also the element nobody tests properly until a complaint arrives. A buyer who wants to avoid that outcome does not need a chemistry degree; they need to know which three or four tests apply to their application, what numbers to write into the specification, and how to read a report when it arrives.

This guide works through those tests in the order a buyer would encounter them: what the property means, which method measures it, how construction and decoration change the result, how to write the specification so a mill can comply with it, and what evidence to insist on before releasing a bulk order. The examples are drawn from the applications that generate the most trim: apparel, home textiles, gift packaging and pet accessories.

What Colour Fastness Actually Measures

Colour fastness is the resistance of a coloured textile to any transfer of colour. That definition is broad because the phenomenon is broad: colour can be lost to light, rubbed off onto an adjacent surface, washed out, or driven out by moisture and sweat. Each of those is a separate failure with a separate cause and a separate test, and a specification that names only the property leaves every one of those decisions to the supplier.

There is a second reason this matters commercially, and it is the one buyers tend to underweight. Trim failure is rarely caught where it can be fixed cheaply. Light fade might be noticed on the shelf; bleeding is noticed in the consumer's laundry; abrasion is noticed when the customer writes to complain. At that point the cost is not the trim. It is the returned units, the negative review, the chargeback, and the question of whether the failure generalises across the whole production run rather than being isolated to one carton. Durability testing is insurance priced in hours of lab time.

Two distinctions are worth making early, because they are frequently blurred.

  • Fastness is about colour transfer; durability is about the ribbon surviving. A ribbon can hold its colour perfectly and still be a poor product if it shrinks, distorts or delaminates. These are separate specifications and they should be written separately.
  • Fibre is not the same as construction. Two ribbons made of the same polyester will behave completely differently depending on weave, density, finish and selvedge construction. Any test result is a result for a specific construction, not for a fibre type.

The Core Test Methods and What They Simulate

Light fastness

Light fastness measures how resistant a colour is to prolonged exposure to daylight. The method of record for most purposes is xenon arc weathering with a controlled exposure cycle and a standard grey-scale for evaluating the colour change. Xenon arc is preferred over a simple ultraviolet lamp because it reproduces a broad spectrum including visible light and heat, and because the exposure is repeatable and dose-measurable, which means two laboratories can compare results.

The evaluation is normally expressed as a grade on the familiar scale from 1 to 5, where 5 means negligible colour change and 1 means severe. A practical commercial rule for decorative trim is grade 4 or better for product that will sit in a lit retail environment or in a home, and grade 3 as a lower bound for product that is packaged and only exposed to ambient light. For ribbon destined for seasonal retail that sits under retail lighting for a full season, ask for the higher grade; a grade 3 ribbon in a bright window can shift visibly in a matter of weeks.

One point that catches buyers: light fastness of a decorated ribbon is the light fastness of the decoration as well as the ground fabric. A printed pigment can be less stable than the base cloth even when both are perfectly stable individually. Ask for the test to be performed on the decorated article.

Rubbing and crocking

Rubbing fastness, frequently called crocking, measures the transfer of colour from the ribbon to an adjacent textile under controlled rubbing. The method uses a crockmeter with a specified load and either a dry or a wet rubbing arm, with a white or standard undyed cotton or wool felt as the rubbing medium. The result is graded by the amount of colour transferred to the felt.

Wet crocking is the harder test and the more relevant one for anything that will meet moisture — apparel next to skin, hair accessories, gift wrapping that is handled with damp hands, and pet accessories. A ribbon that passes dry crocking at grade 4 can fail wet crocking at grade 2, and it is entirely possible for the mill to quote you the dry result if nobody asked for the wet one.

Dry crocking is still worth specifying for reasons specific to trim. Ribbon rub against itself at a fold, against an adjacent panel of the same garment, or against the surface of the product next to it. In a gift set where a ribbon is looped around a box and tied in a bow, the interior of the loop is a persistent rubbing zone. This is a real failure mode in premium packaging and it is invisible unless you test the ribbon in contact with itself.

Washing

Wash testing assesses the ribbon after one or more specified laundering cycles, and evaluates colour change alongside other properties. The domestic washing procedure and the industrial or institutional procedure are different methods with different agitation and temperatures, and they produce different results. A trim intended for a machine-washed garment should be tested with a procedure representative of that garment, not with the gentler domestic cycle used for delicate textiles.

Two things must be stated for the result to mean anything: the cycle count and the conditions. A single-cycle result is close to meaningless for a garment expected to be washed fifty times. And the temperature matters because higher temperatures stress fixation more aggressively, which is precisely where an under-fixed disperse dye will show up.

Perspiration

Perspiration testing measures colour transfer under artificial acid and alkaline perspiration, applied with a controlled pressure and dwell time, with the colour change evaluated on the ribbon and the transfer assessed on an adjacent white fabric. This is the test that catches problems that a wash test will miss, because perspiration is acidic and alkaline, it has a low pH, and it sits against the skin for hours at body temperature.

For trim in apparel, this is frequently the most commercially important fastness test of all. Ribbons that contact skin — collar trims, tie tapes, decorative binding on necklines, hair accessories, and jewellery-style trim on sleepwear — are exactly the applications where a dye that passes everything else can still mark a wearer's skin or a pale garment.

Why Decoration Changes the Result

This is the section that most often separates a buyer who has had a trim failure from one who has not, because it is where the intuitive model breaks down. The intuitive model says the ribbon is either fast or it is not. In practice, the decoration is part of the material system and it has its own failure modes with their own causes.

DecorationPrimary Durability RiskWhat Determines Survival
Woven-in patternMinimal; no separate layer to failYarn colour fastness and weave density
Screen printCracking at flex points and fold linesBinder chemistry, ink deposit, curing temperature
Heat transferPeeling under repeated flexingAdhesive system, dwell time, press temperature and pressure
Foil stampingCracking and flaking; metallic layer has no elasticityAdhesive, foil type, substrate surface energy
Embossing and debossingSurface flattening that alters hand feelPressure, dwell, temperature, fibre resilience
Velvet or flocked pilePile crushing, matting and sheddingPile height, anchoring, backing adhesion
Fringe and multi-yarn trimStrand abrasion and tanglingYarn count, twist, edge treatment

Two practical rules follow. First, test the decorated article. A ribbon that passes every fastness test in plain form and carries a foil-stamped logo may fail at the fold line after a dozen washes, and you will only discover that if the foil goes with it. Second, test in the geometry it will be used in. A ribbon that lies flat will not reveal its flex behaviour; a ribbon that has been folded and knotted reveals it immediately. Ask the laboratory to crease and flex the specimens before the wash cycles if the product involves a knot, a bow or a loop.

Wash Durability Is a Different Question

Wash durability asks whether the ribbon survives laundering as a construction. It is assessed on a different set of properties from colour fastness, and a ribbon can score well on one and badly on the other.

PropertyWhat You Are Looking ForWhy It Fails
Dimensional stabilityMinimal shrinkage in length and widthUnrelaxed tension in the weave; thermoplastic fibres relaxing under heat and agitation
Width stabilityEdges stay square after washingSelvedge construction and warp tension imbalance
Surface integrityNo pilling, matting or fuzzingShort staple fibres and loose floats at the face
Print and decoration adhesionDesign intact after the final cycleInsufficient binder, poor curing, or flexing at fold lines
Hand feelSoftness and drape retainedFinish washed out or over-set by repeated heat
Edge integrityNo fraying or selvedge liftGlued or stitched selvedge rather than woven edge
Colour retentionGrade maintained after washingUnder-fixed dye, or incomplete rinsing at the dye house

Shrinkage deserves particular attention because it is the failure buyers notice immediately and because it is frequently missed in the specification. Polyester relaxes under heat and agitation, and a ribbon that has not been adequately pre-shrunk or heat-set will move. For trim sewn into a garment, a length change of two per cent is completely visible, because the trim is a designed element sitting on the surface where misalignment shows. State the shrinkage tolerance and the measurement method — length and width, before and after, at which cycle — in your specification.

The same logic applies to the edges. A woven-edge ribbon, where the selvedge is formed as part of the weave, will not fray. A ribbon with a folded, glued or stitched selvedge can lift at the corners of a gift box after handling, and the lifted edge is visible even when the colour is perfect.

Abrasion Resistance by Construction

Abrasion resistance is not a fibre property in any practical sense. It is a property of the construction, and this is worth understanding because the intuition runs the wrong way. A satin with long smooth floats feels better and looks more premium than a ribbed grosgrain, and it is the satin that abrades badly, because the floats are long, loosely bound and catch under rubbing before they break.

  • Satin. Smooth, high lustre, but long floats abrade and pill at the face. Acceptable for decorative accents and low-contact trim; poor for handles, bag straps or anything repeatedly handled.
  • Grosgrain. Ribbed surface, higher yarn count in the weft, generally better tolerance of abrasion in practice because the ribs deflect contact and the weave is denser.
  • Jacquard and dobby. Woven patterns put the wear on the high points and are often more durable than a smooth-faced ribbon of equivalent fibre.
  • Woven edge construction. Materially better than glued or stitched selvedge for fray resistance, which matters wherever the trim edge is visible or handled.
  • Velvet and flock. Excellent appearance, poor abrasion tolerance in the sense of pile crushing and matting. Best used where contact is minimal and the surface is not flattened.

Two application notes. For a ribbon that functions as a handle, a tie or a strap, ask for the abrasion result on the specific construction rather than accepting a general fibre rating, and test with the intended substrate — rubbing against a knitted cotton panel behaves differently from rubbing against a smooth coated surface. And for high-contact applications, an abrasion-resistant construction plus a reinforced woven edge is usually a cheaper answer than a heavier, more expensive yarn that still has a weak selvedge.

How to Write a Durability Specification

A durability specification is only enforceable if it names the method, the condition and the threshold. Vague language creates disputes, because the mill and the buyer will each assume a different interpretation and neither will be wrong on their own terms.

FieldWhat to StateWhy It Changes the Outcome
Test method and standardThe named method, including the instrument and the procedure variantDifferent methods produce different grades for the same material
Exposure levelDuration, cycle count, temperature, load, light doseConverts a property name into a measurable condition
Acceptance gradeThe numeric grade required, per propertyRemoves the supplier's discretion over what counts as acceptable
Article testedPlain, decorated, folded, or as-builtDecoration and geometry change the result materially
Sample provenanceProduction-representative sample from the quoted constructionA swatch from a different colour or trial run proves nothing
Which propertiesColour change, transfer, shrinkage, print adhesion, surfaceDetermines which failures the test can detect at all
Report formatNamed method, grade, exposure, date, sample referenceMakes the report auditable and comparable across orders
Retest triggerWhen a retest is required: new dye recipe, new decoration, changed raw materialPrevents a valid historical result being reused for a changed product

The retest trigger is the field that does the most work over a long programme. Without it, a test report from the first order tends to travel with the product indefinitely, and it will still be cited after the dye recipe has changed, after the decoration method has changed, and after the mill has changed a material supplier. Durability results belong to a specific construction at a specific moment, and the specification should say when that moment expires.

Testing Samples, First Production and Reorders

A sensible test programme has three stages, each answering a different question.

Stage one — pre-production sample

Confirm colour against the agreed standard and check hand feel, width, construction and decoration placement. This stage is about appearance and specification compliance, not durability, though it is the right moment to raise any concerns about how the ribbon will be used, because it is still cheap to change.

Stage two — durability test on the approved sample

Run the specified methods against the approved sample and use the results to confirm the specification before committing to bulk. If a result is marginal, this is the cheap moment to change the dye route, the fibre or the decoration method. A colour that fails the light fastness requirement on the sample is a specification problem, not a supplier problem, and the right response is to adjust the specification or the construction rather than to accept a marginal result and hope.

Stage three — first production lot

Retest on the actual first bulk lot, on production-representative samples with the final decoration applied. This is the stage that closes the gap between the sample and the product, and it is the stage most often skipped because the sample result is already in the file. That reasoning fails for predictable reasons: the bulk dye lot may have been produced in a different dyehouse run, the decoration may have been applied on a different machine with different curing, or the bulk may have been woven on a different loom with a different yarn lot. The first lot is where those differences show, and the cost of testing it is trivial compared with the cost of a shipment that fails.

For repeat orders, the rule should be written into the supply agreement rather than left to judgement: a retest is required when the dye recipe changes, when the decoration method or the design changes, when the base material or the mill changes, or after a specified period. Absent that rule, a good result quietly ages into irrelevance.

Documentation and Reporting

A report is only as good as what it lets a third party conclude. The elements that matter are the method name and standard, the exposure level used, the grade achieved, the tested article and its construction, the sample identification tying it to a specific order, the date, and who performed the test.

Where the durability claim matters to your business or appears on your own packaging, ask whether testing was performed at the mill, at an independent laboratory, or on a sample you supplied. These carry different weight. Mill testing is fast and inexpensive and is entirely appropriate for routine confirmation, particularly when the mill runs the test routinely as part of its own release procedure. Independent laboratory testing is worth the cost where the claim will be challenged, where it appears in your own quality documentation, or where the product is going into a regulated category. Customer-supplied sample testing is the most representative of all, because it removes the argument about which article was tested, and it is the right approach for a first order in a new construction.

Be alert to two warning signs. A report that states a grade without naming the method is unusable, because grades across methods are not comparable. And a report that is identical to the previous order's report apart from the date and the sample reference usually means nothing was retested — a legitimate result, once you know what you are looking at, but not the confirmation you were asking for.

Practical Durability Checklist

  • Write the durability specification against the application, not against a generic standard. Say what the trim will meet: light, wash, rubbing, sweat, handling.
  • Name the test method and the standard in the specification, not just the property name.
  • State the exposure level — cycle count, temperature, light dose, load — alongside the acceptance grade.
  • Ask for wet crocking as well as dry wherever the trim may meet moisture.
  • Ask for a perspiration test on any trim that sits against skin.
  • Test the decorated article, not the plain ribbon, when there is a printed, foiled or flocked element.
  • Test in the geometry the trim will use — folded, knotted or looped — not flat.
  • State the shrinkage tolerance for length and width, with the measurement method and the cycle at which it is measured.
  • Confirm the selvedge construction and, where fray matters, require a woven edge rather than a folded or stitched one.
  • Use a production-representative sample for testing, cut from the same construction, colour and decoration as the order.
  • Retest on the first production lot rather than carrying the sample result forward.
  • Write a retest trigger into the supply agreement so a valid result cannot be reused after the recipe, decoration or material changes.
  • Request the full test report with the method, grade, exposure, date and sample reference, not a summary line in a quotation.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is colour fastness in ribbon and why does it matter?

Colour fastness describes how well a ribbon resists the transfer of colour — fading from light, bleeding onto adjacent fabric, or losing colour through rubbing. It matters because decorative trim is usually the most visible element on a finished product, and because trim failure is not always visible at the point of manufacture. A ribbon that passes inspection and then bleeds onto a white shirt in the customer's laundry generates a complaint against your brand, not against the mill, and it does so in the consumer's home rather than in your warehouse. Fastness is therefore a brand-protection specification as much as a material property.

2. Which colour fastness test should I specify for ribbon trim?

It depends on what the ribbon actually experiences, and most buyers need more than one. For light exposure, the standard method is xenon arc or equivalent weathering assessment, which simulates daylight rather than a UV lamp alone. For rubbing, a crockmeter test measures transfer of colour to an adjacent white textile under specified dry and wet conditions. For washing, the relevant method is a domestic or industrial laundering procedure with a specified number of cycles. For wet use, a perspiration test measures colour transfer under acidic and alkaline perspiration, which is the condition that matters most for ribbon trimmed into apparel next to skin. Write the test method and the acceptance grade into the specification rather than naming a property in general terms.

3. What is the difference between wash fastness and colour fastness?

They are different questions that buyers routinely collapse into one. Colour fastness is the broad property of resisting colour transfer by any agency — light, rubbing, washing, perspiration. Wash durability is specifically about surviving repeated laundering without losing dimensional stability, colour, surface finish or print adhesion. A ribbon can hold its colour brilliantly under a rubbing test and still be a poor wash performer, because the dye is stable but the construction has shifted or the printed layer has cracked. If your trim goes into a laundered product, specify both: colour fastness for the colour, wash durability for the ribbon as a construction.

4. How do I read a colour fastness grading scale?

The familiar scale runs from grade 5 (excellent, negligible colour change) down to grade 1 (severe, substantial colour change). Grades 4 and above are generally treated as a commercially acceptable light fastness level for most decorative applications, and grade 3 is often acceptable where the product is not exposed to direct light. For wet crocking and perspiration, a grade of 3 or better is a common commercial threshold, but the right threshold depends on how visible the trim is and whether a failure would be visible to the consumer. The critical discipline is to state the number you require, not the word 'acceptable'. A supplier applying their own judgement about what is acceptable is a negotiation you have already lost.

5. Does decoration method affect ribbon wash durability?

Substantially, and buyers often discover this only after the first bulk wash failure. Woven-in patterns are the most durable because there is nothing to detach. Screen-printed and heat-transferred designs sit in between, and their durability depends on the binder system, the curing or drying conditions and how flexed the ribbon is. Foil stamping is the most fragile, because foil is a thin metallic layer with no elasticity and it cracks at fold lines. Velvet ribbon is vulnerable to pile crushing and matting, which is a different failure mode from colour loss. If your design involves a decorated element, test the decorated ribbon, not the plain ribbon, and test it folded as well as flat.

6. How much does abrasion resistance vary between ribbon constructions?

Substantially, and it follows the structure rather than the fibre. A tightly woven satin with a dense float on the face resists rubbing poorly at the float, because the long floats catch and abrade, but it feels smooth and looks premium. A ribbed grosgrain has more surface area and generally tolerates abrasion better in practice, because the ribs deflect contact. Jacquard and woven patterns have high points that take the wear and are often more durable than a smooth face of the same fibre. Woven-edge construction, where the selvedge is part of the weave rather than glued or stitched on, resists fraying at the edge far better. For trim that will be handled, rubbed against skin or carried as a handle, ask for the abrasion result on the construction you are buying rather than a general fibre rating.

7. How do I specify a wash test so that the result is meaningful?

A wash test is only comparable if the method, the cycle count and the conditions are stated. Specify the washing procedure, the number of cycles, the wash temperature, the detergent type and concentration, the drying method, and the evaluation grade required after the final cycle. Also state what is being assessed — colour change, shrinkage in length or width, print adhesion, surface change, or a combination. Many buyers specify 'washable' and then receive a test performed on an undecorated swatch, which tells them nothing about the product they are actually buying. Ask for the swatch used in the test and confirm it was cut from the same construction, colour and decoration as the production order.

8. What causes colour bleed or dye transfer in ribbon?

Three causes account for nearly all of it. The first is incomplete rinsing after dyeing, which leaves unbound dye on the fibre surface and is the most common cause of bleeding in the first wash. The second is the use of disperse dyes on polyester without adequate fixation or with insufficient heat-setting, which leaves dye mobile in the fibre. The third is direct contact with a wet surface that is more absorbent than the ribbon itself, such as a white cotton panel or a tissue liner, which can wick dye out of the ribbon even when the dye is properly fixed. Of these, the second and third are properties of the finished product that you can only detect by testing the finished ribbon, which is again why testing the production article matters more than testing the yarn.

9. Should I test samples before placing a bulk order?

Yes, and the sample you test should be a production-representative one rather than a swatch cut from a different colour or an undecorated trial. The useful sequence is: approve a pre-production sample for colour and hand feel; request a durability test on that sample against the methods you will specify; then commission the tests again on the first production lot. The second test is not optional for a first order in a new construction or a new dye recipe. It costs far less than the cost of a shipment of trim that fails in the customer's laundry, and it is the only way to close the gap between what a mill believes about its own process and what the finished ribbon actually does.

10. What documentation should a ribbon supplier provide on durability?

Ask for the test report itself, not a summary line in a quotation. A usable report names the test method and standard, the exposure level used, the grade achieved, the laboratory or the mill's own testing facility, the date, and the sample identification tying it to your order. Be suspicious of reports that list a grade without naming the method, and be more suspicious of a report that is identical to the previous order's report apart from the date — that usually means the material was not actually retested. Where the claim matters to your business, ask whether the test was performed at the mill, at an independent laboratory, or on a customer-supplied sample, because those three things carry very different weight in an audit.
