Why the Forecast Is the Hardest Part of a Ribbon Programme

Ribbon is a small line item in a packaging budget and it is one of the hardest to forecast. A carton is either right or wrong. A label is either right or wrong. A ribbon, by contrast, sits somewhere on a continuum: the colour is right, the width is right, the hand is right, the shade is a little warm, the edge finish is a little crisp for a soft gift, and the consumer never quite knows why the gift looks slightly off. That means a ribbon forecast error does not show up as a rejected shipment. It shows up as a slow drain on sell-through, and the feedback reaches the supplier eight to twelve months later, when it is far too late to do anything about it.

That timing gap is the whole problem. A forecast error in February is discovered in November. A forecast error in May is discovered at the Chinese New Year production window, when the loom slots for the following holiday season have already been allocated. So the question is not really "how do I forecast better." It is "how do I make a forecast that is good enough to book mill capacity, and what do I do about the parts I know I will get wrong".

This guide covers the forecasting method we see working for brand buyers, the five variables that actually drive ribbon demand, the booking windows that matter, and the inventory arithmetic that decides whether a peak-season programme is profitable.

The Five Variables That Drive Ribbon Demand

Most forecasting models for ribbon fail because they model the wrong thing. They model units shipped and apply a growth rate. Ribbon demand is not a function of units shipped. It is a function of five specific variables, and if you can get a handle on each of them you can build a forecast that a mill can actually plan against.

The Base Forecast: A Method You Can Actually Run

You do not need a demand-planning system for this. You need a spreadsheet with five tabs and a discipline of updating it monthly. Here is the method in the order it should be run.

Step 1 — Build the base from actuals, not from a growth assumption

Start with what you shipped last year, in metres, per ribbon SKU. Not per unit, not per line. If you have three years of history, use three years, because a single year is nearly always distorted by something: a pandemic year, a supply-disrupted year, a year you entered a new retail account. Use the three-year median per month, not the mean, and then apply your adjustments. A median is robust to the one weird year; a mean is not.

Step 2 — Layer the known events on top

Build the forecast from the actual base plus an explicit list of changes, each with a number and a reason. This is what separates a forecast you can defend to a mill from a number someone guessed. The list should include:

  • New accounts. Not "we won Target" but "Target, 40,000 units/month from March, 1 ribbon SKU, 500 mm per unit."
  • Lost accounts. The reverse, and just as easy to forget as new ones.
  • Line changes. A shift from 300 mm to 700 mm ribbon on an existing SKU changes volume by 130% without changing unit volume at all. This is the single most common source of forecast error and it is invisible unless you audit the spec.
  • Seasonality shape. Not a flat 12 months. A gift programme might be 8% of annual volume in February and 22% in November.
  • Programme wins and losses. A tender you expect to win, a private label programme you are replacing.

Step 3 — Stress the result before you show it to anyone

Run three versions: a low case, a base case and a high case. The high case should be roughly 25-35% above the base for a seasonal programme and 10-15% for a stable one. The low case should be the base minus the growth you have not yet contracted. If the difference between your low and high case is small, you have probably been too conservative in the base, and the mill will price the slot on the low number. If it is enormous, you probably have a demand-plan problem rather than a forecasting problem.

The Booking Calendar: When to Place Capacity, When to Place Orders

This is where most programmes go wrong, and the failure is always the same shape: the buyer waits until they need the goods to book the capacity, and by then the capacity is gone.

There are two distinct commitments, and confusing them is expensive.

CommitmentWhat it reservesWhen to do itCost of getting it wrong
Capacity bookingLoom slots, dye-house capacity, printing slots for a named window and a named volume 120-180 days ahead of the delivery date for a peak-season programme; 60-90 days for a non-seasonal one Slot is gone. You are placed behind other buyers in the queue and your delivery date moves by three to six weeks with no recourse.
Purchase orderActual metres, actual price, actual delivery 30-60 days ahead of the production date, once the forecast is firmed You are committed to metres you may not need, at a price you cannot change.

The Chinese New Year shutdown is the hard constraint in this calendar and it is routinely missed. The factories close for two to three weeks around the lunar new year, which in the 2026-2027 cycle falls in early February. Two consequences follow. First, anything you want produced in January must be on the floor before the shutdown, which means the purchase order needs to be placed in November or December. Second, the weeks immediately after the shutdown are congested, because every buyer who paused comes back at once. For an October delivery, that means your mill-side production should be finished and shipped by late September, with the goods in transit during the shutdown week rather than sitting on a dock waiting for the factory to reopen.

The seasonal booking window, in plain terms

  • January to February: the year-ahead planning window. Capacity for the Q4 peak should already be booked by now if it is not already spoken for.
  • March to April: place capacity bookings for the following year's first half, and firm up the colour and width mix for the coming peak. This is when the mix errors get caught.
  • May to June: place purchase orders for peak delivery. Custom-dyed ribbon needs a lab dip and an approval cycle, so a custom colour for an October delivery has to be sampled by June or it will not make it.
  • July to August: peak production. Your reorders for the mid-season restock land here, and this is the window where a stock-out hurts most.
  • September to October: shipping and replenishment. Anything that needs a second production run for a Christmas replenishment has to be ordered by September, not November.

The Inventory Arithmetic That Decides Whether Peak Pays

Peak season is where ribbon programmes make or lose money, and the arithmetic is unforgiving in a specific way. Let us work through a realistic example.

Suppose a gift programme sells 250,000 ribbon-bearing units across the peak, at 700 mm of ribbon per unit, which is 175,000 metres of annual demand concentrated into the October-to-December window. A buyer who plans for the peak usually assumes peak consumption runs at roughly 2.5 times the baseline monthly rate, so a November run rate of about 55,000 metres is plausible.

Now the cost structure. At a typical custom-dyed polyester satin landed cost in the USD 0.14-0.22 per metre range, 175,000 metres of ribbon is a meaningful number, and the working capital question is: how many weeks of peak cover do you hold, and where does it sit? Ribbon made for October that sits in a warehouse until January has four months of holding cost, and it has also lost its place on the shelf in a retailer who ran out in November and reordered from someone else. The sell-in is not the sell-out.

Forecasting for Repeat Programmes: The Reorder Point

Once a programme is running, most of the forecasting work becomes reorder management. The reorder point is a simple calculation, but most buyers set it by instinct and get it wrong in the same direction: too late.

Reorder point = average daily consumption during the peak period × (reorder lead time in days + safety stock days).

Three things make this calculation unreliable in practice, and each needs an explicit fix.

Problem one: the lead time is longer in peak than in normal times

A reorder placed in October does not move at October speed. The mill's own capacity is fully committed, freight is congested, and the goods you ordered in September may be sitting behind another buyer's order. Model a peak reorder lead time of 8-12 weeks even if your normal lead time is 4-6 weeks. If you model the normal lead time, your reorder point is roughly a third too low and you will stock out in the exact weeks that matter most.

Problem two: consumption is not smooth inside the peak

November is not the same as October. A gift programme commonly runs 60% of its peak consumption in a four-week window around Black Friday and the holiday gifting period, not across the whole quarter. Calculate the reorder point on your actual weekly consumption during the highest three weeks of the previous year, not on a monthly average.

Problem three: the reorder does not arrive because of a specification or compliance issue

A reorder that needs a new lab dip, a new print plate or a compliance document for a new destination market is not a reorder. It is a new order with a new lead time. Any SKU with an open compliance or specification dependency needs a longer modelled lead time than the standard one, and it should be on a watch list rather than an automatic reorder point.

What to Ask a Supplier About Capacity

A good mill will engage with your forecast and tell you something useful about it. A mill that takes a number and gives you a price is running a spot market, not a partnership. These are the questions that separate the two.

  1. What is your current loom and dye-house load for the window I need, as a percentage? A mill that answers "we are at 80% in October" is managing capacity. A mill that says "we are fully flexible" is either lying or not running looms.
  2. What is the slot-booking deadline for an October delivery? Every mill has one. Some are in May, some in July. You need the date, not a vibe.
  3. What happens to my capacity if my volume comes in 20% under forecast? A credible answer is that the slots are released and the difference is charged as a non-production fee, or converted to credit. An answer of "we will keep it for you" usually means the capacity was never genuinely reserved.
  4. Can you hold yarn or greige for my palette? Holding the yarn is worth more than holding the finished ribbon, because yarn can still be dyed into any colour in the palette. If the mill can hold your greige, your colour flexibility survives a forecast miss.
  5. What is the minimum lot you will run on a repeat order, and how long does the setup take? This determines how small a corrective reorder can be, which determines how much buffer you need to carry.
  6. What is your capacity for a mid-season top-up in week 44? This is the question most buyers forget to ask and the one that saves the season. If the answer is "we can only do a full run in November", you need a larger buffer. If the answer is "we can run 5,000 metres of your existing colourway in five days", you can carry almost no buffer at all.

Common Forecasting Mistakes

Mistake one: forecasting in units and converting later

Units are not metres, and the conversion is not constant. A programme that adds a ribbon to an existing pack in one market and replaces a paper band with a ribbon in another has two different metres-per-unit figures for the same unit count. Build the forecast in metres from the start.

Mistake two: treating last year's forecast as last year's actual

Most buyers keep the forecast they sent and the actual they received. The gap between them, per SKU, per month, is the single most valuable dataset you own and almost nobody analyses it. If your forecast has been 30% high for six consecutive quarters, your base is wrong and no amount of analysis on top of it will fix it.

Mistake three: booking capacity with a number you cannot defend

Booking a slot against a forecast you built in an afternoon produces a slot priced for a number the mill does not believe. Good mills will simply decline to reserve on that basis, or reserve at a different rate. If you want a reserved slot, bring a forecast with a base case, a high case, the mix, and the assumption list behind it.

Mistake four: ignoring the colour mix in the capacity number

Capacity is not measured in metres, it is measured in setup cycles. A mill running 4 colours of the same construction in one campaign has one setup; the same metres in 20 colours has twenty. When you book capacity, book it against the colour and width mix, and where the mix is uncertain, book the worst realistic case and pay for the difference if it improves.

Mistake five: forecasting the peak but not the shoulder

The four to six weeks on either side of the peak are where a programme usually runs out. Sales are strong for a reason that ends, the reorder lands on a congested calendar, and the second shipment arrives in January. Build a shoulder requirement into the forecast and into the booking, or carry the buffer deliberately and price it.

Building a Programme That Predicts Well

The programmes that forecast well have three properties, and none of them are sophisticated.

They forecast in metres, by colour and width, per delivery window. Not units, not value, not "roughly the same as last year". A forecast that a mill can plan against is the only kind worth making.

They carry a documented history of forecast versus actual. Not in a spreadsheet nobody opens, but as a monthly artefact that goes to the supplier with the forecast. This is how the relationship becomes a partnership rather than a transaction, and it is also how you find out that your last three reorders were late because your reorder point was wrong.

They buy the slot before they buy the metres, and they know the difference. Capacity booking is a low-cost, low-regret commitment. Purchase orders are a high-cost, high-regret commitment. Mixing them up is how buyers end up with 400,000 metres of custom-dyed ribbon in a warehouse in January, or with an empty slot in October.

For brand buyers running a seasonal ribbon programme, the practical first step is a one-page forecast sheet in the format above, with the base case, the high case, the mix and the assumption list, sent to your mill at the start of the booking window. A mill that receives one of these and does not engage with it is telling you something important about what will happen in October.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand buyers, procurement managers and supply-chain planners most often ask us about forecasting ribbon demand and booking peak-season capacity, from the Chinese New Year production window to reorder-point discipline and colour-mix accuracy. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How far in advance should I book ribbon production capacity for peak season?

For a peak-season delivery, book capacity 120 to 180 days ahead of the delivery date, and place the purchase order 30 to 60 days ahead of production once the forecast is firmed. The binding constraint is the Chinese New Year shutdown, which typically closes factories for two to three weeks in late January or early February. Anything needed in January has to be on the floor before the shutdown, which means the purchase order must be placed in November or December the year before.

2. What is the minimum forecast accuracy a brand buyer should expect?

A well-run programme should hold forecast error within about 10% on a rolling twelve-month basis, and within 15% at the SKU-and-month level once mix effects are stripped out. The most useful practice is to keep a monthly record of forecast versus actual per SKU, because a buyer whose forecast has been consistently 30% high for several quarters has a base-forecast problem that no additional analysis on top will fix.

3. Should I forecast in units or in metres?

In metres, broken down by colour, width and delivery window. Units are not convertible to metres at a constant rate, because programmes add ribbon to some packs and replace paper bands with ribbon in others, and the metres per unit differ by SKU. A forecast expressed in units and colour, with the mix attached, is the only form a mill can actually plan loom slots and dye campaigns against.

4. How much safety stock should I carry for a seasonal ribbon programme?

Enough to cover the gap between your model lead time and your real peak lead time, which is typically 8 to 12 weeks of peak consumption rather than the 4 to 6 weeks of normal lead time. If your mill can run a 5,000 metre top-up of an existing colourway within five days, you can carry almost no buffer. If it can only run a full production cycle, you need a materially larger buffer and you should price it into the programme.

5. What is the difference between booking capacity and placing a purchase order?

Capacity booking reserves loom slots, dye-house capacity and printing slots for a named window and a named volume, without committing to the metres. A purchase order commits to actual metres, at a fixed price, for a confirmed delivery. Capacity booking is a low-cost, low-regret commitment; a purchase order is a high-cost, high-regret one. Confusing the two is the most common way programmes end up with either a warehouse full of custom-dyed ribbon in January or an empty slot in October.

6. How do I handle a forecast that comes in under plan?

Ask your supplier what happens to reserved capacity if volume comes in 20% under forecast. A credible answer is that the slots are released and the difference is charged as a non-production fee or converted to credit. If the answer is that the capacity will simply be held for you, that usually means the capacity was never genuinely reserved and you should assume your slot is not secure.

7. Should I order stock colours or custom colours for a peak season programme?

Stock colour buys you speed and working capital flexibility, since it moves in weeks rather than months, but it cannot be reordered into your exact Pantone later in the season and it carries a shade risk on a branded gift line. Custom colour protects brand consistency but ties up the programme in a longer lead time and a lab-dip approval cycle. For most branded gift programmes, the pattern that works is custom colour for the core and best-selling shades, with stock colour for secondary and replenishment colours that carry less brand risk.

8. What questions should I ask a ribbon supplier about their capacity?

Ask six things: what percentage of loom and dye-house capacity is committed in the window you need; what the slot-booking deadline is for your delivery date; what happens to your capacity if volume comes in under forecast; whether they can hold yarn or greige for your palette; what the minimum repeat lot is and how long the setup takes; and whether they can run a mid-season top-up in a specific week. The last question is the one most buyers forget and the one that most reliably saves a season.

9. How accurate should a buyer's consumption profile be inside the peak?

It should be based on the highest three to four weeks of the previous year, not on a monthly average. A gift programme commonly runs around 60% of its peak consumption in a four-week window around Black Friday and the holiday gifting period. Calculating your reorder point on a monthly average understates consumption in the weeks that matter most, which is exactly when a stock-out costs the most.

10. Why does holding yarn or greige matter more than holding finished ribbon?

Yarn can still be dyed into any colour within your approved palette. Finished ribbon can only be sold as the colour it was dyed. If your mill can hold greige against your forecast and the forecast comes in light, you retain the ability to re-dye within the palette and recover the volume. If you are holding finished custom ribbon and the forecast moves against you, that metre is fixed to a single shade.
