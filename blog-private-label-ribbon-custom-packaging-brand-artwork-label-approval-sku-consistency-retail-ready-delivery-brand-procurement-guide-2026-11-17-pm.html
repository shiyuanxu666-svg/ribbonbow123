Private Label Ribbon and Custom Packaging - From Brand Artwork to Retail-Ready Trim (2026)

Published: November 17, 2026  |  By Smith Ribbon  |  B2B Private Label and Packaging

A private label programme is not one order. It is a system that has to produce the same branded trim twelve months later, on a different production run, possibly at a different mill line, in a slightly different batch of yarn. The visible output is a small ribbon with a logo on it. The actual work is in the parts nobody sees: the artwork file that was actually produced from rather than the one approved, the label format that survives the distribution centre, and the documentation that tells you which physical roll corresponds to which purchase order. Get the system right and the brand looks consistent at scale. Get it wrong and the same product arrives in four shades of the same colour across four deliveries, which is exactly what a customer notices.

The word "private label" covers a wide range of arrangements in decorative trim. At one end it is a stock ribbon in a supplier's own packaging with a retailer's sticker on it. At the other end it is a fully bespoke programme: a custom weave, a custom print, a bespoke label, a custom carton, and a documented supply chain behind it. Both get called private label, and both are quoted and discussed using the same vocabulary, which is where a lot of confusion starts.

This guide follows the path of a private label programme from the brand's artwork file to the delivery of retail-ready, branded trim — and focuses on the part that most programmes get wrong: making the second, third and twelfth order match the first.

What "private label" actually covers

It is worth separating the programme into its layers, because each layer carries a different cost, a different lead time and a different failure mode.

Most private label programmes are strong at the top of this table — the product — and weak at the bottom. The product is what gets negotiated. The documentation is what makes the programme repeatable, and it is the part most often treated as administrative overhead worth avoiding.

The artwork file is the specification

For a printed or decorated trim, the artwork file is effectively the production specification. It determines the colour separations, the print area, the repeat length, the registration tolerance and how much trim allowance is needed at each end. A logo that looks right on a laptop screen can be entirely impractical to print on a 10 mm ribbon.

Resolution and the practical minimum

Print processes in this industry generally want vector artwork for line work and logos, and high-resolution raster at a defined effective resolution for photographic elements. The detail that causes real problems is not resolution but size. A logo 25 mm wide cannot hold fine detail, and the attempt to reproduce it at print quality produces a muddy result that the brand team will reject on the sample — after the artwork has been proofed, approved and costed.

The right sequence is to send the artwork early and let the supplier check it against the production width before anything else happens. This costs nothing and removes the most common cause of a wasted sample round.

Colour in artwork versus colour on the ribbon

This is worth stating plainly because it causes recurring disappointment. A Pantone or process colour reference on a screen is a specification of ink, and ribbon is dyed yarn, not printed ink. A logo specified as a precise Pantone will be dyed to a match that is approximately correct, and whether that match is acceptable depends on how large and how prominent the element is.

Two practical consequences follow. First, for large solid areas of brand colour, the sensible expectation is "a close match to the reference, assessed against an agreed physical standard", not an exact match to a swatch. Second, the way to avoid a dispute is to approve a physical reference — a dyed ribbon swatch, signed off — before production, and to keep it as the standard against which subsequent orders are judged.

Approve a physical standard, not a screen reference. A signed swatch becomes the reference for every future order. A Pantone chip on a monitor cannot be a reference for a dyed yarn, and trying to use one as such is where colour disputes begin.

Repeat length and where the pattern starts

If the design repeats along the ribbon, the repeat length matters practically: it determines how much material is wasted between repeats, and where the pattern begins relative to the cut end. For a ribbon applied to a gift box, where the bow is positioned by eye, a repeat that puts the logo at an awkward point on every second box is a genuine quality issue rather than a cosmetic one. This is worth specifying explicitly, and worth checking on the sample against the actual application.

Label approval: the gate that prevents everything downstream

The single most valuable control in a private label programme is a written label approval that must be signed before production begins. Not a verbal confirmation, not an approval of a low-resolution preview, and not an approval of the artwork file itself.

What the approval should actually contain

A useful approval record states the following, and the supplier supplies them for sign-off before the first production run:

  • The physical or high-fidelity proof — the actual labelled trim, not a mock-up and not the artwork.
  • The label dimensions in millimetres, measured from the approved proof rather than from the design file.
  • The artwork file reference — filename, version and date, so the file that was approved is identifiable later.
  • The colour reference, expressed as the agreed physical standard and its approval date.
  • The position and dimensions of the label relative to the reel or spool format, and the position of the first and last label on the roll.
  • The approval status, with a signature and a date.

Each of these fields prevents a distinct failure. Label dimensions prevent a packaging mismatch downstream. The file reference prevents the approved artwork being replaced by a newer, subtly different version. The colour standard prevents a dispute about whether the shade drifted. The position specification prevents a roll that starts mid-label.

Sample rounds and why they are not wasted time

Most private label programmes run one to three rounds of samples before production. That is normal, not a sign of a problem, and the rounds are cheaper than they look. A sample round costs a small quantity of material and a few days; the same error found after a full production run costs a container, a freight booking and a delivery window.

The rounds work best when each one is bounded and documented. Round one establishes the production appearance from the approved artwork. Round two resolves whatever the first one raised. Anything beyond that usually indicates the specification itself is still moving, and is worth addressing as a specification problem rather than a sample problem.

Custom packaging formats and the cost they carry

Packaging is where private label programmes most often drift, and the drift usually happens for a good operational reason that is never written back into the specification. A shipment does not fit the pallet. A carton is damaged in transit. A label falls off. The fix is made quickly on the floor, and three orders later the programme is running on a packaging format nobody approved.

Two questions decide which format is right. First, where does the customer encounter the packaging — on a retail shelf, in a photograph, or only inside a warehouse? Second, what does the packaging have to achieve operationally — fit a specific pallet, pass a specific in-store test, or carry a specific barcode format? A format should be chosen against those two questions rather than against an assumed look.

Lock the format in writing

Whatever is chosen should be recorded with the same rigour as the label approval: carton dimensions, material, print specification, number of units per carton, gross and net weight, and the barcode or labelling standard. A packaging format that is not written down cannot be held consistent, and it will be quietly changed the first time it causes an operational problem.

Any change to an approved packaging format should require a re-approval, exactly like an artwork change. The rule is simple to state and is the single most effective guard against a programme quietly drifting away from what was agreed.

Brand consistency across a range of SKUs

A private label programme usually involves several products in one brand: a 10 mm satin in the brand colour, a 25 mm grosgrain in the accent, a wired edge in a third finish. The customer experiences these as one brand, and will notice if one of them is visibly off.

Colour consistency within a family

Matching a colour across different materials is harder than it sounds. The same dye recipe applied to satin, to grosgrain and to a velvet pile will not land on an identical value, because the yarns take up dye differently and the surface scatters light differently. This is physics rather than manufacturing failure.

The practical approach is to accept a tolerance and specify it. For a family of trim in one brand, defining the acceptable difference between adjacent SKUs as a stated tolerance — in laboratory terms or, more practically, as an agreed visual judgement against a signed physical reference — resolves this before it becomes a complaint. Without a tolerance, the standard becomes "identical", which is not achievable across materials and which will therefore eventually be declared a failure that nobody could have prevented.

Consistency of presentation

Where the brand appears — on a label, a header card, a carton — the specification should apply across every format it appears on, not just one. The dimensions of the logo, the clearance around it, the weight of the type and the position of the mark should be consistent across all formats, even where the formats themselves differ in size. This is basic brand management, but it is applied to trim packaging less often than to print and digital, and the inconsistency is visible when a set is assembled from mixed pieces.

Traceability: linking the physical roll to the order

This is the part that makes a programme repeatable, and it is the part most often left undocumented. The mechanism is simple: at the point of production, the finished roll or carton is identified with a production reference, and that reference is linked to the purchase order, the approved artwork version and the approved label version.

What that reference buys is practical. When a customer reports a problem six months later, you can establish which production run it came from, what the approved specification was at that time, and which batch of yarn it used — and therefore whether the complaint is a production variation, a specification misunderstanding, or a genuine defect. Without it, the only available response is to argue from memory, which is a losing position in a supplier relationship.

It also makes reordering genuinely repeatable. A repeat order that quotes "as before" is ambiguous — before which version of the artwork, at which colour tolerance, with which packaging format? A programme with a production reference can restate the specification precisely, and a repeat order becomes a routine administrative act rather than a fresh negotiation.

Keep a production reference against every roll. It costs nothing at the point of production and it is the difference between resolving a customer complaint and arguing about one.

Designing for the repeat order

The test of a private label programme is not the first delivery. It is the fourth. Several things should be built into the programme from the beginning with that in mind.

A written specification that can be quoted against

The specification should be a document, not a conversation. It should state the material, width, construction, finish, colour reference, decoration method and artwork version, label specification, packaging format, acceptable tolerances, defect allowance and applicable certification scope. When the next order is placed, that document is the reference, and any difference between it and the new request is a deliberate change rather than an accidental drift.

An archive that survives

Artwork files, proofs, approval records, test reports and colour references should be kept for the life of the programme, not the life of the project. Most programmes lose their history not through deletion but through nobody knowing where it was stored. A single folder per programme, in a location that a person who did not start the programme can find, is sufficient.

A defined change process

Changes should be deliberate and recorded: who approves them, what has to be re-sampled, and whether the change re-opens the label approval. Not every change needs a full sample round, and not every change can skip one. Deciding in advance which is which prevents both unnecessary cost and unnecessary risk.

Six private label mistakes that recur

  1. Approving the artwork file instead of the proof. The file says what you intend; the proof shows what comes off the machine. Approve the proof.
  2. Letting packaging change without re-approval. This is the single most common source of programme drift, and it always happens for a good operational reason.
  3. Expecting exact colour matches across different materials. Specify a tolerance and a physical reference instead, so the standard is achievable and objective.
  4. Running the programme without a production reference. The cost of establishing traceability is trivial; the cost of not having it during a dispute is not.
  5. Not documenting the specification well enough to quote against. If the repeat order has to be renegotiated from scratch, the programme is not a programme.
  6. Treating the sample round as the final approval. Approve the labelled production sample, in the approved packaging, ideally assembled into the finished application — because that is the only way to see the label, the trim and the packaging together.

A workable sequence for a new programme

For a brand entering private label trim for the first time, the following sequence is a sound starting point and avoids most of the rework described above.

  1. Define the product specification in writing — material, width, finish, colour, decoration, tolerances, certification scope. Nothing downstream is meaningful until this exists.
  2. Send artwork for a feasibility check before anything is proofed. Confirm dimensions, separation and the colour approach against the production width.
  3. Agree the colour reference physically — a dyed swatch, not a screen reference — and keep it as the programme standard.
  4. Choose the packaging format against operational questions, not appearance alone, and record it fully.
  5. Produce a labelled sample in the approved packaging and view it in the finished application before sign-off.
  6. Complete a written label approval covering artwork version, dimensions, colour standard and position, signed and dated.
  7. Run the first production order against a retained sample, and retain the production reference for that run.
  8. Write the specification up as a live document at this point, while the details are fresh, and use it as the basis for every subsequent order.

Steps one and eight are the ones that get skipped, and they are the two that make the difference between a private label programme and a series of unrelated orders that happen to carry the same logo.

The practical summary

Approve the proof, not the file. Everything that goes wrong downstream starts from a specification that was approved in the wrong form.

Treat packaging changes like artwork changes. If it would need a re-approval if it were the label, it needs a re-approval if it is the carton.

Agree tolerances and physical references before production. A signed swatch and a stated tolerance turn subjective disputes into arithmetic.

Keep a production reference against every roll. It is the mechanism that makes the repeat order match the first order, and it is the mechanism that resolves a complaint.

Write the specification up as a living document. The programme that survives is the one that can be quoted against without a conversation.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the minimum artwork I need for custom printed ribbon?

Vector artwork for logos and line work, and high-resolution raster for photographic elements. More important than the file format is the size the artwork will appear at on the ribbon. A logo 25mm wide cannot hold fine detail, so send the artwork for a feasibility check against the production width before proofing. This costs nothing and removes the most common cause of a rejected sample.

2. How many sample rounds should I expect before production?

One to three rounds is normal for a new private label programme and does not indicate a problem. Round one establishes the production appearance, round two resolves whatever the first raised. Beyond that, the specification itself is probably still moving, and that is worth treating as a specification problem rather than a sample problem. Each round costs a small quantity of material and a few days, which is far cheaper than discovering the same issue after a full production run.

3. Can I get an exact Pantone colour match on dyed ribbon?

Not as an ink match, because ribbon is dyed yarn rather than printed ink and will not reproduce a Pantone chip exactly. The workable approach is to approve a physical dyed swatch as the programme standard and agree a tolerance for variation between production runs and between different materials in the same family. A signed physical reference and a stated tolerance turn a subjective argument into something measurable.

4. How should custom packaging be specified for a retail programme?

Record the carton or sleeve dimensions, material, print specification, units per carton, gross and net weight, and the barcode standard, and require the same written approval process you use for the label. Packaging formats drift because operational problems get solved on the floor without a re-approval. Making packaging changes subject to the same approval rule as artwork is the most effective guard against a programme quietly moving away from what was agreed.

5. What does a private label ribbon programme need to be repeatable?

Three things: a written specification that can be quoted against, an archive of the approved artwork, proof, approval record and colour references, and a production reference linked to each finished roll. The production reference is what lets you restate a specification precisely on a repeat order and resolve a customer complaint months later. It costs nothing at the point of production and is difficult to reconstruct afterwards.

6. How do I keep brand colour consistent across several different trim products?

Matching a colour across satin, grosgrain and velvet is harder than it looks, because the yarns take up dye differently and the surfaces scatter light differently. This is physics rather than a manufacturing failure. Define an acceptable tolerance between adjacent SKUs, express it either in laboratory terms or as an agreed visual judgement against a signed physical reference, and judge production against that. Without a tolerance the standard becomes identical, which is unachievable across materials and will eventually be declared a failure nobody could have prevented.

7. Should the private label sample be approved in its final packaging?

Yes, and ideally assembled into the finished application. A label viewed flat against a production sample does not show how the trim and the packaging read together, how the label sits on the reel, or how the proportions look once the set is assembled. This is the only way to see the complete result before committing a production run, and it is where a surprising share of last-minute changes originate.
