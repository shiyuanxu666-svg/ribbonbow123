Why the quoted price is not the price

Almost every sourcing problem that reaches us as a factory was originally a costing problem that nobody noticed. A buyer compares two quotes, picks the cheaper one, and discovers eighteen months later that the "cheaper" supplier was cheap on the line item that appears on the quotation and expensive on everything that does not. The landed cost was higher, the quality was worse, the lead time was less reliable, and the second order never happened because the first one did not work commercially.

This is not a supplier problem. It is a comparison problem, and it has a specific cause: the two quotes are not on the same basis, and the buyer has no way of knowing that from the face of the documents.

Here is what a ribbon quotation typically contains. A unit price, sometimes a total, an Incoterm, a lead time, and a payment structure. What it usually does not contain is any indication of which of those numbers changed to make the quote look competitive, or of the costs that will arrive later and are attributed to someone else.

There are three things a buyer can do about this, and they are not difficult. Build a model that captures the real cost structure. Use the same model on every quote so the comparison is genuine. And be honest about which costs are the supplier's, which are yours, and which are nobody's until they land.

This guide is a practical version of that model for custom ribbon: the cost lines, the eight costs that most often hide, the working capital arithmetic that procurement teams routinely forget, and how to run a comparison that survives being questioned by finance.

The three numbers you actually manage by

Before the line items, it helps to be clear about what the modelling is for. There are three numbers a ribbon buyer is really managing, and they are not the same number, and conflating them is why so much sourcing goes wrong.

1. The unit price. What the factory charges per metre or per piece, at a stated quantity and specification. This is what appears on the quotation and it is the number most conversations are about. It is also the number you have the least direct control over, because it is a function of material, construction, decoration, volume and the supplier's own efficiency.

2. The landed cost. The unit price plus everything required to move the goods from the factory to your warehouse, plus duty, plus the finance. This is what the goods actually cost you to own, and it is the number your gross margin is actually calculated from. It is frequently 20 to 40 percent above the quoted price on custom ribbon, depending on the volume and the destination.

3. The total cost of ownership. Landed cost plus the quality costs, the working capital, the inventory carrying, the obsolescence, and the internal cost of running the programme. This is the number that determines whether a private label ribbon line is a business or a hobby, and it is the number almost nobody builds.

The practical point: negotiate on the unit price, but decide on the total cost of ownership. A 6 percent saving on the unit price that comes with a double defect rate, a 40-day lead time and a 30 percent deposit is not a saving. It is a reallocation of cost into places your financial model does not look.

Building the model: twelve cost lines

Here is the structure we use when a buyer asks us to help them build a comparison model for custom ribbon. Twelve lines, and the point of the structure is that every supplier's quote can be mapped onto it, so the comparison is like-for-like by construction rather than by assumption.

  1. Raw material. Yarn or film, by type, and the consumption rate. Polyester satin, grosgrain, velvet, organza, wired and edged ribbon each have different input economics.
  2. Construction and finishing. Weaving, dyeing, sizing, calendering, mercerising, edge treatment. Some of these are in the material price and some are separate — ask which.
  3. Decoration. Screen print, hot foil, heat transfer, jacquard weaving, embroidery, edge printing. Charged by setup plus by metre, or by area.
  4. Setup and tooling. Screens, plates, dies, colour development, moulds. One-off, amortised over programme volume.
  5. Conversion labour. The cost of turning material into finished ribbon: winding, cutting, folding, tying, trimming, kitting.
  6. Packing. Spools, reels, cartons, protective wrapping, and for bows and trims the labour of assembly and set.
  7. Quality cost. Inspection, testing, and the defect and rework rate.
  8. Inland and export handling. Factory to port, terminal and documentation charges.
  9. Ocean freight and insurance. Volume-dependent, and highly sensitive to the container type and the season.
  10. Duty and tariffs. Classified by HS code, which for ribbon depends on construction and material.
  11. Customs, port and inland delivery. Brokerage, port handling, drayage to your warehouse.
  12. Working capital and finance. The cost of the money between payment and cash recovery, plus the cost of carrying inventory.

Three observations about this structure that are worth more than the list itself.

Lines 1 to 7 are the factory's cost; lines 8 to 12 are largely yours. A quote normally only addresses the first seven. The comparison happens because you add the same five lines to every supplier's quote using the same assumptions, which is the only way to make the comparison real.

Some of your lines are estimates, not quotes. You will not know your broker's fee or your drayage cost until you have shipped. Build the model with your best current estimate, mark the uncertain cells, and revise after the first shipment. A model with five uncertain inputs is still enormously more useful than a gut feel, and it improves every time you reorder.

The model is a document, not a spreadsheet. If the assumptions live only in one person's spreadsheet, they will not survive that person leaving, and they will not survive finance asking why this supplier is cheaper. Write down the assumptions in plain language alongside the numbers.

Line one: material and construction

Material is usually the largest single component of a custom ribbon cost, and it is also where comparison is most often done carelessly, because "satin" describes a wide range of products with meaningfully different economics.

Ask what the material is, precisely. Satin ribbon is typically a polyester satin with a specific denier, a specific warp and weft count and a specific lamination or calendering on the reverse to prevent fraying. Different suppliers put different quality into those specifications while describing the finished article with the same two words. A quote for "polyester satin, 15 mm, 100% polyester" is not a specification. What you need is enough to compare: base material and fibre type, width, construction, finish, weight or gsm, and the colour standard.

Understand the difference between dyed and printed. A solid colour woven from solution-dyed yarn behaves differently from a white ribbon that has been piece-dyed, in terms of colour consistency between lots, cost, and how it looks. If you need a colour to match across reorder cycles, that distinction is material to the conversation.

Ask about the width tolerance and what happens outside it. Ribbon width tolerance of a millimetre or two is normal. What matters commercially is whether an out-of-tolerance roll is a credit event, a rework event or a rejection event, and whether the tolerance is measured and recorded per roll. A supplier who measures and records can tell you the distribution. A supplier who does not is asking you to discover the variation in your own warehouse.

Consumption rate matters for cost per unit, not per metre. For bows, flowers, tassels and other assembled trims, the number of metres of ribbon consumed per finished piece is the conversion that turns a per-metre price into a per-piece price. Two suppliers quoting the same construction in metres may be quoting completely different per-piece economics if their consumption rates differ — and consumption rate is affected by cutting layout and yield, which is an operational question a good factory will have an opinion about.

Line two: decoration and finishing

Decoration is where quote comparisons most frequently break down, because the same visual effect can be produced by materially different processes at materially different prices.

Print method drives both cost and capability. Screen printing is cheapest per metre at volume and limited by the number of colours, with each colour adding a screen and a pass. Hot foil gives a metallic finish with more apparent quality and higher cost, and it has a minimum order and a setup. Heat transfer allows complex artwork in small volumes at a higher unit cost. Jacquard is not a decoration applied to ribbon — it is a weaving method, which means it changes the construction cost structure entirely. A quote that says "printed" and a quote that says "foil printed" are not comparable until you know the method, the number of colours, and whether the price includes the setup.

Separate the setup from the run. The per-metre rate and the setup are different economics with different break points, and the amortisation depends on your volume. Ask for both explicitly and separately. A supplier who bundles them into one number is not hiding anything, but they are making it impossible for you to model a reorder correctly.

Colour count is a cost driver, and colour is a strategic decision. Every additional colour in a print adds a screen, a pass, a registration point and a colour risk. A programme with twelve colours across a range is both more expensive and more exposed to variation than one with six. When you are comparing suppliers, confirm that the colour count is identical, because a quote for eight colours against a quote for twelve is not a price comparison.

Finishing is often quoted separately and quietly. Edge treatment, selvedge quality, heat setting, calendering for body, waxing for a stiffer handle, and specific hand-feel requirements. These affect both price and end-use performance. If you are comparing a quote that includes finishing against one that does not, establish which is which before concluding that one is cheaper.

Line three: setup, tooling and amortisation

Setup and tooling are one-off costs, and the single most common analytical error in ribbon costing is treating them as if they recur at the same rate every order.

List the setup items explicitly. For a printed ribbon programme you will typically encounter: screen charges per colour, plate or cylinder charges, new colour development and lab dip charges, die or tooling charges for cutting, and for moulded products the mould amortisation. Each of these may be charged per order, per programme, or per production run, and the difference between those treatments is worth real money.

Amortise across real programme volume, not first-order volume. If you will buy 30,000 metres over three seasons, amortising a screen charge across 30,000 metres is the honest comparison against a competitor quoting the full setup on a 3,000 metre order. Conversely, if you are genuinely buying 3,000 metres and never repeating, the full setup is your real cost and a supplier who helps you see that is more useful than one who quietly amortises it over a volume you have not committed to.

Amortisation should be transparent, and you should check it. If a quoted unit price is low, ask what setup has been absorbed into it and over what volume. Sometimes the unit price is genuinely low because the setup was amortised across other customers' volume. Sometimes the unit price is low because the supplier has not charged for setup it will add later. The question takes thirty seconds and the answer is usually revealing.

Line four: freight, duty and the delivered cost

This is the block that most often turns a "cheap" quote into an expensive order, and it is the block most often compared carelessly.

Know your Incoterm and what it includes. EXW means the factory hands you the goods and everything else is yours. FOB means the price covers everything up to loading at the origin port. CIF adds ocean freight and insurance to the port of destination. DDP means the supplier delivers, cleared and duty paid, to your door. A comparison between a DDP quote and an FOB quote is meaningless unless you have normalised it, and normalising DDP is harder because you cannot see inside it.

For like-for-like comparison, use a common basis. In practice, the cleanest approach is to take each quote to FOB, then build the same downstream cost block yourself using the same freight rate and the same assumptions. You then compare like-for-like, and you also gain visibility into what the supplier is actually charging you for.

Understand how ribbon is classified for duty. HS classification for ribbon depends on construction and material, and the duty rate varies accordingly. Woven ribbon of man-made fibres, elastic ribbon, metallic ribbon and other constructions can fall into different headings with materially different rates. Ask the supplier for the HS code they intend to declare and check it against your own understanding, because a misclassification that gets corrected later at your border is an unpleasant surprise. We are happy to state the classification we declare for a given article and the supporting basis for it.

Volumetrics matter more than weight. Ribbon is light and frequently bulky. A 40-foot container that you would expect to hold a large volume of ribbon may hold considerably less than the weight limit implies, because the volume is constrained by how the rolls or cartons are packed. Ask for the actual loading plan — how many cartons, what the internal dimensions are and how the packing is arranged — because it determines how much fits in a container and therefore your freight rate per metre. This is a frequent source of a real cost difference between two suppliers quoting identical specifications.

The eight costs most quotes hide

These are the costs that reliably appear in real programmes and reliably fail to appear in quotations. They are listed roughly in order of how often they surprise people.

1. Incoming inspection and sorting labour. Your team opens cartons, checks against the specification, handles discrepancies and deals with exceptions. On a large programme this is real hours at a real rate, and it is often the largest cost that appears in no quote at all.

2. The cost of a failure at retail. If a defective batch reaches your customer's shelf, the cost is not the ribbon. It is the returns, the inspection, the markdown, the reputational damage and the account review. For a retailer doing annual supplier reviews, one serious quality event can cost more than a year of price savings.

3. Defect and rework cost. Estimate it from your own history, or ask the supplier for their defect rate and apply it: defect rate multiplied by the cost of a defective unit, which includes material, labour, freight, and the handling cost of sorting and reworking. A supplier with strong incoming controls will have a low, stable number and should be willing to state it.

4. Obsolescence and clearance. This is the cost of a colour that did not sell, a seasonal line that ran late, or a design that was discontinued. It is a function of forecast accuracy and SKU rationalisation rather than of the supplier, but it lands in the ribbon programme's cost of ownership and it should be in the model.

5. Inventory carrying cost. Safety stock, buffer for seasonal variance, and slow-moving stock sitting in the warehouse. Capital cost plus storage plus the obsolescence risk. If your programme holds three months of coverage on a fast-moving line, that capital is tied up.

6. Sample and strike-off costs. Dev samples, lab dips, strike-offs and pre-production runs. Some suppliers absorb these, some charge them, and the treatment varies across a single programme. Over twelve months these add up to a real number and should be tracked.

7. Currency movement. The gap between the exchange rate at quotation, at order and at settlement. On a six-month order cycle with a 6 percent adverse move, this is material. A forward contract or a currency clause in the agreement addresses it.

8. The internal cost of running the programme. The time your team spends on samples, approvals, follow-ups, inspections, claim handling and reorders. It is the largest single line on this list for most buyers and it never appears in a quotation because it is not the supplier's cost.

What we would tell a buyer who can only do one thing: build the model once, with all twelve lines, and use it on every quote including your own current supplier's. The second use is the one that matters, because it shows you what your current landed cost actually is — and it is frequently higher than the finance team believes.

Working capital: the cost of your own payment terms

Payment terms are usually discussed as a negotiation lever. They are also a genuine cost line, and one that most cost models omit entirely.

The arithmetic is simple. Order value multiplied by the number of days your cash is tied up, divided by 365, multiplied by your cost of capital. A 30 percent deposit on a 40,000 USD order with a 90-day cycle time means 12,000 USD is tied up for three months. At an 8 percent annual cost of capital, that is roughly 240 USD — which sounds trivial until you multiply it across a dozen programmes or apply it to the full order value rather than the deposit.

The full picture is wider than the deposit. The gap between paying the supplier and collecting from your customer includes the production cycle, the ocean transit, customs clearance, the delivery to your warehouse and your own receivables period. On custom ribbon imported into Europe or North America, that gap is commonly 90 to 150 days from deposit to cash. Multiply by the order value, and the working capital cost is routinely larger than the entire decoration cost of the article.

What this means practically. First, a supplier offering better terms is making a real economic concession that belongs in your comparison, not a gift to be refused politely. Second, the deposit percentage is worth negotiating explicitly, because it is the parameter with the largest leverage on the cash number. Third, reorder frequency is a cost lever in disguise: a programme that reorders more frequently, in smaller lots, ties up less capital and carries less obsolescence, at the price of more frequent setup and freight.

We are happy to discuss payment structures that reflect your cash cycle, because a supplier who understands your position can often structure something that works for both sides. What we will not do is quote a price on an assumption about terms you have not confirmed, because it makes the comparison meaningless.

Comparing two quotations properly

With the model built, the comparison itself is straightforward. This is the sequence that produces a defensible answer rather than an arguable one.

  1. Verify equivalence first. Same construction, width, material, decoration method, colour count, tolerance framework, packing and quantity. A cheaper quote for a different article is not a cheaper quote. This step eliminates more apparent savings than any other.
  2. Normalise the terms. Convert every quote to FOB, or to landed cost on your own freight assumption. Record the exchange rate and the date you used.
  3. Build the same downstream block for each. Freight, duty, clearance, delivery — identical assumptions across suppliers.
  4. Add your own lines. Inspection labour, defect allowance, working capital, inventory carrying. Same assumptions across suppliers.
  5. Compute cost per usable metre. Net off expected defects and rejects if the quote does not already reflect them. This is the number that goes into your margin model.
  6. Test sensitivity. What happens if freight rates rise 20 percent, or the exchange rate moves 5 percent, or the volume drops 30 percent? The quote that wins at base case is not always the quote that wins under stress.
  7. Then negotiate on the levers. Now that you know the cost structure, you can have a real conversation about where the cost actually sits.

On step six in particular. A quote that is 4 percent cheaper but breaks at a 30 percent volume drop is not the better quote if the programme is volatile. A quote that is 6 percent more expensive but stable at half the volume may be the better quote for a brand that is still finding its demand. This is the part of the analysis that a spreadsheet full of unit prices does not do for you, and it is frequently the part that determines whether a private label ribbon programme works.

Where the negotiation room actually is

Buyers often arrive expecting the negotiation to be about the number on the quotation. It rarely is. The number on the quotation is usually already close to where the supplier can go, and pushing on it produces a small concession and a strained relationship.

The negotiation room is in the cost structure, and it is found by working on the levers that actually move cost:

Volume and consolidation. Consolidating across SKUs and across programmes, or committing a larger volume with a longer horizon, gives the supplier a reason to improve their own utilisation. This is the lever with the most real value and it costs the buyer nothing but a commitment.

Specification simplification. Fewer colours. Fewer decoration methods. A standard construction from a proven range rather than a bespoke one. Fewer widths. A common core across the range that you differentiate with decoration rather than with construction. Every one of these reduces the number of changeovers, set-ups and unique materials, and the supplier can usually show you exactly where the saving sits.

Production run length and sequencing. A longer production run per colour, with a planned sequence across the programme, reduces changeover loss. Some buyers can offer to accept a slightly longer lead time in exchange for a better price, and some suppliers can offer a shorter lead time at a premium. Both are legitimate levers that only become visible once you are talking about the model rather than the number.

Terms and deposit structure. As set out above, this is a real economic concession and it belongs in the comparison rather than beside it.

Freight and packaging. Carton configuration, roll format, container loading plan, consolidation of shipments. These affect both the freight rate per metre and the damage rate on arrival, and the damage rate is a cost line that quietly runs through everything downstream.

Payment of samples and setup. Small amounts, but across a twelve-month programme they add up, and a supplier who can absorb them or amortise them is doing something real for you.

What we would tell a buyer who has been told a lower price is available elsewhere: ask for the same breakdown. A supplier who can show you where the cost sits will usually meet a target price if the target is genuinely achievable, and will tell you clearly if it is not. A supplier who answers a request for a breakdown with a lower number is telling you that the number is all you are going to get, and that the next order will be the one where the structure catches up with you.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions procurement managers and brand owners most often ask about ribbon cost analysis: how to compare two quotations, what FOB really excludes, how to calculate the working capital cost of your own payment terms, and which costs most quotes hide. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between FOB and landed cost for custom ribbon?

FOB is a port-to-port price. It covers everything the supplier does up to loading the container at the origin port and it stops there. Landed cost is the total that reaches your warehouse, and it adds ocean freight, insurance, duty and tariffs, customs clearance and brokerage, port and documentation charges, inland delivery and, usually, the financing cost of the money you paid the supplier. For custom ribbon, the gap between the two is typically a meaningful share of the ex-works price rather than a rounding error, so comparing quotations on FOB without normalising them is comparing two different things.

2. How do I compare two ribbon quotations that look different on the face of them?

Normalise them onto the same basis before comparing anything. Get each quote broken into the same line items: material, decoration, setup and tooling amortisation, finishing, packing, inland and export handling, and the freight term. Confirm that quantity, width, construction, decoration method and colour count are actually equivalent, because a cheaper quote for a different article is not a cheaper quote. Only then convert every quote to the same landed basis at the same freight rate and the same volume, and compare the total cost per usable metre. A supplier who can show you a line-item breakdown makes this easy; a supplier who cannot is telling you something useful about what will happen when you change something.

3. What is the working capital cost of a ribbon order and how do I calculate it?

It is the cost of financing the gap between paying the supplier and selling the finished goods. The calculation is straightforward: order value multiplied by the number of days between your payment and your cash being recovered, divided by 365, multiplied by your cost of capital. For a 30% deposit on a 40,000 USD order with 90 days of cycle time, the deposit alone is 12,000 USD tied up for three months. At an 8% annual cost of capital that is around 240 USD, which sounds trivial until you multiply it across a dozen programmes. It is also the reason that terms, deposit structure and reorder frequency have a bigger effect on your real cost than a one percent saving on the unit price.

4. Should I include defect and rework costs in my ribbon cost model?

Yes, because they are real and they are not evenly distributed across suppliers. A supplier with strong incoming controls and a stable process will run a low defect rate; a supplier without them will have a rate you can actually observe in the claims you receive. You can estimate it from your own history if you have any, and otherwise ask the supplier directly for their defect and rework rate, which is a question a competent supplier will answer with a number. Then apply it: defect rate multiplied by the cost of a defective unit (material plus labour plus freight plus the handling cost of sorting and reworking) is a per-unit cost that belongs in the model.

5. How much should tooling and setup be amortised across an order?

Across the total volume you will genuinely purchase over the life of the programme, not across the first order alone. Screen printing setups, plates, dies and new colour development are one-off costs that repeat far less often than a buyer assumes when comparing a first order to a reorder. If you will buy 30,000 metres across three seasons, amortising a setup across 30,000 metres is the honest comparison against a competitor quoting at full setup cost on 3,000 metres. Ask what the setup costs, how much of it is per-order versus per-programme, and how it is amortised in the quoted unit price. A supplier who explains the amortisation clearly is easier to negotiate with than one who simply quotes a lower number.

6. What costs do buyers most often forget in a ribbon cost model?

In our experience the recurring omissions are: incoming inspection and sorting labour at your warehouse; the cost of a quality failure at retail, which is many times the unit cost of the ribbon; obsolescence and clearance on slow-moving colours; the carrying cost of safety stock; sample and strike-off costs charged across a programme; the currency movement between quote, order and settlement; and the internal cost of the people running the programme. The last one is frequently the largest and it never appears in a quotation because it is not the supplier's cost.

7. Should I use DDP or FOB when comparing suppliers in different countries?

For a like-for-like comparison, FOB or EXW normalised to a common basis is usually cleaner, because DDP pricing embeds the supplier's freight contract, their duty arrangement and their handling, which you then cannot see or challenge. DDP is attractive when you lack the volume to move a container yourself, and it does reduce administrative load, but you should compare it against a FOB-plus-your-own-model figure rather than against another supplier's DDP. If you do use DDP, ask specifically whether it is DDP to your door and what is excluded, because a DDP quote that excludes duty and import handling is not DDP in the sense most buyers assume.

8. How do I negotiate price without damaging the supplier relationship?

Negotiate on the model, not on the number. Ask for a line-item breakdown, then work on the levers that affect the cost rather than demanding a discount on the total. Volume consolidation, fewer colours, a standard construction instead of a bespoke one, a longer production run that reduces changeovers, a simpler decoration method, accepting a different core rather than a different ship date, and freight terms that suit the volume are all levers where the supplier can genuinely move. A buyer who arrives with a well-built model and a clear volume plan gets a better outcome than one who opens with a percentage reduction request, because the first conversation is about cost structure and the second is about who absorbs the cost.
