Most ribbon disputes are not about the ribbon. They are about the roll. A buyer approves a 38 mm grosgrain in a laboratory, and three containers later the line that was supposed to run 1,200 units an hour is running at 700 because the rolls arrived on 76 mm cores, cross-wound the wrong way, with edge selvedge that frayed on the first guide. The ribbon itself passed inspection. The format failed.

This guide explains the four properties that decide whether a roll of ribbon actually works on your equipment: winding format, core size, roll length and outside diameter, and edge quality. It is written for brand owners, packaging buyers and trim converters who are about to place a production order and who have never written a roll specification before. Nothing here is exotic. It is the part of the purchase order that most people leave blank and then pay for at the receiving dock.

Why the roll matters more than buyers expect

Ribbon is specified by width, construction, material, colour and decoration. Those five things decide how the ribbon looks and how it performs in the finished product. None of them decide whether your line can run it.

A roll is a mechanical object. It has a core, an outer diameter, a mass, a direction of unwind, a tension profile across its width and an edge condition. Your machine has a matching set of expectations about all six. When the two sets disagree, the failure does not look like a ribbon failure. It looks like an operator failure: a jam, a broken guide, a skipped stitch, a roll that keeps running out, a splice that breaks the bow, a line that stops every eleven minutes to re-thread.

The expensive part is that most of these failures are discovered during production, not during incoming inspection. The ribbon has already been converted, decorated, packed, shipped and landed. By the time you know the 76 mm core is wrong, you have paid for the ribbon, the freight, the duty, the labour and the downtime. This is why the roll specification belongs on the purchase order rather than in a note to yourself.

The two winding formats, and how they differ at the machine

Almost all ribbon arrives in one of two winding formats. They are not interchangeable, and the difference is the single most common cause of line problems when a buyer switches supplier.

1. Shuttle wound (also called spool wound)

The ribbon is wound in a single layer, edge to edge, in one direction across the width. The layers do not overlap. The result is a roll where the ribbon travels in a flat ribbon across the face of the roll, exactly as it came off the loom.

What it is good for: bows, single-layer wrapping, narrow trims, any application where the ribbon must not be twisted, layered or re-wound before use. Shuttle-wound ribbon also unwinds predictably because the whole width is exposed at once and there is no risk of the face layers peeling apart.

What it costs you: the roll holds relatively little ribbon for its size, the core is often wider than the ribbon, and the roll is heavy for the metre count. Shuttle winding is also more expensive to produce, which is why it tends to be reserved for narrower widths and higher-value product.

What to check: the number of metres per roll is lower than you may expect, and the roll may be too heavy for a manual handling trolley. Specify the metres per roll explicitly rather than accepting whatever comes.

2. Chequer wound (also called transfer wound, cross-wound or cross-wound transfer)

The ribbon is wound in many overlapping layers, with the ribbon crossing back and forth at a shallow angle, so that the roll can be unwound continuously without the layers unwinding themselves.

What it is good for: long continuous runs, high-volume feeding, and any application where the ribbon is fed from a take-up reel. Chequer winding lets one roll carry far more metres, which means fewer roll changes, less downtime and lower handling cost per metre.

What it costs you: the winding angle has to be right for your take-up. Too steep and the ribbon climbs the roll and falls off; too shallow and the ribbon slides across the face and creases. The winding angle also affects the width of the ribbon you effectively feed, which matters on bow-making and winding equipment calibrated to a specific ribbon width.

What to check: ask for the winding angle and the ribbon width tolerance in the same sentence. A supplier who answers both questions without hesitating is a supplier who controls the process.

The mistake we see most often

A buyer awards a programme, then switches the manufacturing source in year two because the price improved by 4%. The new source ships chequer-wound ribbon to a line that was set up for shuttle-wound. The line does not jam outright — it runs, but it runs worse. Operators compensate by hand, output drops by 15%, and the problem gets reported as "the new supplier's quality is lower" when the actual cause is roll format.

Whenever you change supplier, and whenever you change line, write the roll format into the first purchase order as a fixed term rather than leaving it to the factory's default.

Core size: the cheapest specification you will ever write

Core size is the inside diameter of the tube the ribbon is wound on. It is measured in millimetres, and for ribbon the common sizes are 25 mm, 38 mm (also sold as 1.5 inch) and 76 mm (3 inch). There is nothing exotic about it, and yet a mismatch here will stop a line cold.

Core sizeTypical useWhat it means for your equipment
25 mmSmall spools, narrow trims, bobbin workLight, easy to handle, but more roll changes on a long run. Poor fit for spindles sized for 38 mm.
38 mmThe default for most decorative ribbon and trimWorks with the widest range of standard spindle and holder tooling. Specify this unless you know your line needs otherwise.
76 mmHigh-volume continuous feeding, chequer windingCarries the most metres per roll and minimises roll changes, but needs a 3 inch mandrel and a holder that can take the mass.

Two practical points. First, the core is not always the same size as the ribbon width. A 25 mm ribbon wound on a 38 mm core leaves visible flanges; a wide ribbon on a narrow core will not hold its shape. If you need a specific core for a reason — a carton size, a machine mandrel, a downstream feeding system — state it. Second, cores should be new, not recycled, if you are feeding into tension-controlled equipment. Recycled cardboard cores vary in wall thickness, run slightly out of round, and create a wobble that shows up as ribbon tension variation rather than as an obvious fault.

Roll length, outside diameter and the carton

Roll length and outside diameter are two different ways of describing the same physical constraint, and both must appear on the purchase order because both constrain something real.

Roll length matters for handling and for roll change frequency. A 300 m roll is easy to carry, easy to install and easy to return; a 1,000 m roll needs a trolley and a reason. If your line runs a bow machine at moderate volume, a shorter roll frequently beats a longer roll, because the operator can swap it in thirty seconds without a tool.

Outside diameter matters for the carton, for the shelf in the warehouse and for the machine's own geometry. A roll that is too wide for the mandrel will not mount; a roll that is too heavy for the take-up will stall under load; a roll that does not fit the carton means you are paying to ship air.

The relationship is simple and worth stating explicitly: metres per roll depend on ribbon width, thickness and winding format, which is why a factory cannot simply promise "1,000 m per roll" across a whole programme. Ask for metres per roll per SKU, and ask what the outside diameter will be at that metreage. Then check the diameter against your carton and your machine before you approve it, not when the container arrives.

Edge quality: selvage, and the two millimetres that matter

Selvedge — the woven edge of the ribbon — is the part buyers think least about and pay for most often. A ribbon with a clean, dense selvage can be fed without special guides. A ribbon with a loose, fraying selvage will shed fibre into the mechanism, will narrow slightly as it runs, and will break at the point where the guide contacts it.

Three specific things to ask for:

  • Selvage density. A tight edge that resists fraying when pulled. For narrow trims on high-speed lines, a dense selvage is a continuous-running requirement, not a preference.
  • Edge straightness. Ribbons are not perfectly straight. A bow that looks acceptable on a flat table can curl or twist when the edges are not parallel. If your product is dimension-critical, specify a maximum deviation across the width.
  • Width tolerance at the edge. A ribbon that measures 10 mm at one point in the roll and 10.4 mm forty metres later will not die consistently. Ask for width tolerance within the roll, not just the nominal width on the label.

What actually stops a line: five mechanical factors

When a buyer reports that "the ribbon keeps breaking", the cause is usually mechanical rather than material. Five factors account for most of it:

  1. Core concentricity. A poorly wound core is oval or off-centre, and that wobble translates directly into ribbon tension variation and lateral movement on the guide.
  2. Tension uniformity across the width. Ribbon that is tighter on one edge than the other will twist the moment it is loaded, and a twisted ribbon cannot be cut cleanly.
  3. Mass and inertia. A full 76 mm roll of heavy velvet or thick grosgrain on a mandrel that was designed for satin will stall under acceleration.
  4. Splices and welts. A glued or welded splice in the middle of a roll is invisible until it reaches the guide. Specify splice type and location, or specify a maximum splice count per roll.
  5. Interleaf and packing. Wax paper, tissue or a polyethylene interleaf between layers stops the ribbon from scuffing and creasing on the roll face. Without it, chequer-wound ribbon can take a set and develop a permanent crease.

Ask for the roll before you commit the programme

The cheapest test in this entire guide costs nothing and takes a week. Before placing a production order, ask the supplier to send you one production roll of the exact construction, width, material and decoration — not a promotional sample, not an offcut, not a hand-sampled laboratory roll. Run it on your line for a full shift and record what happens.

Measure the actual metres. Time the roll change. Watch the selvage on the guide. Run it to the end of the roll and see how the last two hundred metres behave, because that is where tension problems usually show themselves. Count the roll changes per thousand units and multiply by your shift pattern. That number is your real operating cost of the format, and it is invisible until you measure it.

Do this once per construction, once per core size and once per line. Most programmes run on two or three distinct ribbon constructions for years, so the testing effort is small and the answer stays valid for a long time.

The mistakes that cost the most

Leaving the roll format out of the purchase order. The factory will use its default, the default will not match your line, and the problem will be discovered in production under time pressure.

Changing supplier without re-testing the roll. A new factory can ship the same ribbon on a different core with a different winding format at the same specification. The ribbon is identical. The format is not.

Optimising for long rolls on a short run. A 1,000 m roll is not free material — it is a mass, an inertia and a slower changeover. For anything under a few hundred units a day, frequent shorter rolls usually cost less in total.

Ignoring the interleaf. Creases and scuffing show up as quality rejects at the customer, not at your dock, and you will rarely connect them back to the roll packing.

Not specifying the core material. New versus recycled core is a one-word specification that prevents a chronic tension problem.

Working with your supplier

Good roll specifications are not adversarial. A factory that is handed a clear roll specification will quote against it, sample against it and inspect against it, and the inspection you receive at your dock will start passing on the first time. A factory given an incomplete order has to make assumptions, and you end up paying for the assumptions you did not write down.

Ask for three things in writing before you issue the first order: the roll specification table above, a production roll for your own line test, and a signed sample of the roll label format so that receiving and stores are aligned from the first container.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, packaging buyers and trim converters most often ask us about ribbon rolls and machine compatibility. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between shuttle wound and chequer wound ribbon?

Shuttle wound ribbon is wound in a single layer, edge to edge, so it unwinds flat and will not twist — ideal for bows and single-layer wrapping, but it carries fewer metres per roll and costs more. Chequer wound (cross-wound or transfer wound) ribbon is wound in overlapping layers at a shallow angle, so it feeds continuously without the roll unwinding itself — ideal for long runs and take-up reels, but the winding angle must match your equipment or the ribbon will climb or crease.

2. What core size should I specify for decorative ribbon?

38 mm (1.5 inch) is the safest default because it fits the widest range of standard spindles and holder tooling. 25 mm is used for narrow trims and bobbin work but causes more roll changes. 76 mm (3 inch) carries the most metres and minimises roll changes on high-volume lines, but your mandrel must be a 3 inch mandrel and the holder must tolerate the mass of a full roll of heavier constructions such as velvet or thick grosgrain.

3. Why does my line run worse after I change ribbon supplier?

In most cases the ribbon construction and quality are not the issue — the roll format is. A new factory can supply the same ribbon on a different core size, with a different winding format or angle, a different selvage condition and a different interleaf. If you change supplier, re-run the production roll test on your own line before the programme goes live, and keep the roll specification as a fixed term on the purchase order rather than accepting the supplier default.

4. How many metres per roll should I order?

That depends on your line volume and how you change rolls. For a bow or trim line running under a few hundred units a day, 300 to 500 metre rolls usually cost less in total because they are fast to swap and light to handle. For high-volume continuous feeding, 1,000 metre chequer-wound rolls reduce changeover time significantly. Note that metres per roll depend on ribbon width, thickness and winding format, so ask for metres per roll and maximum outside diameter per SKU rather than accepting a programme-wide figure.

5. What is a selvage and why does it matter so much?

The selvage is the woven edge of the ribbon. A dense, tight selvage resists fraying when it passes through guides and dies, while a loose selvage sheds fibre into the mechanism, narrows the ribbon as it runs and becomes the first point of failure. For narrow trims on fast lines, specify selvage density and a maximum loose edge — for example no more than 0.5 mm — as a measurable requirement rather than a preference.

6. Are recycled cardboard cores acceptable?

They are acceptable for low-speed, low-tension applications, but for tension-controlled feeding we specify new cores. Recycled cores vary in wall thickness and run slightly out of round, and that wobble appears downstream as ribbon tension variation and lateral movement rather than as an obvious fault, which makes it difficult to diagnose at the receiving dock.

7. What should a roll label contain?

At minimum: SKU, dye or production lot, shade reference, roll number within the batch, and the actual metreage on that roll. A lot and roll number are what make a roll traceable — without them, a colour complaint or a defect found in production cannot be tied back to the production window it came from, and the affected quantity becomes a guess.

8. Can I request a production roll sample before ordering?

Yes, and we recommend it for every new construction, core size and line combination. Ask for one production roll of the exact construction, width, material and decoration — not a promotional sample or a laboratory offcut — run it on your line for a full shift, and measure actual metreage, roll change time, selvage behaviour and performance over the last 200 metres. Most programmes run on two or three ribbon constructions for years, so the test is cheap and stays valid.
