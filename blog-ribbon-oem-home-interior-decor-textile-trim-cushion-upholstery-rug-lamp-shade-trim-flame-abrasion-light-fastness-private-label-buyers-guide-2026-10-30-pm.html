Interior Trim Is a Different Specification Problem

Decorative ribbon for a gift box is an aesthetic and a brand-signalling decision. Decorative ribbon for a home is an aesthetic decision that sits inside a residential environment, a retail compliance framework and a set of physical tests that do not apply anywhere else in the decoration world. Buyers who carry a gift-box brief into a homeware programme and then wonder why the quotes disagree, why the mill asks unusual questions, or why the sample passes inspection and then pucks along the seam after a season in a customer's living room, are almost always experiencing the same thing: a specification that did not describe the environment the trim will actually live in. Three things make interior trim different. First, fire. Any trim that goes into an upholstered product, a cushion, a rug, a curtain or a window treatment falls inside the flammability expectations of furniture retailers, homeware brands and, in many markets, building regulation. This is not an optional test and it is not usually something a general decoration ribbon mill has run. Second, wear. A home is a place where products are touched, wiped, cleaned, pulled and moved. The trim on a piped cushion edge and the trim on a basket handle are high-contact components in a way that a ribbon on a gift bow is not. Third, the environment itself - light, humidity, cleaning chemicals, and the fact that a product bought in January in a dry warehouse may spend July in a coastal apartment. And there is a commercial reason to treat interior trim seriously rather than as a sideline. Homeware and soft furnishings are a large, design-led category with high repeat-purchase behaviour, and the buyers in it are professional: they work with structured specifications, they test to written methods, and they treat a supplier who asks good questions as an asset rather than as a source of delay. A mill that learns to speak this language gains access to programmes that run for years and into ranges rather than single items. At Smith Ribbon, in Xiamen since 2004, home and interior decor is a meaningful and growing share of our decorative trim work, and the difference in how those programmes run compared with general gift packaging is very visible. This guide is written for the buyer who has to specify, source and approve trim for a homeware, soft furnishings or interior accessories programme - whether that is a cushion brand specifying piped satin, a rug maker specifying a heavy grosgrain border, a lighting brand specifying a tapered lampshade trim, or a homeware retailer building a private-label bedding accessories line. The advice is written for B2B procurement and product roles, and it assumes you need a specification a mill can quote and a quality regime you can defend to a retailer or a compliance auditor.

Where the Trim Actually Goes: Six Positions and Six Different Problems

The most useful first step in an interior trim brief is to state, for each trim position, exactly what the trim does and what it touches. Six positions cover almost all of the interior trim market, and each one carries a different specification. 1. Cushion piping. A corded or flat trim wrapped into the seam of a cushion cover. It is the highest-contact decorative component in the category - handled, pulled, sat on, and rubbed by clothing. It must be dimensionally stable enough that the seam does not pucker, colour fast enough not to mark a pale cover, and it must not be so stiff that the cover looks over-specified. A satin with a fibre core is the classic choice; the core diameter determines the piping's bulk, and this is the single parameter that most affects the finished cushion's feel. 2. Upholstery edging and decorative bands. Flat trim applied to the outside of a sofa, chair or headboard, either as a contrast band or as edge trim. This is a high-abrasion, high-visibility position. It is also the position where flame retardancy most often becomes mandatory rather than advisory, because the trim is part of an upholstered article covered by furniture flammability rules. 3. Rug borders and edge binding. Heavy, firm, non-stretch trim on the perimeter of a rug, mat or runner. This position demands structural stability - the trim carries load at the rug edge and must not extend or contract enough to lift the corner - and it is almost always a heavy grosgrain, woven tape or a jacquard woven border. 4. Lampshade trim and suspension tape. A narrow trim used as the bottom edge or decorative band of a lampshade, and a woven or flat tape used to suspend a shade. The trim sits on a cone or drum, often at a taper, which means it must accommodate a geometric transition without buckling. It may also carry the electrical load path in some constructions, which is a safety matter rather than an aesthetic one and must be handled as such. 5. Window treatments and soft furnishing trims. Curtain heading tape, tiebacks, valance trim, and the tie-backs and decorative edges on cushions and throws. These are the positions where cleaning regime matters most, because the finished product is cleaned on a cycle specified by the brand and the customer. 6. Decorative accessories and finishing touches. Bow ties, rosettes, tassels, cords and wrapped handles on storage baskets, boxes and organisers. This is the position closest to the general gift-packaging world, where the specification discipline is familiar. The point of listing them is not the list. It is that a single RFQ that covers a cushion range and a basket range with one generic description of "decorative ribbon" will produce quotes for products that are wrong for at least one of the positions. Ask for a line item per position, with its own specification. The mill can quote six trims competently; it cannot quote one ribbon that satisfies six incompatible requirements.

The Five Material Families and What Each One Does

Five families cover most interior trim. The right choice depends on the position, the environment and the signal you want the trim to carry. Satin and polyester satin. The standard for cushion piping and soft edging because it drapes, takes a stitch cleanly, presses into a piping cord without adding bulk, and holds a soft sheen that reads as considered rather than utilitarian. Polyester satin has good colour depth and takes printed artwork with clean definition. Its weaknesses are abrasion resistance, which is lower than a heavy construction, and sharp crease behaviour along a folded edge - which matters where the trim wraps a tight radius, as piping does. Grosgrain and heavy polyester grosgrain. The structural choice. Ribbed, firm, with almost no stretch, it holds an edge, takes a stitch without distorting, and carries load without relaxing. Used on rug borders, basket handles, waistbands, straps and anywhere the trim has to do a structural job. Grosgrain is also the most economical way to add a coloured edge to a large area of a homeware product, which is why it appears in so much private-label soft furnishing. Velvet and velvet-faced ribbon. Carries a premium homeware signal through texture rather than print or colour. Used on cushions, bedding accessories, dressing-room accessories and gift-style home products. Velvet has a pile, which brings its own requirements: pile direction, pile crush, the tendency to show pressure marks and shading, and the need to specify whether the trim is cut pile-up or pile-down. A velvet trim specified without pile direction is a specification that will produce two different-looking products from the same roll. Cotton and cotton-blend tape and webbing. Used where a natural, matte, washable appearance is wanted, and where a product has to be laundered. Cotton shrinks, and cotton blends with polyester are engineered to manage that, but the shrink figure has to be in the specification and it has to be the shrink figure for the specific construction and the specific wash condition. Jacquard and woven decorative trim. Damask, brocade, metallic thread and textured woven effects, appearing on cushions, upholstery, rugs and at the premium end of lampshades. Jacquard trim is where a brand's pattern and the mill's pattern capability meet. If the trim carries a specific motif that is part of the brand identity, this becomes a design-development conversation rather than a catalogue selection, and the artwork, the repeat and the minimum order quantity need to be agreed at the design stage rather than at the quoting stage. Two cross-cutting variables. The first is fibre content and the associated fire and wash behaviour. Polyester, cotton, rayon and their blends behave differently under every test that matters here, and a recycled polyester (RPET) variant has its own documentation requirements under textile regulations and eco-design rules in the EU. The second is width and construction weight, because these drive both cost and behaviour: a 12 mm satin piping and a 25 mm grosgrain border are different products in every sense, including how they behave on the sewing line and how they drape over a cushion edge.

Flame, Abrasion, Light, Washing and Humidity

This is where interior trim earns its own specification, because a trim that performs perfectly in a gift box can fail in a home in ways no general decoration test would predict. Flame retardancy. The relevant references are California Technical Bulletin TB 117, which addresses flammability requirements for upholstered furniture and mattresses including requirements for components and trim; 16 CFR Part 1631, the US federal flammability standard for upholstered furniture and mattresses; and in Europe, EN 1021-1 and EN 1021-2 for upholstered seating and EN 13773 for textiles used in upholstered furniture. The buyer should state the standard, the test method and the pass criterion, and should state whether the trim is applied to the outer cover or is in contact with the filling material, because that distinction changes the applicable requirement. The commercial reality is that most general decoration ribbon mills have not run these tests, and a mill that has to bring in a flame-retardant chemistry or a specialist finisher will reflect that in price and lead time. Find out early rather than at the sample stage. Abrasion. Martindale is the reference, expressed in cycles, with the test method and conditioning stated. A trim used on a daily cushion, a sofa band or a basket handle needs a figure that reflects high contact, and the specified figure should be one the mill can evidence for that specific construction rather than for a fabric they happen to have tested. A mill quoting 20,000 cycles for a generic ribbon without saying it is their base fabric is quoting a number that does not apply to your trim. Light fastness. Measured under ISO 105-B02, with a rating of 4 as a sensible floor for interior trim and 5 or better where the product sits in a sunny position or a retail window. Fade in direct sunlight is a common and entirely avoidable complaint, and it is avoidable only if the rating was specified before the material was chosen. Colour fastness and crocking. Dry rubbing under ISO 105-X12 and wet rubbing under ISO 105-X11. Wet crocking is the more important of the two indoors, because homes contain moisture - a hand with a drink, a cleaning wipe, condensation on a window - and a trim that transfers colour under wet contact will mark a pale cushion cover or a light wall. Specify both, name the methods, and test on the finished colour and finish, since metallic thread, flock and heavy backing all change the result. Colour fastness to cleaning agents. If the finished product is wipeable or cleanable, the trim's resistance to the specific cleaning chemistry matters. Many cleaning products are alkaline, and an unbranded dispersion dye can be affected. This is a specification detail that costs nothing to include and saves a customer complaint later. Shrinkage after washing. Where the trim is in a washable product, specify the shrinkage after the stated wash cycle and the stated condition, for the specific construction. Cotton-heavy trims shrink; cotton-polyester blends shrink less and predictably. The figure must come from the actual construction, not from a family average. Dimensional stability across humidity. This is the failure mode that causes most interior trim returns, and it fails slowly enough to escape notice at the factory. A piping that grows in length pucks along a seam; a rug border that extends lifts a corner; a trim on a tapered lampshade goes slack as the cone widens. The measurement is normally dimensional change after conditioning at a stated temperature and relative humidity. Because a home experiences seasonal humidity swings that a warehouse does not, this is worth specifying even for products that are never washed, and the specification should state the direction of change you can tolerate, not just the magnitude. And abrasion by friction from neighbours. On a shelf, a cushion rubs against a neighbouring product. A trim with a poor crocking result will transfer onto that neighbour. This is a retail-shelf failure rather than a manufacturing one, and specifying crocking against a pale neighbour fabric is the mitigation.

Colour, Print, Texture and the Limits of Decoration

Interior trim is design-led, and the colour and decoration decisions are made at the brand level long before anyone speaks to a mill. That creates a specific risk: the brand approves a colour against a graphic rendering, and the mill approves a dye lot against a Pantone chip, and the two are not the same reference. Approve against a physical standard, not a chip. Ask the mill to produce a physical swatch, have it sealed and retained, and approve that swatch. Keep one at your site. Every subsequent batch should be compared against it - ideally with a spectrophotometer and a stated Delta E tolerance rather than by eye under a warehouse light. Specify the illuminant and the measurement face, because the ribbon face and the reverse of a woven ribbon are measurably different, and a velvet measured under the wrong illuminant will give you a figure that does not correlate with how it looks. Design continuity across a range is a bigger problem than it looks. A cushion range of twelve colourways, a rug border and a lampshade trim in what the brand calls "sage" must agree with each other across three different constructions and possibly three different mills. The way to achieve that is to hold one physical standard per shade and require every supplier to submit against it, and to nominate one reference sample for the range that travels between suppliers. Where the same shade appears in both a satin and a velvet, expect them to read differently even when measured identically - a velvet surface reflects light differently from a satin - and decide in advance which is the reference rather than discovering the disagreement at shelf review. Print and decoration. Screen printing, heat transfer and foil are all used on interior trim, with screen printing still dominant for grosgrain and satin in volume programmes. Specify the method, the ink system, the number of colours and the repeat length. For a jacquard or woven patterned trim, the artwork needs to be developed with the mill's jacquard capability in mind - repeat length, whether the pattern must register across a seam, and whether the design is a stock pattern or a custom one. Custom jacquard development carries a setup charge and usually a higher MOQ, and it should be discussed at design stage rather than at quote stage. Metallic, flock and velvet pile. Metallic thread and metallised film in an interior trim is a design feature rather than a food-contact issue, but it brings two requirements: a wear specification, because metallic thread abrades and can break down to reveal a dull base, and a cleaning constraint, because a flocked or metallic surface cannot generally be wet-cleaned without damage. Velvet's pile direction and pile crush have already been noted; the addition to make is the anti-crush requirement if the product is sat on or pressed. And the dye-lot question that catches everyone. Dyes are the one component where batch-to-batch variation is intrinsic rather than a defect. A mill that has never worked with a customer on dye-lot continuity is often surprised by how much the buyer cares. Agree the tolerance, hold the standard swatch, and have the mill retain a sample from every approved lot. For a range that runs seasonally, ask the mill to hold the yarn and dye lots for a defined period so that a re-order nine months later does not arrive in a visibly different shade.

Format, Sewing Behaviour and the Production Line

Trim arrives at the sewing room as a roll, and a lot of avoidable downtime comes from specifications that did not describe the physical format. The four numbers that matter. Width in millimetres, length in metres per roll, core diameter, and whether the trim is supplied flat or as a corded piping. Corded piping has a separate manufacturing route - a core is inserted and the ribbon is wound around it - and it carries a different price and a different MOQ, and it is quoted separately from flat trim. A brief that says "piping" without saying whether it wants flat satin plus a separately purchased cord, or factory-corded piping, produces quotes that are not comparable. Core diameter. The two common cores are 25.4 mm (one inch) and 76 mm (three inches). Homeware sewing rooms typically use one-inch cores for trim and three-inch for wider webbing, and some industrial cutting tables are built around a specific core. State which the buyer has, or state that the mill should supply cores to the buyer's specification. Width tolerance and how it behaves on the sewing line. A trim with a generous width tolerance will produce visibly uneven piping on a cushion because the cord core sits differently relative to the edge. Specify the width tolerance and the seam allowance the trim is designed for, and give the mill your actual seam construction - the finished seam size and whether the trim is enclosed or caught. A mill that has been given the seam construction will size the trim for it; a mill that has not will size it for a generic one. Bevelled edges. A woven trim has a raw edge. On a piped cushion this matters greatly, because the raw edge is enclosed but on a flat band it is visible. Specify a bevelled or heat-sealed edge for any trim that will be visible edge-on, and specify the tolerance on the edge condition, because it is one of the most common causes of a quality rejection on arrival. Stretch and the piping dimension. Piping that stretches along its length will produce a pucked, gathered cushion cover - the classic amateur upholstery failure. Polyester satin has minimal stretch; cotton and some blends do not. Where stretch matters, specify the stretch figure in the specification. Splice control. A roll that is spliced or knotted mid-roll stops a sewing run. Ask for the maximum number of splices per roll, whether they are detectable, and the roll length that avoids splicing. At Smith Ribbon, in Xiamen since 2004, we size roll length to the customer's line and state the splice count in the packing list, because an undetected splice in a high-volume cut-and-sew operation costs a buyer more than the price difference between two mills ever will.

MOQ, Sampling and the Documentation Package

Three practical mechanics that come up repeatedly in interior trim sourcing. MOQ is usually a material MOQ, not a setup MOQ. The binding constraint is usually the dyed yarn lot - a colour needs a minimum dye-lot quantity to be economic, commonly 300 to 500 metres per colour, sometimes higher for a shade requiring a specific dye. Corded piping adds its own minimum, because the core is a separate component with its own supply. A programme with ten colourways is really running ten yarn lots, and the effective minimum is ten times the per-colour figure. This is worth modelling explicitly, because it changes which range architectures are viable: a twelve-colour cushion range and a three-colour core range have very different economics at the same order quantity. The workable planning approach for homeware is to concentrate colour investment - carry two or three neutral shades in depth across the whole range, run seasonal accents in smaller quantities on a longer replenishment horizon - and to ask the mill for the minimum on each colourway separately rather than a single programme figure. Sampling has three distinct stages and they are not interchangeable. A yarn or greige sample shows the construction. A lab dip or strike-off shows the colour and print but not the finished hand. A woven and finished sample shows the real product. For an interior programme the finished sample is the one that matters, and it should be produced on the production loom with the production colour recipe and the production finish. For trim that will be corded, cord a sample as well, because the hand of a corded trim and the way it sits in a seam are not predictable from flat. Ask whether the sample was made on production equipment. And the documentation package. Ask for seven named items, because a request for "certificates" returns a portfolio rather than what you need:

1. A full material declaration covering every component - fibre, backing, adhesive, any metallic thread, any flock or padding, and any cord core.
2. The flame retardancy test report for the specific standard you need, with the method, the pass criterion and the sample description matching your actual construction.
3. The abrasion and Martindale report, with the method and conditioning stated.
4. The colour fastness reports - light fastness under ISO 105-B02, dry crocking under ISO 105-X12, wet crocking under ISO 105-X11 - with methods stated.
5. The dimensional stability report, with the conditioning regime and the tolerance on change.
6. Quality and social audit documentation - ISO 9001 for the quality system, plus the social and ethical audit credentials your retailer requires.
7. Batch traceability - a lot code on every carton linked to yarn lot, dye lot and production date, with a stated retention period for the retained standard swatch. Treat a supplier that answers with a generic certificate folder as a supplier that did not read the brief. The right response is not to reject the supplier but to ask again with the seven items named individually. Most mills that have worked with homeware brands will produce all seven; the ones that cannot have not previously been set up for it, and you now know that before you have committed rather than after.

Writing the Brief That Gets a Quotable Specification

Pulling the above together into a brief that a mill can quote without a follow-up call takes about two pages per trim position. This is the structure we would use if we were buying. Section one - the programme. Who you are, the product the trim goes into, the channels and markets, the annual volumes by colourway and by position, and the target launch date. Volumes are a forecast, clearly labelled. Section two - the position and its contact condition. For each trim line: where it sits in the finished product, what it touches, whether it is in direct contact with the filling material in an upholstered article, and whether it is machine-washed, wipe-cleaned or neither. Section three - the material and construction. Family, fibre content and any recycled content, width and tolerance, weave, construction weight, edge condition, whether flat or factory-corded, core diameter, roll length, and the seam construction the trim is designed for. Section four - the decoration. Method, artwork status, colour references with the tolerance and the measurement method, number of colours, repeat length, metallic or flock or pile detail and its position in the product. Section five - the performance requirements. Flame standard with method and pass criterion, Martindale figure, light fastness rating, dry and wet crocking ratings, shrinkage after wash where applicable, dimensional change with the conditioning regime, and cleaning resistance - each as a testable line rather than a descriptor. Section six - the packaging and logistics. Carton quantity, carton dimensions, whether cores are supplied by the mill or the buyer, shipping configuration, destination and delivery term. Section seven - the documentation. The seven items named individually, with the market named. Section eight - the questions. Ask what the mill needs that you have not specified, what it would change about the specification and why, what it would recommend instead, and what it can hold consistently versus where it is at the edge. A mill that proposes a different construction for a cushion piping - a specific heavier satin, a different cord diameter, a different pile for the velvet line - is doing the actual work, and those proposals deserve evaluation rather than dismissal. One closing point on the relationship. Interior trim programmes reward suppliers who understand the sewing room and the audit. They are rarely one-off: a cushion range that sells well becomes a bedding range, then a lighting range, then a coordinated collection across a whole home, and the mill that understands the colour continuity requirement and the wash regime becomes part of your supply base rather than a vendor youwash regime becomes part of your supply base rather than a vendor you re-tender each season. Frequently Asked Questions

Does decorative ribbon need to be flame retardant for home use?

Usually yes, or at least the specification has to address fire. Any trim used in upholstered furniture, cushions, rugs, curtains and window treatments falls inside the fire-safety expectations of most furniture and homeware retailers and of building regulations in many markets. In the United States the two references you will meet are California Technical Bulletin TB 117, which covers the flammability requirements for upholstered furniture and mattresses including the requirements for the filling material and any components including trim, and 16 CFR Part 1631, the federal flammability standard for upholstered furniture and mattresses. In Europe the reference is EN 1021-1 and EN 1021-2 for upholstered seating and EN 13773 for textiles used in upholstered furniture. The point for a buyer is that the requirement applies to the trim as a component, and a mill that only supplies generic decoration ribbon will not have tested for it. Specify the standard, the test method and the pass criterion - and specify whether the trim is in direct contact with the filling material or applied to the outer cover, because the answer changes the test.

Which ribbon materials are used for interior decor trim?

Five families cover most of the interior trim market. Satin and polyester satin is the standard for cushion piping and soft edging because it drapes, takes a stitch cleanly and presses into a piping cord without bulk. Grosgrain and heavy polyester grosgrain is the structural choice for rugs, basket handles and anywhere a firm, non-stretch edge is needed. Velvet and velvet-faced ribbon carries a premium homeware signal and is used on cushions, bedroom and dressing-room accessories. Cotton and cotton-blend tape and webbing is used where a natural, washable, matte appearance is wanted. Jacquard and woven decorative trim - including metallic thread, damask and brocade effects - appears on cushions and upholstery and at the premium end of lampshades. Within each family the fibre content, construction, width and finish determine how the trim behaves under flame, abrasion, light, cleaning and humidity, and those are the properties that should drive the specification.

How should I specify colour fastness for interior trim?

Light fastness and colour fastness are two different requirements and they are usually asked about separately. Light fastness is resistance to fading under light exposure, measured on the ISO 105-B02 scale, where a rating of 4 or better is typical for interior trim and 5 to 6 is expected where the product sits in a sunny position or in a retail window. Colour fastness, or crocking, is the transfer of colour onto another surface through rubbing, measured under ISO 105-X12 for dry rubbing and ISO 105-X11 for wet rubbing. A trim that is light fast but not colour fast will leave a coloured mark on a white cushion cover or a pale wall. For interior use, wet crocking is the more important of the two because moisture - a drink, a cleaning wipe, condensation on a window - is present in a home environment. Specify both, name the method, and require the test on the finished colour and finish, because a foil or metallic thread changes the result.

How do dimensional stability and humidity affect interior trim?

Dimensional stability is the property that causes most interior trim failures, and it fails slowly rather than suddenly, which makes it easy to miss. A cushion piping that grows or shrinks in length will begin to pucker along a seam; a rug border that changes length will lift at one corner; a lamp trim on a tapered shade will slacken or go tight as the shape changes. The measurement is normally a dimensional change after conditioning at a stated temperature and relative humidity - commonly a specified change after 24 hours at conditions such as 20 degrees and 65 per cent relative humidity. The reason this matters more for interior than for gift packaging is the environment: a home experiences seasonal humidity swings that a warehouse does not, and a product that has been in a dry warehouse through winter and then moves into a humid home will behave differently from the way it was tested. Specify the dimensional change limit, the conditioning regime, and the direction of the change you can tolerate.

What is the cleaning and care requirement for decorative interior trim?

A trim that has to be dry-clean only is often incompatible with the product it is sewn into, and this is a frequent point of friction between a trim supplier and a homeware brand. The practical approach is to define the cleaning regime of the finished product first - wipe clean, machine washable, washable at 30 or 40 degrees, spot clean only, dry clean - and then specify the trim against it. That means specifying the colour fastness under the wash conditions, the shrinkage after washing, any sheen change, and whether the trim can be ironed, since a metallic or flocked trim cannot. Include in the specification the requirement that the trim not discolour or lose body after the specified number of cleaning cycles, and be explicit that this is a cycle count rather than a single test. Retailers increasingly ask for the care instructions of a finished product to be unambiguous, which means the trim's care behaviour has to be known and written down.

What documentation should I require from a ribbon supplier for an interior trim programme?

Seven named items, requested individually, because a request for certificates returns a portfolio rather than what you need. First, a full material declaration covering every component of the trim - fibre, backing, adhesive, any metallic thread, any flock or padding. Second, the flame retardancy test report for the specific standard you need, with the test method, the pass criterion and the sample description matching your actual construction, because a flame test on the base ribbon does not cover a finished trim with a backing. Third, the abrasion and Martindale report. Fourth, the colour fastness reports, light and wet crocking, with the methods stated. Fifth, the dimensional stability report with the conditioning regime. Sixth, quality and social audit documentation - ISO 9001 for the quality system plus the social and ethical audit credentials your retailer requires. Seventh, batch traceability, with a lot code on every carton linked to the yarn lot, dye lot and production date. Ask for these by name and treat a supplier that returns a generic folder as a supplier that has not read the brief.

How should I approach MOQ and colour planning for a multi-SKU interior trim range?

Model the range as yarn lots, not order quantities. Ribbon MOQ is usually a material MOQ constrained by the dyed yarn lot, commonly 300 to 500 metres per colour, with print setup charged once per screen or plate on top. A cushion range of twelve colourways is therefore twelve yarn lots, and the effective minimum is twelve times the per-colour figure. That changes which ranges are viable at a given volume. The workable planning approach for home ware is to concentrate colour investment: identify the two or three neutral shades that carry most volume across the whole range and carry those in depth, then run the seasonal accent colours in smaller quantities with a longer replenishment horizon. Ask the mill for the minimum quantity on each colourway separately rather than a single programme figure, and ask what happens to the minimum if you consolidate two shades into one dye lot, which is sometimes possible and often reduces both cost and MOQ.
