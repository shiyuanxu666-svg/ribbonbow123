Most supplier problems in this category do not announce themselves. There is no moment at which a factory tells a buyer that its dye house is running two shifts instead of three, that its colour master has been replaced by a sample approved over email, or that its inspection records are reconstructed at the end of the month. What happens is that the third order arrives late, or the fourth order arrives in a shade nobody can explain, or a batch that fails inspection at the customer's warehouse turns out to have been inspected, and the paperwork says it passed.

Supplier auditing exists to surface those things before the deposit is paid, when switching costs are still theoretical. The discipline is not really about visiting factories. It is about asking the same structured questions of every candidate in the same order, so that the answers can be placed next to each other and compared, rather than remembered as an impression of whether somebody seemed organised.

This guide is written for sourcing managers, procurement specialists and category buyers who own decorative trim and ribbon, and for founders who are about to commit real money to an overseas manufacturer for the first time. It covers what an audit is actually trying to establish, what you can verify remotely versus what requires a visit, the specific documents worth requesting and what each one does and does not prove, how to test a capacity claim, how to verify a quality system on the floor, and the red flags that should end a supplier relationship before it starts.

It assumes a purchase from an overseas manufacturer of ribbon, bows or related decorative trim, which is the normal case for this category. The same structure works for domestic suppliers with the verification steps simplified, since the burden of verification shifts from documentary evidence to direct observation.

What a Supplier Audit Is Actually For

An audit has one objective: reduce the probability of a supply failure you did not anticipate, to a level you can live with. Everything else is method. It is worth being clear about this because supplier audits are often treated as a ritual with a familiar agenda, and the ritual produces compliance rather than information.

There are four things you actually need to know about any supplier before you commit. Can they make what you need, at the volume you need, when you need it? That is capacity and scheduling. Is what they make going to match what you approved? That is quality control and process consistency. Are the materials what they say they are, and can you prove it to your customer? That is traceability and documentation. And can you run a conversation with them when something goes wrong, which will certainly happen? That is the relationship and the communication channel.

An audit that does not produce evidence on all four is incomplete, and the most common version of incompleteness is over-weighting the first and under-weighting the last. Capacity is easy to ask about and easy to impress with. Whether a supplier will tell you bad news early is much harder to establish and much more consequential, because a supplier who hides a delay until it becomes a crisis will cost you far more over a year than one who loses you a small order.

It also helps to separate what an audit can prove from what it cannot. A good audit establishes fitness for a defined specification at a defined point in time, under a defined order pattern. It cannot establish that the supplier will still be performing this way in two years, that their quality system will survive a change of ownership or a change of production manager, or that the specific people you spoke to will still be there. This is why an audit is paired with a written agreement and a periodic re-verification rather than treated as a one-time gate.

Before the Audit: Preparation That Halves the Time

The audits that produce good information are prepared. The buyer arrives with a written specification, a question set, a document list, and a clear picture of what a good answer looks like. The audit that produces vague impressions is the one where the buyer arrives with a folder of photographs and a list of questions memorised on the flight.

Prepare four things.

The specification. Send it to the supplier in advance and ask them to quote against it and to confirm in writing that they can meet each element. A supplier who identifies a problem with your specification at the quotation stage is telling you something useful; a supplier who raises it during the audit is telling you the quotation was optimistic.

The question set. Write it down in a fixed order, so that every supplier is asked the same questions in the same sequence. This is what makes the answers comparable, and comparability is the main thing an audit buys you.

The document list. Decide in advance what you want to see, so the request is not steered by what happens to be offered.

The decision criteria. Write down what a pass looks like before you see any evidence, because the natural tendency in an audit is to accumulate positives and rationalise them. Two or three non-negotiables, decided in advance, are worth more than a long list of preferred attributes.

One practical note on language. Factory communication is typically conducted in Chinese with occasional English, and the most common failure in a remote audit is not the supplier evading a question but the question not reaching the person who can answer it. Ask questions that can be answered at the level of the plant, and where an answer requires knowledge from higher up, note that it is a follow-up item rather than accepting a vague response in the moment.

The Documents Worth Asking For, and What Each Proves

Documents are the cheapest part of an audit because they can be requested and reviewed before you travel. Not all documents are equally useful, and the useful distinction is between a document that evidences a system and a document that evidences a claim.

DocumentWhat it actually provesWhat it does not prove
Business licenceThe legal entity exists and its registered scope covers the activityThat the registered scope is the activity actually performed
Quality certificate (e.g. ISO 9001)A third party assessed a management system at a stated site and scope, at a stated timeThat the certificate covers your product, or is still current, or that the site named is the site you use
Product safety or fibre certificateThe specific material tested against a named standard for a stated scopeThat the certificate covers your colourway or your factory
Social compliance audit (e.g. BSCI, SEDEX)Conditions at a site were assessed against a defined protocol, with findings and datesThat conditions have not changed since, or that every subcontractor is covered
Test reports (fibre, colourfastness, heavy metals)The specific batch or material tested, with the method namedAnything about future production
Capacity statementA stated intention, and often a real planPhysical reality, unless reconciled with a plant walkthrough
Quality agreement and specification sheetThat both parties have agreed what the product isThat the factory can make it

The most important discipline here is verification against an independent source. Every certificate has an issuing body with a public register, and checking your supplier's certificate number on that register takes two minutes and removes the largest category of fraud in this category, which is the certificate for a different site, a different product scope, or a different company entirely. The PDF is worthless as evidence; the register entry is the evidence.

For social and environmental credentials, the check is slightly more nuanced. A social compliance audit is a snapshot with findings and a corrective action plan, and it is normal and legitimate for it to have findings, provided those findings were addressed and the report shows the follow-up. A supplier who presents a report with no findings and no date should be treated as presenting something less than what it appears to be.

Also ask for documents you might not think of. Ask for the actual specification sheet the line works to, not your own version back. Ask for a recent production batch inspection record. Ask for a customer reference in your market segment and, if they offer one, ask the customer directly.

Verifying Capacity and Lead Time

Capacity is the claim most often inflated and the one with the clearest verification method, because a plant either has the machines or it does not and a walkthrough settles it.

Start by asking for capacity in machine hours per week, not in output units. Output figures can be quoted at an ideal rate that ignores changeovers, and a factory running three different specifications on one line can show impressive theoretical capacity and have no available capacity at all for your order. Machine hours per week is a harder number to inflate and it is directly comparable between suppliers.

Then ask the question that reveals the truth indirectly: what is your bottleneck, and what is its current utilisation. A supplier who knows their own constraint can tell you that the warping line runs at ninety-two per cent while the dyeing house has slack, and that tells you where your order sits in the schedule and how much of your lead time is queueing rather than processing. A supplier who cannot name the constraint is telling you that they do not manage to a constraint, which is a different business and probably not a problem if they are a large and mature factory, but a very large problem if they are small and growing.

Now reconcile the claim against the physical plant. On a video walkthrough, count the machines. Check the condition and the age of them. Look at whether the machines are running and whether material is queued in front of them, because a line with material stacked waiting is a line at capacity, while an idle line with a stated high capacity figure is a line whose figures are theoretical. Look at the finished goods area, which is the most informative space on any factory floor, because its volume tells you the true order book in a way that no management presentation will.

For lead time, distinguish quoted lead time from achievable lead time, and test the second one by asking a question that has a known answer. Ask what the lead time was on their most recent comparable order, and when it was delivered. Or ask them to commit to a date for a small trial order and see whether they hit it, which is a cheaper test than discovering a three-week slip on a container.

Verifying the Quality System on the Floor

Quality claims are the easiest to fake and the most consequential to get wrong, because a quality failure typically surfaces at your customer's warehouse rather than at yours, and by then the cost of the failure is the whole shipment rather than a shipment fraction.

Ask four questions in sequence. What do you measure? Where in the process do you measure it? What standard do you measure it against? And what happens when it fails? A genuine system answers all four. A nominal one answers the first two and becomes vague on the third and fourth.

What is measured. In ribbon production, the meaningful measurements are width and weight across the roll, edge quality, colour against master, surface defects, and for printed ribbon, registration and print coverage. A supplier who measures only length and count is measuring the things that are easy, not the things that matter.

Where it is measured. Incoming inspection of raw material, in-process checks at defined intervals rather than only at the end, and final inspection against a written standard. Ask specifically about the first of these, because incoming inspection is where most systems are thin, and it is where a colour batch that is slightly off gets into production rather than being caught.

Against what standard. Written, agreed, and specific enough that two inspectors would reach the same conclusion. A tolerance written as "good quality" is not a standard. A tolerance written as a numerical range on a measured characteristic, with the measurement method stated, is. The method matters as much as the number, because without it the tolerance cannot be enforced at goods-inward inspection, which is the only moment it will ever be applied.

What happens on failure. There should be a documented route: quarantine, notification to you, disposition options of rework, downgrade or scrap, and a record. Ask to see a rejected batch record and what was done with it. This is the most revealing question in the whole audit, because a supplier who shows you a rejected batch and explains the disposition has demonstrated a working system, whereas a supplier who cannot produce one has told you that inspection happens but outcomes are not managed.

On the floor itself, look for the physical evidence that matches the described system. Is there incoming material held in a separate area pending inspection? Are there goods marked as quarantined? Is there a measurement instrument that appears to be in use, and does it have a calibration record? Are there rejection records visible at the line? None of this needs to be formal or sophisticated. It needs to exist.

Material Sourcing and Traceability

Material is where the value of your product lives and where the risk concentrates, because the yarn your supplier buys from a third party is upstream of your awareness and upstream of your control.

Ask who supplies the base fibre and yarn, whether it is in-house or sourced, and whether it is a consistent source or varies between batches. A supplier that buys yarn from whoever is available will produce a ribbon whose shade drifts with the substrate, which is the most common cause of colour inconsistency in this category, and it is a cause that cannot be fixed at inspection because the goods genuinely meet the specification you wrote against a different substrate.

For certified or recycled material, ask for chain of custody documentation that links the fibre in your order back to a certified source. This is a documentation chain rather than a physical one, and its absence is meaningful: certified fibre without intact chain of custody is worth nothing commercially, and a supplier who cannot produce the documentation has either not bought certified material or has not bought enough to warrant the paperwork.

Ask about incoming material inspection in the same terms as finished goods, because this is where a good supplier separates itself. Do they test incoming yarn or accept it on the mill's certificate? Do they dye in-house, and if they do, do they keep a lab dip record against the master? A dye house with a working recipe book and retained recipe samples is an asset worth paying a little more for, and a dye house that re-creates a colour from a photograph each time is a recurring cost to you that will not appear on the quotation.

Management, Communication and Language

The softest part of an audit and sometimes the most predictive. The question is not whether somebody is friendly, it is whether the communication channel works when there is a problem.

Ask who your day-to-day contact is, who the escalation contact is, and what happens operationally when a shipment is late. Note whether the person answering is the person who can act, or an intermediary. Note the response time to a written question in the days before the audit, because that response time is what you will get when a container is sitting at the port.

Ask about the production manager and how long they have been in the role. In this category, a great deal of consistency lives in the knowledge of one or two people, and if the person who holds the colour recipe and the person who holds the bow pattern both change at once, the supplier is effectively a new supplier with a good reference list. This is not a reason to disqualify anyone, but it is a reason to ask about knowledge retention, and to build a written specification and a master sample into the relationship so that the knowledge is not only in people.

Also test one real exchange during the audit, even if only a small one. Send a technical question, a week in advance, about a detail of your specification that has an unambiguous right answer. The quality of the reply tells you about the technical function behind the sales contact, which is the function that will actually solve your problems.

Red Flags That Should End the Process

Most of these are behavioural rather than documentary, which is why they only surface if the audit is done properly.

Reluctance to send a video walkthrough of the production floor. Virtual tours are normal and expected. A supplier who declines needs a reason that survives a second question.

Certificates that do not appear on the issuing body's register. This is the clearest single disqualifier in the category, and it is trivial to check.

Capacity figures that do not reconcile with the plant. If a factory shows six looms and quotes capacity implying fifteen, the quotation is not a quotation.

No inspection records, ever. Not poor records. None.

Refusing to state fibre composition in writing. Composition drives your duty classification, your compliance obligations and your material performance claims simultaneously. A supplier who will not put it in writing is asking you to carry that risk.

Price that is materially below the market. If a quotation for a standard specification comes in at a level that does not cover conversion cost, the gap will be recovered from somewhere, and it will not be recovered from margin on the next order.

Every answer delivered by a single person who never puts anyone else on the call. Organisational depth is a capacity question in disguise.

None of these is individually fatal. Together, or in combination with an inability to produce records, they mean that your risk is higher than your price advantage, and the sensible response is to move down the supplier list.

A Compact Question Set You Can Reuse

The following twenty questions, asked in this order, cover the four areas that matter and can be run over a single call. Adapt the wording, keep the order, and use them unchanged across candidates.

1. What is your legal entity name and what is the registered manufacturing scope?

2. Which certifications do you hold, for which site, for which product scope, and when do they expire?

3. Can I verify those certificate numbers on the issuing body's public register today?

4. What is your base material and fibre composition, and who supplies the yarn?

5. Do you dye, weave, print and finish in-house, and which steps are subcontracted?

6. What capacity do you have in machine hours per week, and what is your bottleneck and its utilisation?

7. How many lines do you have of each type, and what are their approximate ages?

8. What was the lead time on your most recent order of a comparable specification?

9. What do you measure, where, against what written standard, and with what method?

10. Do you inspect incoming material, and on what basis?

11. Can I see an inspection record from a recent production batch?

12. Can I see an example of a rejected batch and the disposition applied?

13. How do you handle non-conforming material, and who is notified?

14. Do you retain colour recipes and master samples, and how are they stored?

15. What is the setup cost for a new colour and for new printed artwork?

16. How long have the people who hold our colour recipes and patterns been in their roles?

17. Who is my day-to-day contact and my escalation contact?

18. What happens operationally when a shipment is late?

19. Can I place a small trial order against a committed date and have you confirm the date in writing?

20. Can you provide a customer reference in my market segment?

Ask the nineteenth question as an actual order rather than a hypothetical. It is the cheapest and most honest test of scheduling discipline available, and the answers to the other nineteen become considerably more credible once it has been answered.

Summary

An effective supplier audit is a fixed set of questions asked of every candidate in the same order, backed by documents verified against independent registers and by a walkthrough that reconciles claims against physical reality. Establish the four things that matter: capacity, consistency, traceability and communication. Use the same specification for every quotation, so the answers are comparable. Check certificates on the issuing body's register rather than accepting a PDF. Ask for capacity in machine hours and for the bottleneck by name. Test the quality system with the sequence of what, where, against what and what happens on failure, and ask to see a rejected batch. Verify fibre composition in writing and check the chain of custody where certification is claimed. Ask for a video walkthrough of the production floor and the finished goods area, and treat reluctance as information. Write your pass criteria down before the audit rather than after it. And end with the small trial order against a committed date, because it is the only question that cannot be answered with a rehearsed answer.
