Most ribbon programmes do not fail on the fabric. They fail on the paperwork between the factory and the warehouse — the approvals that get skipped when a programme is busy, the sample that is signed off in a hurry, and the lot that ships four months later that nobody has ever formally released. This guide is about that middle layer: the approval discipline that sits between a signed development brief and a carton arriving at your dock.

We call the process sample-to-production release. It is a small, unglamorous discipline that most brand buyers already half-practice, but that almost nobody has written down. Written down, it becomes a control that protects you in the three moments that matter: when a colour drifts, when a delivery is short, and when a customer asks for evidence.

Why Approvals Drift

Approvals drift for four reasons, and none of them is incompetence.

Time pressure. A launch date moves, a retail slot is fixed, and a sample that was going to be reviewed properly gets reviewed in a Thursday-afternoon video call with three people on it. Nobody signs anything, so the programme advances on tacit approval. That approval is not evidence, and it will not survive a dispute.

Sample confusion. A factory sends a sample. The buyer approves it. Then the factory asks whether it is the development sample or the bulk sample, and nobody can say. If the two are not explicitly separated in your brief, the colour you approved on a different substrate may not be the colour that ships.

Drift without a re-approval trigger. Dye lots change. Yarn suppliers change. Finishing equipment changes. A line that produced an approved lot in March may produce a visibly different lot in September — and if your agreement has no re-approval trigger, the factory is technically compliant while your product quietly degrades.

No retained evidence. The approved sample goes into a drawer. Two years later, when a customer escalates, nobody can prove what was approved, when, or against which physical sample. You are left arguing from a photograph in an email.

Every one of these failures is cheap to prevent and expensive to fix. A written approval procedure costs a page of your specification and a few hours of your team's time.

The Four-Stage Approval Ladder

We use a four-stage ladder because each stage answers a different question, and the questions are not interchangeable.

StageThe question it answersWhat it physically isWho signs
1. Lab dip / formulationIs the colour direction right?A dye trial on the proposed fibre and finish, unbleached edge visibleDesigner or colour approver
2. Development sampleDoes the construction, width, finish and hand feel right?The actual construction, finished as a prototype, not a swatchProduct or technical manager
3. Pre-production sampleWill the production line reproduce stage 2?Made on production equipment from the actual production lot, full widthQuality plus product, jointly
4. Pre-shipment releaseDoes the packed lot match what was released in stage 3?The packed carton contents, checked against the stage 3 referenceQuality, against a written release note

The single most common failure is collapsing stage 1 into stage 2. A lab dip tells you about colour. It tells you nothing about how the ribbon will drape, how the edge will behave, or how the print will sit on it. If you approve colour on a lab dip and then complain about the finished product, you are complaining about something you never approved.

The second most common failure is skipping stage 3 entirely. The factory produces a bulk lot, you inspect it, you find it acceptable, and the programme moves on — but nobody has established that the production line can reproduce what the development sample showed. Stage 3 is what catches a machine change, a yarn substitution, or a dye-lot drift before it reaches a customer.

What a Development Sample Must Prove

A development sample is only useful if it proves the things you cannot check on a swatch. Before approving it, work through this list explicitly:

  • Width and construction. Measure across the ribbon at three points, not one. Check the selvage and the edge for fraying or yarn pull-out. Ribbon that measures correctly at the head of the roll and poorly at the tail is a loom-set problem, and it is a common one.
  • Hand and drape. Fold it, tie it, let it hang. A ribbon that feels right in the hand but collapses on a gift box is the wrong weight for the job, and you will only discover this on the shelf.
  • Colour under the light you actually sell in. A sample approved under a factory fluorescent tube will not necessarily match under retail lighting or on a phone camera. Approve under at least two light sources, one of which is daylight-equivalent.
  • Surface and finish. For velvet and flock, check pile direction and rub. For satin and organza, check sheen and whether the organza is crisp or limp. For printed ribbon, check the decoration against the base fabric, not in isolation.
  • Decoration registration and adhesion. If the ribbon is printed, screen-printed, hot-transferred or foiled, the development sample must carry the actual decoration. A printed sample on an undecorated construction proves nothing about print adhesion, registration or coverage.
  • Wound form. Ask for the ribbon wound on a representative core at a representative length. How it arrives affects how you store it, how you convert it and how much waste you generate at the slitting station.

Two practical notes. First, always request a length that lets you cut a full application — a bow, a gift box closure, a handle wrap — rather than a 50 cm swatch. Many specification failures only appear when the ribbon is under load. Second, request more than one sample in the same delivery, ideally from different positions on the same roll. A single sample tells you about one point in the roll; two samples tell you about the run.

Counter Samples: The Step Most Buyers Skip

Here is the part that changes the relationship with your factory.

A counter sample is the sample the factory retains. It is made from the same lot, at the same time, by the same machine as the sample you receive — but it is the factory's copy, kept under controlled conditions, and it becomes the physical reference for every future dispute.

Why it matters: colour disputes are almost always a memory dispute. The buyer says “this is not the shade we approved”, the factory says “we produced the approved shade”, and there is no evidence either way. With a counter sample in a sealed bag with a signed label — lot number, date, machine, operator, dye batch, and the names of everyone who approved it — the conversation becomes factual. Place it next to the delivered goods and the question answers itself.

The counter sample also serves three further purposes that most buyers do not think about:

  • Re-approval reference. When you change a decoration, a dye lot, or a substrate and need to re-approve, the counter sample from the previous approved lot is your baseline — it is what “no change” is measured against.
  • Batch-to-batch reference. When a repeat order arrives, the counter sample from the last shipment tells you whether the new lot is continuing the standard or drifting from it, before you accept delivery.
  • Dispute evidence. A retained, signed counter sample is documentary evidence that survives a conversation with a customer. It is materially stronger than an email thread.

Ask for it in the specification. Specify the bagging format, the label fields, and the retention period — we recommend a minimum of three years, longer for a colour you consider core. Ask for a count of retained samples on request, with the lot list. A factory that cannot produce its retained-sample register on demand is telling you something important about its record-keeping.

Re-Approval Triggers: Write Them Into the Specification

An approval that never expires is not an approval; it is an assumption. Re-approval triggers are the clauses that turn tacit acceptance back into a controlled process.

These are the triggers we recommend writing into any ribbon specification:

  • Dye lot or yarn batch change. Any change to the dyehouse batch or the fibre supplier triggers a new lab dip and, if the colour is in the top tier of your tolerance, a new stage 2 sample.
  • Production site or line change. Moving production to a different loom, a different weaving house, or a different finishing line triggers a stage 3 pre-production sample.
  • Material substitution. Any change to the base fibre, the yarn count, the weave construction or the width tolerance triggers a full stage 2 re-approval. This is the clause that protects you when a raw material becomes unavailable mid-season.
  • Decoration method change. Switching from screen print to heat transfer, or changing the foil supplier, changes the hand, the coverage and the cost. It triggers a decorated sample approval, not just a colour check.
  • Annual re-confirmation. A written re-confirmation once per year, attached to the current retained sample. It closes the loop on a standard that was approved two years ago by a team that no longer exists.
  • Tolerance breach. If an AQL inspection on a lot records a failure, the next lot of that SKU is a stage 3 sample rather than a routine delivery, until a clean run is recorded.

Write these as obligations, not as suggestions, and give each one a named owner and a time limit. “The factory shall submit a lab dip for approval within five working days of any notified dye lot change” is enforceable in a way that “please advise us of significant changes” is not.

The Production Release: What Ships Without Your Signature Does Not Ship

The final stage is the one most often skipped, and the one with the clearest commercial consequence.

A production release is a written statement, issued before the goods leave the factory, that the packed lot matches the stage 3 pre-production sample and the written specification. It carries the lot number, the roll count, the total metreage, the AQL inspection result, the colour-difference readings, and the name of the person who released it.

The reason to insist on it is simple: once the goods ship, your leverage collapses. A claim against a shipment that has already arrived at your dock, in your warehouse, and has already been split across three customer programmes, is a negotiation. A claim raised against a release note, before the goods leave the factory, is a decision the factory will make in your favour because it is cheaper for them to do so.

We recommend the release note travels with the shipment as a document pack, and that a copy is retained in your own system against the PO and the lot number. When the repeat order comes six months later, the release note from the previous run tells you what the standard actually was, rather than what you think it was.

Building the Procedure Into Your Own Specification

None of this needs a large quality system. It needs six fields in your ribbon specification, and a short internal rule about who signs what.

Field one: the stage list. Name the four stages and state which material each one produces.

Field two: the approval authority. Write down who may sign a stage 2 and who may sign a stage 3, and whether design approval and quality approval must both be present at stage 3. Ambiguity here is what produces skipped approvals.

Field three: the counter-sample requirement. State that the factory retains a counter sample from each approved lot, specify the bagging and labelling, state the retention period, and require a retained-sample register on request.

Field four: the re-approval triggers. List the six triggers above, adapted to your programme, with a named owner and a time limit on the factory's response.

Field five: the release-note requirement. State that no lot ships without a signed production release note, and list the fields it must contain.

Field six: the evidence-retention rule. State how long you keep the release notes, the inspection reports and the retained samples, and who owns the archive.

Two practical disciplines make the difference between a procedure that exists and a procedure that works. Have one person own it. A named person in your organisation who holds the master specification, the approval log and the archive. If nobody owns it, the specification becomes a PDF that drifts from reality within two seasons. And review the approval log quarterly. Look at how many samples were approved, how long each stage took, how many re-approvals were triggered, and how many releases were issued without a sample. Those four numbers tell you where your programme is actually losing control.

What to Ask a Factory

If you are qualifying a new supplier, these are the questions that reveal whether the release discipline exists, without needing to visit the site:

  1. Show me a retained-sample register for the last twelve months. Which lots have counter samples, where are they stored, and who has access to them?
  2. Show me a production release note for a recent shipment. What does it contain, and who signs it?
  3. What happens to your approval when the dye lot changes? Do you re-submit a lab dip every time, or only when the difference is visible to the eye?
  4. How long do you keep an approved lab dip, and can you retrieve one for a lot we shipped two years ago?
  5. If we reject a lot at our dock, what evidence do you need from us, and how long does the claim take to resolve?
  6. What is your re-approval lead time? If we ask for a new stage 3 sample today, when do we receive it?

A factory that answers all six precisely, with paperwork, is a factory with a release process. A factory that answers all six vaguely is telling you that its quality system lives in people's heads rather than in documents — and that your programme will absorb the difference every time something changes.

The Real Payoff

None of this makes the ribbon better. It makes the ribbon consistent, which is what actually protects a brand programme.

Consider what the approval discipline buys you in three situations. When a customer complains about a shade, you have a counter sample and a release note, so the conversation is about evidence rather than memory, and it resolves in days rather than weeks. When a repeat order arrives, you have the previous release note, so you can confirm the standard without re-negotiating it from scratch — and you can check the new lot against the retained sample before accepting it. And when a factory change happens mid-season, you have written triggers, so the change arrives as a sample for approval rather than as a surprise in a carton.

The commercial effect compounds. Reduced claims handling, fewer expedite shipments, fewer emergency re-approvals, and a supply base that can absorb change without the programme stopping. That is the difference between buying ribbon and running a ribbon programme.

Most importantly, it changes what you can say. When a large retail customer asks for evidence — and they increasingly do — the answer is not “I think it should be fine”. The answer is a release note, an inspection report, a retained sample and a named approver. In a category where the product is visually trivial and the margin is thin, being able to prove your standard is quietly one of the more valuable things you own.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand buyers, product managers and quality teams most often ask us about approval discipline in ribbon programmes: how many sample stages are really needed, what a counter sample is and why it matters, which production changes should trigger a fresh sample, and what evidence to request when a customer escalates a complaint. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between a lab dip, a development sample and a pre-production sample?

A lab dip proves colour only: it is a dye trial showing the shade on the proposed fibre and finish, and it tells you nothing about drape, width, edge or how a print will sit on it. A development sample is the actual construction finished as a prototype, and it proves width, hand, finish and decoration. A pre-production sample is made on the production equipment from the actual production lot, and it proves the line can reproduce what the development sample showed. Collapsing the first two is the most common specification error buyers make, because they approve colour on a swatch and then complain about the finished product.

2. What is a counter sample and why should my factory keep one?

A counter sample is the sample the factory retains from the same lot, made at the same time and on the same machine as the sample you receive, kept under controlled conditions and sealed in a labelled bag. It is the physical reference for every future dispute. Colour disagreements are almost always memory disagreements, and a retained, signed counter sample turns that conversation from opinion into evidence: put it next to the delivered goods and the question answers itself. Specify the bagging format, label fields and retention period in your purchase specification, and require a retained-sample register on demand.

3. Which production changes should trigger a fresh sample approval?

At minimum: a dye lot or yarn batch change, a change of production site or line, any material substitution including base fibre, yarn count, weave construction or width tolerance, a change of decoration method such as screen print to heat transfer, and an annual written re-confirmation of the standard. A tolerance breach on an incoming lot should also move the next lot to pre-production sample status until a clean run is recorded. Write these as obligations with a named owner and a time limit rather than as requests for courtesy notification.

4. What should a production release note contain before goods leave the factory?

At minimum the lot number, roll count, total metreage, the AQL inspection result, the colour-difference readings against the approved reference, the name and date of the person releasing it, and the reference to the pre-production sample the lot was matched against. The commercial reason for requiring it is leverage: a claim raised before the goods ship is a decision the factory makes in your favour, whereas a claim raised after arrival, once the goods are in three customer programmes, is only a negotiation.

5. Do I need all four approval stages for every ribbon SKU?

No, and a sensible specification will scale them. Stage 1 and stage 2 are essential for any new colour or new construction. Stage 3 is essential for a new programme, for any change that touches material or equipment, and for anything on your critical path. Stage 4, the production release, should apply to every lot that ships on a specification you own, because it costs the factory one document and it is what you will use the first time something goes wrong. The point is not to add stages but to make it explicit which stages apply to which SKU.

6. How long should a ribbon supplier keep approved samples and production records?

We recommend a minimum of three years for retained counter samples and release notes, and longer for colours or SKUs you treat as core to the programme. Consumer-facing programmes and some home-goods categories carry claim exposure well beyond one season, so three years is a practical floor rather than a generous target. Specify the retention period in the purchase agreement so it is an obligation rather than a courtesy, and state who owns the archive on your side so the evidence does not disappear when a buyer leaves.

7. What evidence should I ask for when a customer escalates a colour or quality complaint?

In order: the production release note for the lot, the AQL inspection report and colour-difference readings, the retained counter sample from that lot, and the stage 3 pre-production sample the lot was released against. That set lets you establish whether the delivered lot was released to an agreed standard and whether it matches that standard on the shelf. It also tells you whether the issue is a lot-specific escape or a systematic problem, which determines whether you are claiming one shipment or raising a process issue with the supplier.

8. How do I stop approvals from being skipped when the programme gets busy?

Three controls. First, name the people who may sign each stage in writing, so a launch date pressure cannot manufacture an approval. Second, make the production release note a precondition of shipment rather than a post-hoc document, because a release that is required before dispatch cannot be skipped the way an approval can. Third, review the approval log quarterly and look at four numbers: samples approved, time per stage, re-approvals triggered, and releases issued without a corresponding sample. Those four numbers show you where the programme is actually losing control, rather than where you assume it is.
