A unit price tells you what the mill charges for the ribbon. It does not tell you what the ribbon costs to own. Those two numbers are frequently very far apart, and the gap is where most private label programmes quietly lose money — not in the negotiation, but in the cost of things that were never counted.

Most buyers understand this in principle. Very few build a model. The typical approach is to compare two quotes on unit price, pick the lower one, order, and discover the difference later when the shipment is short, the approval is late, the cartons are undersized, or the freight rate moved. By then the cost has already landed and it is difficult to attribute.

This guide is about building the model properly, so that supplier comparison happens on the same basis and so that the true cost of an option is visible before you commit. It is written for procurement managers and brand owners who need a defensible number rather than an intuition.

Why the Quoted Price Is the Least Interesting Number

A quote for custom ribbon typically contains a unit price that already assumes several things: a standard packaging configuration, a standard lead time, a payment term of some assumed length, a certain quality level, and a certain level of buyer involvement in the artwork and colour process. Change any of those assumptions and the same factory will quote a different price. Change several and the "cheapest" quote can become the most expensive.

Two factories quoting 0.42 and 0.47 per metre for the same printed grosgrain may be quoting genuinely different products, or they may be quoting the same product under different assumptions about packaging, setup cost recovery, defect liability and delivery terms. Without unpacking both quotes to the same assumptions, the comparison is meaningless. The lower number is not necessarily the lower cost, and the higher number is not necessarily the better value.

This is the first discipline of landed cost modelling: normalise the quotes before you compare them. Every quote must be restated under a common set of assumptions about specification, packaging, quantity break, incoterm, payment terms, defect allowance and lead time. Only then does the comparison carry information.

The Cost Components That Sit Outside the Quote

The hidden costs in a ribbon programme fall into a small number of families. Knowing which family a problem belongs to tells you who can fix it.

Material and specification costs

The mill price covers yarn, dyeing, weaving, finishing and conversion. It does not cover the decisions that change the material cost: upgrading to a better polyester, adding recycled content and its associated certification, specifying a narrower or wider width than the standard line, requesting a wider colour tolerance than the mill normally holds, or a construction the standard line does not run.

The trap is that these often appear as separate charges, so they do not get compared between suppliers. A supplier who quotes 0.40 with 0.04 of specification premiums is not necessarily cheaper than one who quotes 0.46 inclusive. Normalise both to a single delivered specification before comparing.

Setup and development costs

New artwork, new colour development, new dies or screens, new tooling and new documentation all carry one-off costs. These are frequently amortised into the unit price without being shown, which means the first order carries the whole development cost and the reorder appears to be cheaper. That is not a price reduction; it is the amortisation showing up.

Do the opposite: strip development out of the unit price and look at the programme as a whole. Development cost divided across the expected total volume of the programme gives an amortised figure that belongs in the model, and the underlying number tells you whether the programme is worth launching at all.

Logistics costs

Freight is quoted by volume, and volume is a function of how you pack. A mill that ships 20 metre rolls in a 0.6 cubic metre carton creates a different freight cost per metre than the same mill shipping 100 metre rolls in a denser format — sometimes the difference is 30 to 40 percent of the freight line, which is significant on a low-value, high-volume product.

Beyond the freight rate itself there are cargo dimension charges, container utilisation losses, and the cost of a shipment that arrives and must be rehandled because it does not match the receiving dock's expectations. Ask for the carton specification and the cubic volume per order, then compute freight per metre at the actual shipping weight and cube. Ask also whether the mill can offer consolidated shipping across your orders or against your schedule.

Cost of capital and payment terms

Payment terms are a real cost and are routinely ignored. Paying at 30% deposit and 70% before shipment, or on bill of lading copy, is materially different from paying 30 days after arrival, and the difference is a genuine financing cost that belongs in the model.

A practical way to express it: discount the cash flow. Take the quoted price, subtract the value of early payment at your cost of funds, and you have the economic price. For a large brand with a working capital cost of 8 percent, moving from 50 percent advance and 50 percent before shipment to 30 days after arrival is worth roughly 1.5 to 2 percent of the order value — a real number that should appear in a supplier comparison, not in a negotiation conversation you remember afterwards.

Quality and delivery costs

These are the hardest to estimate in advance and therefore the most often omitted. A defect rate of 1.5 percent on a 50,000 metre order is 750 metres of scrap, rework or credit — and if the defect is discovered at your customer's receiving dock rather than at the mill's inspection, the cost multiplies because you have freight, customs, sorting and a customer conversation attached to it.

Estimate defect cost from your own history if you have it, and if you do not, ask the factory what its own outgoing rejection rate is and for what period. Then convert that into a cost per order and add it to the model. The point is not that the number will be exact. The point is that a supplier claiming a zero defect rate and one claiming 1.2 percent should not be compared on unit price alone.

Delivery failures cost more than the ribbon. A missed date on a seasonal programme means air freight, or a missed retail window, or a promotion that runs without product. Set a delivery cost estimate — even a rough one — and let it inform your buffer decision.

Administrative and management costs

Every programme consumes buyer and supplier time: sample rounds, artwork revisions, inspection coordination, claim handling, reconciliation. These rarely get counted, and they are where small programmes become expensive. A 3,000 metre first order with three sample rounds and a colour rejection may consume more internal hours than a 30,000 metre order with an established specification and no deviations.

Count them roughly. If a programme requires 40 hours of internal coordination and you cost internal time at an hourly rate, that is a real number. It also tells you something operationally valuable: a programme that is administratively expensive is usually a programme with unclear specifications, and fixing the specification is cheaper than absorbing the coordination cost forever.

Building the Model

You do not need a spreadsheet with fifty rows. You need a model that is consistent between options and honest about what you do not know. A working version has six blocks: material and conversion, development amortised across programme volume, logistics, working capital, expected quality and delivery cost, and internal coordination. Then you express the total as a cost per unit or per order.

Three practices keep it useful.

Separate the facts from the assumptions. Facts are the quoted unit price, the carton dimensions, the payment terms, the lead time, the incoterm. Assumptions are the defect rate, the internal hourly cost, the probability of a late delivery, the amortisation volume. Mark them clearly in the model. When a supplier argues with your number, you want to know instantly whether they are disputing a fact or an assumption.

Test the sensitivity, not just the total. Most models are wrong somewhere; the useful question is which assumption would have to be wrong for the ranking to change. If the answer is "a defect rate of 4 percent instead of 1 percent would flip the comparison", you know exactly where to focus your due diligence. If the answer is "the freight rate would have to double", you can stop worrying about freight and focus on something else.

Keep it versioned and comparable. A model that is rebuilt from scratch for each new quote loses its value, because you cannot compare a model built today against one built last quarter with different assumptions. Reuse the same structure and change only the inputs.

Reading a Quote Properly

The quote itself is a document with information in it beyond the number. A good quote states the specification in enough detail to reproduce the product: construction, width, composition, colour reference, finish, print method and number of colours, core type, winding, roll length, packaging, tolerance statements and any exclusions. A quote that cannot be used to reconstruct the product is not a quote; it is an indication, and it should not be compared with one that is.

Check the exclusions list carefully. Standard exclusions include colour matching beyond a stated tolerance, testing not listed, samples, tooling amortisation over a quantity below the stated break, and delivery outside standard packing. Each exclusion is a cost that will reappear later. Two quotes at the same unit price with different exclusions can differ by a meaningful percentage in delivered reality.

Look at the price breaks and ask what changes between them. If the price at 10,000 metres is only 4 percent below the price at 5,000, the break is weak and you may be paying for setup amortisation rather than scale. Ask what the mill would need to change to earn a better break, and whether there is a construction or packaging change that would make the volume economically attractive to them.

Finally, check how the quote treats defects. A quote with a stated defect allowance of 0.5 percent has made a commercial commitment that will appear in your model. A quote that says nothing about defects leaves you carrying 100 percent of the cost. Ask for it in writing, even if the answer is that the mill stands behind its AQL inspection with replacement of rejected units — that is a commitment and it should be priced.

What the Model Changes About the Decision

The value of a landed cost model is not the number it produces. It is that it changes what you negotiate and what you watch.

It changes what you negotiate. Once development cost is separated from unit price, you can negotiate it directly — asking for a higher first-order volume in exchange for absorbing setup, or asking whether a different material specification would remove the premium entirely. Once logistics are visible per metre, carton format becomes a negotiation topic rather than an operational detail. Once defect cost is in the model, the inspection regime becomes a business decision with a price.

It changes what you watch. A programme whose model is 78 percent material and conversion can absorb a freight increase, a payment term shift or a modest defect rate without changing supplier preference. A programme whose model is 45 percent material and 25 percent logistics and 15 percent quality cost is fragile, and should be structured with more contractual protection or a second source even if the current supplier looks best on paper.

It changes what you scale. When you expand a programme, you know which cost elements fall with volume and which rise. Material conversion cost falls with volume. Logistics falls with volume only if the packaging format is optimised. Quality cost is usually flat per order but flat across a much larger volume, so it grows as a share. Administrative cost per order falls but does not disappear. Knowing this in advance means you know which benefit you are actually buying when you commit to a larger minimum, and you can test whether the mill's volume incentive is aimed at the cost element that matters most to you.

Where Buyers Get It Wrong

Optimising the wrong component. Chasing 3 percent off a unit price that is 70 percent of total cost, while ignoring a carton format that adds 8 percent to freight, is negative-value negotiating effort. Weight your effort by the size of the component.

Treating a low quote as risk. A quoted price far below the market is a signal to ask more questions, not to celebrate. Sometimes it reflects genuine scale and a long production run; sometimes it reflects a thin specification that will be revised upwards later; sometimes it reflects a first order that is being quoted below cost to establish a relationship. You cannot tell from the number, which is exactly why the number alone is not the analysis.

Modelling the average instead of the realistic case. Quote averages assume the programme runs exactly as planned, which is optimistic in a way that is easy to miss. Model a realistic case alongside the planned case, with a modest defect allowance, a realistic sample count, and the shipment configuration you will actually use.

Rebuilding the model for every quote. This is the most common failure and it destroys the value of the exercise. If every comparison starts from a new template, you can never tell whether the supplier changed or your assumptions changed. Reuse the structure.

Making It Routine

The model earns its keep when it is used without heroics. Build it once per category, keep it as a template with your standard assumptions, and run it for every new quote. Review the assumptions quarterly rather than per programme, because defect rates, freight indices and your own internal hourly cost change slowly and rarely need per-order attention.

Keep the output visible. A one-page summary per programme — the total cost per unit, the ranked cost drivers, the three assumptions the result is most sensitive to — is what a category manager or finance reviewer actually needs. The full model is the working document; the summary is the decision document.

And review results against reality after each programme closes. Compare the modelled cost with the actual invoiced cost, the actual freight, the actual defect and claim cost, and the actual hours spent. A model that has never been checked against outcomes is a set of beliefs, not a tool. The first time you find a 4 percent gap between modelled and actual is also the first time you find out which assumption was wrong.

The Number Worth Trusting

A landed cost model does not give you certainty. It gives you a defensible comparison and, more usefully, a clear view of where your programme's cost actually lives. That view is what lets you negotiate well, structure the programme intelligently and know which risks are worth paying to remove.

The unit price on the quote is the number everyone discusses. It is also, in most private label ribbon programmes, one of the smaller determinants of total cost. The programme that succeeds over three years is rarely the one that found the lowest quoted price; it is the one that knew what its costs were made of.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions procurement managers, finance reviewers and brand owners most often ask us about ribbon landed cost, quote comparison and total cost of ownership. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between unit price and landed cost for a ribbon program?

Unit price is what the mill charges per metre or per piece for the product itself. Landed cost is everything required to make that product available and usable: the unit price plus development and tooling amortised across programme volume, freight and duty to your destination, packaging and container utilisation, payment terms and the working capital they create, expected defect and rework cost, the cost of delivery failures, and the internal coordination time the programme consumes. For most private label ribbon programs, the unit price accounts for somewhere between 55 and 75 percent of total cost, which means a quote comparison on unit price alone ignores a quarter or more of the real economics.

2. How should I compare two ribbon quotes that have different packaging, payment terms and lead times?

Normalise both to the same assumptions before comparing. Restate each quote under a common specification (construction, width, composition, print, colour tolerance, packaging, core and winding), a common quantity break, a common incoterm, a common payment term, a common lead time and a common defect allowance. Then compute a single delivered cost per unit for each. Also strip development and setup cost out of the unit price and amortise it separately across expected programme volume, so that the first order and the reorder are comparable. Without this normalisation, a lower unit price frequently reflects looser assumptions rather than a better price.

3. Should I include the cost of internal staff time in a landed cost comparison?

Yes, and it is frequently more decisive than buyers expect. Count the hours your team will spend on sample rounds, artwork revisions, inspection coordination, claim handling and reconciliation, and apply your internal cost rate. A small first order with three sample rounds and a colour rejection often consumes more internal hours than a much larger order with an established specification. The number also carries an operational message: an administratively expensive program is usually one with unclear specifications, so reducing the specification ambiguity is cheaper than absorbing the coordination cost indefinitely.

4. How do I value payment terms in a supplier comparison?

Treat the difference as a financing cost and discount the cash flow. Compute what an advance payment or shipment-stage payment is worth at your cost of funds compared with the net-terms alternative, and subtract that from the quoted price to get the economic price. Moving from 50 percent advance with 50 percent before shipment to payment 30 days after arrival is typically worth roughly 1.5 to 2 percent of order value for a large brand, which is meaningful on high-volume programs and routinely omitted from comparisons. Negotiating payment terms on the same factory with the same volume is one of the cheaper levers available to you.

5. What should I ask a supplier to include in a quotation so that it can be compared properly?

Ask for a specification detailed enough to reproduce the product: construction, width, composition, colour reference, finish, print method and colour count, core type, winding and roll length, packaging and carton configuration, tolerance statements, and an explicit list of exclusions. Also ask for the price breaks, the stated defect allowance or warranty position, the payment terms, the incoterm, the quoted lead time for a first order and a repeat, and the development and tooling costs shown separately. A quote that cannot be used to reconstruct the product is an indication rather than a quotation and should not be compared against one that can.

6. How do I know if a quoted unit price is unrealistically low?

Treat a price significantly below the market range as a question rather than an opportunity, and check four things: whether the specification behind the number is the same as the one you need, whether development cost has been absorbed into the unit price so the reorder will look more expensive, whether the volume assumption matches what you will actually order, and whether the first order is being quoted below cost to win the relationship. Low prices at the start of a relationship are common and legitimate when the volume case is real. They become a problem when the programme reaches reorder and the price resets upward without warning, which is exactly what separating development cost from unit price in your model will reveal.

7. How often should I update the landed cost model assumptions?

Quarterly is a reasonable cadence, and per-programme review of the assumptions is not necessary because they move slowly. The assumptions worth watching are freight and duty indices, your internal coordination cost rate, the defect rate for each supplier and construction, and the amortisation volume used for development recovery. What matters more than the frequency is that you use the same template across every programme and every supplier, so that changes in the model are attributable to a real change rather than to a rebuilt spreadsheet. After each programme closes, compare modelled against actual invoiced, freight, claim and labour costs; the first material gap you find is usually where your assumptions are weakest.
