Why Two Quotes for "The Same Ribbon" Differ by 30 Percent

Ask four mills for a quote on the same item — a 20 mm printed grosgrain bow, 5,000 pieces, delivered to Rotterdam — and you will likely receive four numbers that differ by more than 30 percent in either direction. If you pick the lowest, you may be choosing a factory that has misunderstood the specification. If you pick the highest, you may have overpaid for a promise that was never commercially necessary.

The gap is almost never a matter of one factory being dishonest. It comes from the fact that a quotation is not a price for a product. It is a price for a product plus a set of assumptions, and two suppliers will rarely assume the same things. The seven assumptions that move the number most often are these:

  • Material construction. Polyester grosgrain versus a polyester/cotton blend with a different weft density. Different yarn counts change both cost and drape.
  • Decoration method. Screen print, heat-transfer film, foil stamping and digital printing have entirely different setup costs, per-piece costs and minimum order quantities.
  • Colour treatment. Stock colours carry no development charge. A Pantone match for a colour outside the mill's stock range carries a dye-lot setup charge and sometimes a colour-hold fee.
  • Finish and format. Winding onto reels, cut to length, folded into bow shapes, or converted into finished trim sets with backing cards.
  • Quality evidence. Whether OEKO-TEX testing, a specific colour tolerance, batch-level colour records or a third-party inspection are included or charged as extras.
  • Packaging specification. Polybag per bow, header card, barcode labelling, carton configuration and pallet optimisation all change the unit cost of the delivery, not of the ribbon.
  • Commercial terms. Incoterm, payment schedule, whether tooling and dies are amortised into the price or charged separately, and what happens to the price at 10,000 pieces versus 50,000.

Every experienced buyer eventually learns the same lesson: you cannot compare quotations until you have made their assumptions identical. That means writing a specification detailed enough that each factory prices the same object, and then reading each response for the places where the factory has quietly priced something you did not ask for.

OEM, ODM, Private Label: Which Engagement Are You Actually Buying?

These three terms are used interchangeably in most supplier conversations, and they describe genuinely different scopes of work. Being clear about which one you want is the cheapest decision you will make in the whole programme, because it determines who carries the design risk, who owns the tooling, and how long development takes.

OEM — Original Equipment Manufacturing

You provide the design, the specification, the artwork and the colour reference. The factory manufactures to your brief. This is the most common model in the ribbon and trim industry and it suits brands that already have a strong visual identity and want manufacturing capacity rather than design help. In an OEM engagement the mill owns the equipment, not your design: dies, screens, printing plates and moulds remain the mill's property unless you negotiate otherwise.

ODM — Original Design Manufacturing

The factory contributes design capability. A textile mill with in-house pattern development, in-house bow assembly and in-house print development can propose construction, propose colourways and propose structure, and you choose what to adopt. ODM is slower to start and faster to iterate. It works well when you are entering a category and do not yet know what the product should look like, and it works badly when you have a precise brand aesthetic that you do not want diluted by a supplier's house style.

Private Label

Private label is not a manufacturing model so much as a commercial one. Your name appears on the product, the customer buys your brand, and the supply chain underneath is invisible to them. You may combine private label with OEM or ODM production; the two questions are separate. What private label changes is the stakes around minimum order quantity, because the economics of a private label programme are driven by how many units you can move through your own channels before the next reorder, not by how much you can afford to place with the mill.

Working rule: specify the engagement type in your brief, in one line, in plain language. "This is an OEM engagement. We supply final artwork, Pantone references and construction details. We request a quotation for manufacturing only." One sentence removes a week of ambiguous email in either direction.

How the OEM Process Actually Runs

There is a predictable sequence underneath every ribbon OEM programme, whether the supplier calls it a 9-stage process or a 22-stage process. The stages differ in granularity; the underlying logic does not. Knowing the sequence tells you which question to ask at which moment, and — more usefully — which questions you have skipped when the factory starts asking odd questions.

Stage 1 — Enquiry and feasibility

The factory asks what you want to make, roughly how much, and when you need it. A competent supplier will ask about application, target retail price and required certification here. If they only ask for quantity and size, you are dealing with a price-comparison exercise, not a partner.

Stage 2 — Specification and development brief

This is where the work becomes real. Construction, material, width, tolerance, decoration method, colour reference, certification requirements, format, packaging, Incoterm, required-on-shelf date and artwork ownership all get written down. The output of this stage is a document both sides can point at for the rest of the programme.

Stage 3 — Quotation

The factory prices the specification and returns a quotation with a breakdown. A useful quotation separates one-time costs (tooling, dies, screens, colour development, sample charges) from recurring costs (unit price, packaging, freight) and states the volume band the unit price applies to.

Stage 4 — Artwork and colour development

Your artwork is converted into the factory's production format. For printed ribbon this means separating the design into the specific spot colours that will be printed, each of which needs its own screen or plate. If your design has nine colours, that is nine screens and a different unit cost structure than a two-colour design. Pantone references are matched to the mill's dye or ink range, and a lab dip or printed proof is produced.

Stage 5 — Sampling

Samples are where programmes succeed or die. A pre-production sample proves the construction, colour and print. A pre-shipment sample proves the production lot. They are not interchangeable, and a buyer who approves a pre-production sample and then rejects the production lot for a difference the pre-production sample should have shown has lost a month of lead time. We have covered the distinction in detail elsewhere on this site.

Stage 6 — Approval and booking

Written approval of the marked sample, the colour standard and the specification, followed by a deposit and a booked production slot. Everything changes at this point: capacity, price validity and delivery date are now commitments rather than estimates.

Stage 7 — Production

Weaving, dyeing, finishing, printing, cutting, assembly and packing. During this stage the useful questions are about status and about evidence — batch records, colour measurement data, test certificates — rather than about progress percentages.

Stage 8 — Inspection

Inspection against an agreed standard and sampling plan, usually AQL-based, before the goods leave the factory. If you have not agreed the standard in writing, this stage becomes an argument about intent rather than a measurement.

Stage 9 — Packing, shipping and documentation

Carton marking, shipping documents, certificates, the export declaration and, if you use a third-party inspector, their report. A well-run factory treats this stage as part of the product because for many importers it determines whether the container clears without a delay.

Planning MOQ, Lead Time and the First Reorder

Minimum order quantity is a negotiation, not a fact

Published MOQs are usually set to protect the mill from changeover cost, not to reflect the true economic floor. A mill running an 8 mm printed organza will often accept a smaller run than a mill running a custom jacquard weave, because the setup differs entirely. Your realistic levers are volume, number of colours, number of SKUs, format and payment terms. A single SKU at 2,000 pieces is harder for the mill than three SKUs at 3,000 pieces each. A reel format is easier than a cut-and-folded trim set. These are the levers to move before you start asking for a discount.

Lead time has three parts, and buyers only count one

  • Pre-production lead time — the period from deposit to production start. This is where setup, dyeing, screen making and scheduling sit. It is typically 2 to 4 weeks for a printed programme with stock material, longer when a new colour must be developed.
  • Production lead time — weaving, dyeing, printing, finishing and assembly. This scales with volume and with mill load. In peak season for gift and holiday trim, the same programme that ran 15 days in February runs 35 days in October.
  • Transit and clearance — ocean freight is 4 to 7 weeks from South China to Europe, 3 to 5 weeks to North America, plus customs clearance and inland delivery. Buyers who quote "35 days" as the total lead time are describing only the second of the three parts.

Budget a realistic first cycle of 10 to 14 weeks for a new programme from brief to goods on shelf in Europe, and plan the second order before the first ships. Because of the peak-season effect, the most reliable launch window for a holiday or gift programme is a production slot booked in the first half of the year, not the second.

The first reorder is where private label economics are decided

Private label programmes usually have a predictable shape: a development order to test the market, then a scale order if it works, then a seasonal series. The mistake most small brands make is treating the development order as a one-off cost and then discovering at reordering time that the price rises, the lead time lengthens, or the colour drifts because a new dye lot was used on a different machine.

Three things protect you at the reorder stage. First, keep the dye recipe and the physical standard swatch under version control, and ask the factory to hold a reference standard. Second, put colour tolerance and colour continuity into the specification, with a ΔE limit and a defined measurement method. Third, write a supply agreement that covers price adjustment, capacity reservation and a defect remedy route, so that a price increase or a quality dispute has a pre-agreed path rather than an argument.

Packaging Specification: Where the Real Cost Hides

Retail buyers consistently underestimate packaging. A single bow that ships loose in a polybag is cheap to make and almost useless to receive, sort and shelf. The moment you add a header card, a barcode, a polybag and a carton configuration, you are looking at a meaningful share of the delivered unit cost — and often the difference between a product that reaches the shelf presentable and one that reaches the shelf crushed.

Specify packaging as a bill of materials, not as a description. For each finished unit state: the primary pack (polybag material and thickness, or header card stock weight and finish), whether a barcode label is applied and where, the inner carton or bundle quantity, the master carton dimensions and material, pieces per carton, and the pallet configuration. Then state the requirement that actually matters: a defined maximum compression load for the master carton, and a maximum carton gross weight for manual handling at the distribution centre.

Three failures recur. Cartons specified without gross weight limits arrive at the warehouse and are rejected by the carrier or split by hand. Barcodes applied after the master carton is closed cannot be scanned at receiving. And polybags specified without a suffocation-warning marking cause the whole pallet to be pulled at a retailer in some markets — a cost that has nothing to do with ribbon.

Private Label Cost: The Only Numbers That Matter

Private label decisions get made on unit price, and unit price is the least informative number available. Three numbers carry the real economics.

1. Fully loaded cost per unit at the point of sale

Unit price plus freight plus duty plus inbound handling plus packaging plus your own labour, divided by the number of sellable units. This is the number your finance team will ask for, and it is the only one that survives a margin review.

2. Development cost as a percentage of the first-year programme

Tooling, dies, screens, colour development, sample charges and photography, divided by the value of the first-year orders. If you are planning a private label launch, the development cost is paid once and amortised across everything you subsequently sell, so a slightly higher unit price can be the better deal. Buyers who optimise unit price alone systematically over-order the first run.

3. Contribution per unit at your actual sell-through rate

Retail price minus landed cost minus your channel cost, multiplied by the number of units you actually sell before the season ends — not the number you ordered. A 20 percent unsold rate on a seasonal private label programme is the single largest source of margin loss in this category, and it is entirely a planning problem rather than a factory problem.

Mistakes That Cost Buyers the Most Money

  1. Approving a pre-production sample and rejecting the production lot for the same attribute. If the attribute matters, it must be on the pre-production sample, and the acceptance criterion must be written down before approval.
  2. Specifying a Pantone number and assuming it will be matched. Pantone is a colour reference system for print, not a specification for dyed textile. A Pantone match on grosgrain needs a tolerance and an approval method, or you are buying a surprise.
  3. Leaving packaging undefined and letting the factory choose. The factory will choose whatever is cheapest, and you will find out at receiving.
  4. Comparing quotations that quote different things. Different MOQs, different terms, different scopes. Normalise before you compare.
  5. Not requiring the certification to be named at quotation stage. If certification is added after the order is placed, you have accepted the cost without comparing it.
  6. Treating the factory's sales answer as the specification. Sales summaries lose detail. The specification is the document.
  7. Forgetting who owns the dies and screens. If they belong to the mill and the programme ends, reordering means new setup charges and possible re-approval of the artwork.
  8. Not agreeing the inspection standard before production. Disputes about quality are almost always disputes about what was agreed.

A Working Checklist for a First Ribbon OEM Programme

Before you release a purchase order, confirm each of these is answered in writing:

  • Construction, material and width specified numerically, with tolerance
  • Decoration method named, with the number of colours and their references
  • Colour approval route defined, with tolerance and measurement method
  • Certification named at product level, with the mill's certificate scope checked
  • Format defined: reel, spool, cut length, folded bow, or assembled trim set
  • Packaging specified as a bill of materials, including carton and pallet
  • One-time costs separated from recurring costs, with tooling ownership stated
  • Volume band stated for the quoted unit price, with the price at your forecast volume
  • Incoterm, destination port and payment terms stated
  • Required-on-shelf date stated, with the factory's confirmation of the delivery date
  • Sample types and approval stages defined, with acceptance criteria
  • Inspection standard and sampling plan agreed before production

Twelve items. If all twelve are answered, the programme runs predictably, and the cost of your first order is close to the cost of your tenth. If four or five are open, you will pay for them in delays, in samples you cannot use, and in a dispute you did not need to have.

Choosing a Factory You Can Grow With

The mill you choose for your first 2,000-piece order will, if the programme works, be the mill you rely on for your fiftieth order. Evaluate them on the things that matter at scale rather than on the price of the sample.

Ask about capacity honestly. A mill with fifteen thousand square metres and two hundred staff running hundred thousand metres a day is a different proposition from a mill with a small workshop, and the difference shows up in your lead time two years later, not in your quotation today. Ask whether your programme is on the same line as the mill's core business, and ask what happens to your slot in the peak season that precedes your category's season.

Ask how they handle a specification change after booking. Any factory that says nothing changes after booking is either not running custom work or is telling you what you want to hear. The right answer describes a written change procedure with a cost and a time impact.

Ask how they will handle the second year when your demand doubles. The answer should involve capacity planning, not improvisation.

If You Are Starting From Zero

Here is a workable order of operations for a brand that has never manufactured ribbon before:

  1. Choose one product. One width, one colour, one decoration. Not a range.
  2. Write the specification using the checklist above. If you cannot fill a field in, that is your first research task, not a reason to leave it blank.
  3. Send the identical specification to three to five factories and ask each for a quotation with the breakdown structure described above.
  4. Compare the quotations after normalising scope, volume band and terms. Then shortlist on capability, not on price alone.
  5. Order samples from two shortlisted factories. Compare them against the specification, not against each other's marketing photographs.
  6. Approve one, book it, and run a modest first order sized to real sell-through data rather than to the factory's minimum.
  7. Write the supply agreement before the first repeat order, while the relationship is good.

That is the whole method. Nothing here is exotic, and nothing here requires you to be a textile engineer. It requires you to decide what you are buying, write it down, and hold both sides of the conversation to the same document. The factories that do well under this method are the ones that reward clarity, and the ones that do not will tell you quickly.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement managers most often ask us about OEM, ODM and private label ribbon programmes. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between OEM and ODM in ribbon manufacturing?

OEM means you supply the design, specification, artwork and colour reference, and the factory manufactures to your brief. ODM means the factory contributes design capability — construction, colourways, structure — and you choose what to adopt. ODM is slower to start but faster to iterate, and suits brands entering a category without a settled product definition. OEM suits brands with a clear visual identity who need manufacturing capacity rather than design help. Private label is a separate question: it describes whose name is on the product, and can be combined with either model.

2. What is a realistic minimum order quantity for custom printed ribbon?

It depends on the decoration method rather than on the factory's published policy. Screen-printed and digitally printed ribbon can often start around 1,000 metres per colour, and woven custom jacquard carries a much higher floor because the loom setup is the constraint. At Smith Ribbon we support OEM and ODM customisation from 1,000 metres with small-batch runs from 500 metres, and published MOQs across the industry range from roughly 500 metres for stock-colour printed goods to several thousand metres for custom-woven designs. The realistic levers are volume, number of colours, number of SKUs, product format and payment terms — a single SKU is harder for a mill than three SKUs of similar volume, and a reel format is easier than a cut-and-folded trim set.

3. How long does a first ribbon OEM order take from brief to delivery?

Budget 10 to 14 weeks for a new programme delivering to Europe. The cycle has three parts that buyers routinely underestimate. Pre-production typically takes 2 to 4 weeks for setup, dyeing, screen making and scheduling. Production takes 15 to 35 days depending on volume and mill load — the same programme that runs in 15 days in February often takes 35 in October. Ocean freight from South China to Europe runs 4 to 7 weeks, to North America 3 to 5 weeks, plus customs clearance and inland delivery. Quoting a single lead-time figure usually means only the middle of those three parts is being counted.

4. Should I compare ribbon quotations on unit price or landed cost?

On landed cost, and only after normalising the quotations to the same scope. A quotation that appears 15 percent cheaper may exclude the tooling, quote a lower MOQ, use a different Incoterm, or omit packaging and certification that the other quote includes. Normalise first: same product specification, same volume band, same format, same packaging, same certification scope, same Incoterm and payment terms. Then build the landed figure — unit price plus freight plus duty plus inbound handling plus packaging, divided by sellable units. The difference between the two numbers is usually the real story of the comparison.

5. How do I keep the ribbon colour consistent between my first order and my reorder?

Three mechanisms, all of which need to be in place before the reorder rather than after it. First, keep the dye recipe under version control at the mill and ask for a physical reference standard from the approved lot to be retained for comparison. Second, write a colour tolerance into the specification with a defined measurement method and instrument, typically a ΔE limit under D65 illumination. Third, state the colour continuity requirement explicitly — that replenishment orders must match the approved standard within that tolerance — so the requirement is enforceable rather than implied. Most colour drift complaints we see trace back to a colour that was approved verbally and never specified numerically.

6. What should a ribbon packaging specification include?

Specify packaging as a bill of materials per finished unit: the primary pack (polybag material and thickness, or header card stock weight and finish), whether and where a barcode label is applied, inner carton or bundle quantity, master carton dimensions and material, pieces per carton, and pallet configuration. Then state the two requirements that actually protect the shipment: a maximum compression load for the master carton and a maximum gross carton weight for manual handling at the distribution centre. Omitting gross weight causes split cartons and carrier surcharges; specifying barcodes after the master carton is closed makes them unscannable at receiving; and missing suffocation-warning markings on polybags can get an entire pallet pulled at certain retailers.

7. Who owns the dies, screens and printing plates in a private label ribbon programme?

This is negotiable and is left undefined surprisingly often. By default the tooling stays with the mill, which means that when your programme ends, reordering incurs new setup charges and may require re-approval of the artwork. If the programme is a significant private label investment, ask explicitly for ownership or for a contractual right to the tooling, and for the colour recipe to be released to you at the end of the programme. Tooling charges are a one-time cost that appears in the quotation; tooling ownership is a contract term that has to be written into the supply agreement. It should also be addressed before the second order, while the relationship is still cooperative.
