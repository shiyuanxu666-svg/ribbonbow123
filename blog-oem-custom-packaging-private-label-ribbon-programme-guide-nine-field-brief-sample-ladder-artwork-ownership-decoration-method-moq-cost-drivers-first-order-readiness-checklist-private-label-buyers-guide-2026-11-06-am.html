What an OEM Packaging Programme Actually Is

Private label is often described as "just putting my logo on their ribbon." That framing is the single most expensive misconception in this category, and it is the reason so many brand owners discover in month six that they cannot reorder what they launched, cannot prove they own the artwork, and cannot get a second quote because the specification they gave was a photograph rather than a document.

An OEM packaging programme is a manufacturing relationship in which you specify a physical product precisely enough that two different factories would build materially the same thing, and precisely enough that you can change factory without rebuilding your brand. That is the actual goal. Not "find a cheap ribbon supplier." Not "find a factory that will put my logo on it." The goal is a transferable specification.

Every section below exists to move you from the first position to the second. A specification that only your current factory can interpret is a hostage negotiation. A specification that any competent mill can quote against is an asset, and it is also what lets you renegotiate pricing later without re-sourcing from scratch.

We have seen the failure modes below repeatedly, in both directions: brands who over-specified and paid for unnecessary tooling, and brands who under-specified and paid for it in rejected shipments instead.

The Nine Fields a Factory Cannot Guess

A factory will happily quote a one-line brief. It will also happily quote it wrong, because the information missing from a one-line brief is not missing from the price. It is missing from the quality of what you receive, and you pay for it at the receiving dock rather than in the quotation.

Here are the nine fields we ask for before we put a number on anything. If your brief contains all nine, you will receive comparable quotations. If it contains four, you will receive quotations that look comparable and are not.

That gap is typically discovered at onboarding, and it is expensive at that point because you are committed.

The Sample Ladder: Five Steps, Each Proving a Different Thing

The single most common sourcing error is approving a sample that does not prove what you need it to prove. A swatch on a business card is not a pre-production sample. A pre-production sample is not a production sample. Approving the wrong one means discovering a problem at 10,000 metres instead of at 10 metres.

StepWhat it isWhat it provesWhat it cannot prove
1. Lab dip / colour chipWoven swatch, undecorated, in the target colourWhether the mill can hit your colour at all, in the material you specifiedAnything about print, finish or width
2. Printed swatchDecorated sample on correct stock, nominal print settingsRegistration, ink coverage, hand, colour interaction with decorationHow it looks at 5,000 metres on a running loom
3. Pre-production sampleFull-width, full-length, produced on production equipmentEdge finish, selvedge, roll winding, dimensional consistencyBatch-to-batch colour continuity
4. Production sample / first-offTaken from the actual production runHow the real run behaves, including dye-lot effectsNothing about consistency across the run
5. Sealed golden sampleOne signed, sealed reference unit retained by youThe benchmark every future shipment is measured againstNothing — it is a reference, not a test

Step 5 is the one most brands skip and most regret skipping. A signed and sealed golden sample, held by you, is what makes a dispute about a shipment resolvable in a week instead of a quarter. Without it, a colour objection becomes an argument about whose memory of the approved shade is more reliable, and the mill has a stronger incentive to be certain about it than you do, because the mill has the production records.

Two practical notes. First, approve each step against a written specification, not against a photograph, and store the specification with the sample. Second, put a date and a name on every approval. An unsigned approval in an email thread is worth very little when you are trying to recover a price increase or a defect credit eighteen months later.

Artwork and Ownership: Get This Right Before You Print

Intellectual property in private label is usually discussed as a legal matter, and it is a legal matter, but the practical failure is an operational one. The artwork file sits on the mill's server, the mill's pre-press team has made edits nobody logged, and when you move to a second source for a price improvement, the new factory cannot get the file because you never actually held it.

Three things to settle, in order:

1. Who owns the artwork, in writing, and when does it transfer

The brand owner should own the artwork outright from the moment of creation. The contract should say so explicitly, and it should say what happens to the file when the programme ends: the mill returns all print-ready files, dies, screens and colour standards, or destroys them and confirms in writing. "Files are held for the duration of the programme" is not good enough if you may exit the relationship at any point, because you would then have to re-create your own artwork from scratch.

2. Do you hold a copy of the print-ready file

You should receive a full print-ready package for every artwork you use: vector artwork with fonts converted to outlines, a separated colour file per print colour, and a written specification of the declaration the mill used. In practice this is the item brands most often do not request, and most often need. A logo drawn on a photograph of a swatch is not a production file, and if your second source needs a plate, you will be waiting a week for a file that should have existed already.

3. Are custom dies, screens or plates treated as your property

A metal die, a screen or a printing plate is tooling, and tooling has a physical existence somewhere. The usual arrangement is that tooling is paid for by the brand and remains the brand's property, physically located at the mill, and returned or destroyed on exit. What you want to avoid is a termination that requires the mill to "dispose of tooling at its discretion" while your artwork still depends on it.

Choosing a Decoration Method You Can Actually Cost

The decoration method drives unit cost more than the base fabric does, which surprises people who assume ribbon is a textile purchase. It also determines which decorations are possible at which widths, and it determines the tooling cost you pay before you sell a single metre.

The mistake we see most often is specifying a method by name without specifying the coverage, the colour count and the substrate it will run on. "Screen printed" is not a specification. "Screen printed, four spot colours, maximum 60% coverage per pass, both faces, on 40mm polyester satin face" is a specification, and it is the difference between a quotation you can compare and one you cannot.

MOQ and the Cost Drivers Behind Your Unit Price

Ribbon pricing is not one number, it is a stack. Understanding the stack is what lets you argue about the parts that are negotiable and accept the parts that are not. A typical ex-works unit price for custom printed polyester satin contains:

  • Base fabric — the yarn and weave, often the largest single component, and the part that moves most with polymer and cotton price cycles.
  • Colour conversion — dyeing to your standard, which carries a setup cost per colour and a labour component per metre. The setup is why a second colour on an existing order is cheaper than a first colour on a new order.
  • Decoration setup — screens, plates, dies. Amortised across the run, which is why small quantities are disproportionately expensive per metre.
  • Decoration run cost — inks, foil, machine time, which scale with metres.
  • Finishing — hemming, cutting, heat-sealing ends, winding, which scales with metres and with roll format.
  • Quality cost — the inspection, testing and rework that your specification implicitly demanded.
  • Overhead and margin — which is where the negotiation actually lives.

Two practical levers are usually overlooked. The first is colour consolidation: a programme that uses four colours across 3,000 metres is far more expensive per metre than a programme using two colours across 12,000 metres, even where the total decoration cost looks similar on paper. The second is roll format: reel length is a free choice, and choosing a reel that fits your carton plan and your container plan reduces freight per metre measurably, without touching the mill's price at all.

Our standard terms for OEM customisation run from 1,000 metres per colour, with small-batch runs available from 500 metres where the decoration is screen print and the artwork is simple. Those thresholds are workable for a brand testing a product line, but they are not efficient, and a buyer who plans around them will find the per-metre cost higher than it needs to be.

How to Structure the Agreement

The commercial agreement should be short enough to be read and specific enough to be useful. The clauses that earn their place in a first-order agreement are the ones that address the specific risks of decorative trim: the specification attachment, the golden sample as the acceptance reference, the defect remedy, the delivery window and the tooling and artwork ownership provisions.

Two of these deserve particular attention. The first is the defect remedy ladder: a tiered structure that says what happens at a defect rate below the agreed AQL, what happens above it, and at what point you require rework, at what point you require replacement, and at what point you require a credit. A remedy that is not tiered is either too soft to be useful or too harsh to be signed. The second is the price adjustment mechanism: an agreed index reference and an agreed review cadence, so that a six-month-old price does not become either a windfall for the mill or an open-ended exposure for you.

Longer relationships are usually better served by a separate supply agreement covering capacity reservation, forecasting, lead-time commitments, quality escalation and review cadence, rather than by overloading the first purchase order. Most of the friction in a packaging relationship comes from not having agreed on those things before the first peak, not from the price.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Specifying by photograph

A photograph of a ribbon establishes an impression, not a specification. It cannot be quoted against reliably, it cannot be used to compare quotations, and it cannot be used to argue a quality claim. Every field in the nine-field table above exists because someone, somewhere, learned what happens when it is left out.

Mistake 2: Treating the first price as a permanent one

The first quotation carries setup cost recovery, learning curve and a relationship premium that will not be there on the second order. If you do not expect the price to come down and you do not ask, it will not come down. Plan a price review at a defined point, with the order history as your evidence.

Mistake 3: Leaving artwork and tooling unaddressed

It feels like a formality at the start of a friendly relationship. It is not. It is the clause that determines whether you have a right to your own logo at the end of it.

Mistake 4: Not naming the compliance requirement

The mill will make whatever it is certified to make. If you want a product class outside that scope, you have to ask for it explicitly, and you have to ask early, because certification takes time.

Mistake 5: Skipping the golden sample

It costs one unit and a signature, and it is the only artefact that survives a dispute with a supplier six months later.

Mistake 6: Accepting a lead time instead of a date

"Four to six weeks" means two different things in November and in March. Ask for the date the material has to be on the floor to hit your in-store date, and confirm it in writing.

What a Good First Conversation Looks Like

If you are starting from zero, the fastest route to a usable quotation is a thirty-minute call with the factory's own technical and merchandising staff in the room, not just a salesperson. Bring the brief, bring the artwork, and be explicit about the field you are unsure about. A factory that asks you hard questions about coverage and width and colour tolerance is telling you something about how the relationship will go. A factory that quotes immediately from a photograph is telling you something else.

Bring the deliverable date, not just the quantity. Bring the compliance list, not just the product. And if the answer is that something you want is not possible at your volume, that is useful information to receive in week one rather than after you have committed to a launch date.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, procurement managers and private-label buyers most often ask us about setting up an OEM custom ribbon and packaging programme, from MOQ and artwork ownership to colour tolerance and sample approval. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How long does it take to launch an OEM custom ribbon programme from first brief to shipment?

For a standard custom printed polyester satin programme with a new colour and new artwork, a typical path from complete development brief to shipment is 45 to 70 days: colour development and lab dip approval in 7 to 12 days, printed swatch and pre-production sample in a further 7 to 10 days, then 25 to 40 days of production and 7 to 15 days in transit depending on destination and delivery term. Programmes with jacquard woven patterns, wired edges or multi-colour lamination run longer because setup is a loom programme rather than a print run. The single largest avoidable delay is artwork arriving late or in a non-print-ready format, which can add two to three weeks on its own.

2. What is the minimum order quantity for custom printed ribbon, and does it apply per colour?

Our standard OEM customisation starts at 1,000 metres per colour per style, and we accept small-batch runs from 500 metres where the decoration is screen print and the artwork is simple enough not to require a separate plate. In practice the MOQ applies per colourway, not per order, which is the point that surprises first-time buyers: 1,000 metres split across four colours means 1,000 metres of each of four colours, and four dye-lot setups, not 4,000 metres of mixed stock. If your launch plan needs more colours at less depth per colour, that is a conversation worth having before the brief is written, because the alternative is usually to reduce the colour count or to raise the volume per colourway.

3. Do I own the artwork and the custom tooling if I work with your factory?

Yes. The brand owner owns the artwork. We ask for a signed acknowledgement that the artwork, the print-ready files, the colour standards and any custom dies, screens or plates are paid for by the brand and remain the brand's property. On termination of the programme, those items are either returned to you or destroyed on your written instruction, and we confirm either outcome in writing. We also recommend, and practise as standard, that you receive a complete print-ready file package for every artwork you use, so that your brand is never dependent on our pre-press team to reproduce your own logo if you move to another source.

4. What is the difference between a lab dip, a pre-production sample and a golden sample?

A lab dip is an undecorated woven swatch in your target colour, and it proves only whether we can hit the colour in the material you specified. A pre-production sample is a full-width, full-length decorated sample produced on production equipment, and it proves edge finish, selvedge quality, roll winding and dimensional consistency. A golden sample is a signed, sealed reference unit that you retain and use as the benchmark for every subsequent shipment. They each prove a different thing, and approving one does not substitute for approving the others. Most disputes we see come from a buyer approving a printed swatch and assuming production behaviour has been verified when it has not.

5. How do I make sure two factories quote the same thing so I can compare them?

Send the same nine-field development brief to every factory: material and construction, width and tolerance, colour standard, decoration method with coverage and colour count, finish, order profile and MOQ, pack format, delivery term and destination with in-store date, and compliance requirement. Ask each factory to quote against each field separately rather than returning a single unit price, so you can see where the quotations actually differ. In our experience, quotations that look comparable on price are frequently not comparable on specification, and the differences usually surface as claims and rework after the first shipment rather than as a clear advantage at the point of comparison.

6. Which certifications do I need, and can you supply them?

It depends on your end market and your retailer's vendor compliance requirements, so the honest answer is that it depends and you should specify it in the brief. In practice the most commonly required are OEKO-TEX Standard 100 for the base fabric, FSC where paper or board elements are involved, BSCI and SEDEX/SMETA for social compliance, and ISO 9001 for quality management. We hold these and can supply current certificates and test reports. The important point for a buyer is that you need to confirm the scope of each certificate covers your specific product class, not merely that the certificate exists. Certificate scope, expiry and covered product categories are what an auditor actually reads.

7. How do you handle colour matching, and what happens if the delivered colour is off?

We work against a Pantone reference and an agreed physical lab dip, and we measure with spectrophotometer against the approved sample, with a target of ΔE within 1.5 for most programmes. Where a brand has no physical reference, we produce a lab dip first and get that approved before any print sample, because a Pantone chip read through a woven textile is not a reliable target on its own. If a delivered lot falls outside the agreed tolerance, the remedy is set out in the agreement: rework, replacement or credit depending on the defect rate and the timing. This is why the golden sample matters, and why we would rather resolve a colour question against a sealed reference in week one than against a recollection in month nine.

8. Can you produce from recycled or sustainable materials under my own brand label?

Yes. We work with RPET recycled polyester and with certified sustainable material pathways, and we can support the documentation a brand needs to make an environmental claim. The requirement on your side is to be specific about the claim you intend to make, because recycled content claims are regulated and a claim you cannot substantiate is a liability rather than an asset. Tell us the claim you want to make and the market you will make it in, and we will tell you which evidence we can produce and which we cannot. It is better to design the supply route around the claim at the start than to add certification to a finished programme later.
