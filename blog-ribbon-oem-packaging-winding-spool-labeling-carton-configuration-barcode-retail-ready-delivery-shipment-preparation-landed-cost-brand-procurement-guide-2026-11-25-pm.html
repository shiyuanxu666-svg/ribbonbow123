The core insight: ribbon packaging is specified in about four lines on a quotation and then lives with the goods for the life of the programme. It determines how the ribbon arrives, how a warehouse stores it, whether a store can put it on a shelf without a knife and a frustrated customer, whether your stock system can find it, and how much of your freight budget goes into air rather than product. Almost none of it is visible until the first pallet arrives at a distribution centre and the goods cannot be put away.

This guide covers the physical packaging specification for ribbon in commercial supply: winding formats, spool and core choices, labelling, barcode handling, carton configuration, the interaction with minimum order quantities, what packaging actually costs inside a landed cost, and how to write these requirements into a supply agreement so they hold across reorders.

It is written for brand owners, product developers and procurement managers who buy ribbon for packaging, gift sets, apparel trim, hair accessories or home textiles, and who are responsible for the goods arriving in a condition a third-party warehouse can handle without a phone call.

The reason this deserves attention is a quiet asymmetry. A ribbon specification error in fibre or colour will be discovered by your own quality team within a day of arrival. A packaging specification error is discovered by your customer's warehouse, possibly at their distribution centre, possibly weeks later, possibly at a store rather than a warehouse, and by then it is a service failure rather than a product return. Packaging faults do not generate complaints about ribbon. They generate complaints about your operation.

The Decision That Gets Made Before Anyone Thinks About It

Ribbon packaging is almost always inherited rather than chosen. A buyer specifies a width, a construction and a colour, asks for a price per metre, and the quotation includes a packaging line that the buyer reads as a cost rather than a specification. The mill is not being unreasonable — packaging is genuinely part of the price and the mill's default is usually a sensible one. But the default is built for the mill's convenience, not for your warehouse.

Consider what a mill's default packaging actually optimises for. It optimises for shipping density, for protection against moisture and abrasion during export, for minimum labour in the packing room, and for a packing sequence that works with the winding machine in front of the operator. All legitimate objectives. None of them is your handling requirement.

The specific consequences are easy to predict. A roll wound too loosely arrives with edge waviness that makes cutting to width inaccurate. A carton packed too tightly opens on the pallet with rolls compressed at the bottom. A label placed over the spool flange means the warehouse cannot scan it without unrolling the roll. A carton count that does not divide into your store's order quantities means your receiving team is opening boxes to make partial shipments all day. A packing list that lists metres rather than rolls means your stock system counts rolls and your invoice says metres, and the reconciliation takes a week every month.

None of these is dramatic. All of them cost money, and they cost money in the place where money is hardest to see: operational labour in someone else's warehouse, billed back to you eventually in service charge or quietly absorbed until the account is under review.

There is also a forward-looking reason to specify packaging deliberately. Packaging specification is one of the few elements of a ribbon programme that becomes more valuable as volume grows, because it is the point at which a mill's efficiency genuinely transfers to you. A packaging specification that suits a 500,000-metre annual programme can be worth several per cent of landed cost at scale, and it can also make your goods genuinely easier to resell, which matters if the range is ever discontinued and the remaining stock has to clear.

Winding Formats: Flat, Folded, Double and Center-Wound

The winding format describes how the ribbon is arranged on the spool or core, and it is the first decision that determines how the goods behave downstream. The main formats are these:

Flat wound, single layer. The ribbon winds in a single layer across the width of the spool flange. This is the format used where the ribbon will be used from the roll directly, typically in packaging operations or craft and gift applications. It is simple, it prints and cuts predictably, and it is easy to measure remaining length from the outside of the roll.

Flat wound, multiple layers. Several layers build the roll width across the spool. This increases the length per roll and reduces the number of rolls per carton, which typically reduces packaging material and freight density costs. The trade-off is that the outer layers are more prone to compression and edge damage, and a roll that has been compressed in transit can crease badly when unwound.

Center-wound (fold-wound). The ribbon is folded so both ends meet in the middle of the roll, and the ribbon is used by unwinding from the centre. This is the format used for retail display spools, particularly in hair accessories and gift ribbon sold on a card. It is convenient for the end user and generally not appropriate for industrial or bulk use.

Double-wound. A wider spool carries two narrower ribbons wound side by side, usually for specific narrow-gauge products. This reduces the number of spools per carton for a given total metreage and is worth asking about if you buy several narrow widths.

Folded on a card or hanger. Ribbon measured to a fixed length and folded onto a card or header card with a hole, for hanging display in a retail environment. This is a retail-ready format rather than an industrial one, and it brings its own considerations: card stock specification, hole position, hole reinforcement, and whether the card is printed with your branding and a barcode.

Each format also has a winding direction — usually described as face in or face out — which determines which side of the ribbon is exposed on the outside of the roll. For a printed or metallised ribbon this matters, because you do not want the printed face buried against the core where it will crease, and you do not want the reverse face exposed to handling. Getting this wrong is entirely avoidable and entirely the sort of thing that is only noticed when a warehouse team tries to feed a roll into a machine and the ribbon prints on the wrong side.

The practical point is that winding format is not cosmetic and should not be left to default. If your goods are consumed in a retail environment, a retail-ready format will save your staff considerable time and will materially improve how the product presents. If your goods go into a production line, a flat-wound bulk format will serve you better.

Spool and Core Specifications

Where a ribbon is wound on a spool rather than a plain core, the spool becomes a packaging component with its own specification, and it is worth specifying deliberately because it is a packaging material that your customer will handle, store and eventually dispose of.

The variables that matter:

  • Flange material and thickness. Cardboard, plastic, or fibreboard. Cardboard is economical and recyclable; plastic is more robust and can be reused; fibreboard is heavier and more rigid. Plastic spools are standard for retail display because they hold a card and survive handling, but they are more expensive and have become a target for packaging reduction commitments.
  • Flange diameter and hub diameter. These determine how much ribbon sits between the flanges and therefore how much is lost as unusable core and end waste. A tight hub means less waste but a smaller usable inner diameter, which matters for machines with a minimum spindle size.
  • Width tolerance. The spool width must match the ribbon width with enough clearance for the ribbon to seat without binding, but not so much that the roll becomes unstable in the carton. This is a real and frequently neglected tolerance.
  • Load rating. Whether the spool is intended for a specific maximum metres or weight. This matters if the ribbon will be handled by an automated system that lifts rolls by the spool.
  • Printability. Whether the flange carries printed information. For retail spools this is where your branding, the colour name and the barcode usually live.
  • Compatibility. Whether the spool fits your customer's existing machinery, or theirs. A retailer's existing planogram and hanging fixtures will usually determine the spool format far more than the ribbon itself.

The single most common practical error here is specifying a spool width without checking the tolerance, and then discovering that the ribbon binds on the spool and the rolls arrive with a permanent curl. If you are specifying ribbon for a machine, give the mill the machine's spool width and ask what clearance it recommends rather than assuming a nominal figure is a manufacturing dimension.

A second point worth making is about consistency across reorders. Because spools are a purchased consumable, a mill that changes spool supplier between batches will change the appearance and dimensions of your goods without any change to the ribbon specification. It is entirely reasonable to ask that the spool type and supplier be held constant for the life of the programme, and to note it in the specification.

Why Handling Specifications Matter More Than Packaging Aesthetics

There is a persistent temptation to treat packaging as a branding opportunity — a window box, a printed carton, a ribbon band around the bundle. It is a reasonable thing to want, and for a gift programme it is often correct. But for a high-volume brand programme, the operational specifications dominate the aesthetic ones, and mixing up the priorities is how programmes end up with beautiful cartons that a distribution centre cannot stack.

The operational specifications that actually cause problems are worth listing plainly, because each is a question you can put into a purchase order today:

Compression resistance. Cartons get stacked, and ribbon is soft. A carton of ribbon that will be stacked under heavy cartons needs a defined compression rating and a defined maximum stack height. Without one, the bottom rolls deform and the goods arrive with creases and flattened cores. This is the single most common carton-related failure in bulk ribbon supply.

Moisture protection. Polyester ribbon is not seriously hygroscopic, but cotton, rayon and paper-based elements are, and cartons stored in transit containers or in a warehouse near a dock can absorb enough moisture to make packaging soft, printing to run and labels to lift. Ask what the packaging's moisture protection is and how the goods are conditioned before packing.

Abrasion and edge protection. Ribbon edges are the most vulnerable part of the product. A carton that allows rolls to shift during transit will scuff and fray edges, and a high-shine or printed ribbon shows it immediately. Corner protection, edge guards and a carton that does not under-fill are all straightforward asks.

Opening and re-closure. Cartons that arrive split open create a secondary handling event at the receiving dock, and a carton that cannot be re-closed after a partial pick creates loose goods that get damaged. If your programme involves partial shipments or picking from a carton, specify a re-closable carton.

Empty space. An under-filled carton is not free. It occupies the same freight space as a full one and increases damage during transit. This is often visible in the price per carton and worth asking about directly.

Pallet configuration. How cartons stack, how many per layer, and the pallet dimensions. If your goods arrive at a European distribution centre, the pallet footprint and the carton count per layer determine how much is broken down on arrival, and broken-down goods are labour.

None of these specifications is exotic. Each is a line in a purchase order once you know it exists. And each one, if wrong, is discovered by your customer's warehouse rather than by you.

Labelling: What Goes on the Roll and Why

Labelling looks trivial and is not, because the label is the only thing that connects a physical roll to your stock system, and it is where the most common data errors in the whole supply chain originate.

What a good roll label carries:

Identity. Your purchase order number or your internal style number, the mill's job number, and the roll's own sequence number within the delivery. The roll sequence number matters more than people expect: when a customer receives a mixed delivery and one roll is wrong, being able to identify exactly which roll it is, and which rolls are fine, is the difference between a partial credit and a full rejection.

Product description. Construction, fibre, width, colour reference, finish and decoration. Enough for a warehouse to identify the goods without opening anything.

Quantity. Metres on the roll, and the number of rolls in the carton, and the total. This is where the metres-versus-rolls reconciliation problem starts, and it is worth stating both on the carton label so that a discrepancy is visible before it becomes an argument.

Lot and date. A dye lot or production batch reference and a production date. Without these you cannot trace a quality issue to a production run, and your corrective action becomes guesswork.

Country of origin and compliance marks. Required for import, and required by many retailers as a condition of listing.

Placement matters as much as content. A label on the outside of a folded or center-wound spool that covers the ribbon face is a label that damages the product. A label applied to the outer wrapping of a carton rather than to each roll means the rolls lose their identity the moment the carton is opened, which defeats the purpose. A label applied in a position that makes the spool unusable on a machine — across the flange where a clamp would grip — is a label that stops the roll being used at all.

One point on durability that is frequently overlooked: labels on ribbon spools need to withstand cold storage, condensation and the glue plasticisers that leach from the surrounding plastic in a warehouse. A water-soluble label is a serious hazard in a warehouse, because it means an unidentifiable roll. Ask for a permanent adhesive and, for cold-storage distribution, a freezer-grade adhesive.

Barcodes, GS1 and the Supply Chain Data Problem

Retail-ready ribbon increasingly carries a barcode, and this introduces a data dependency that sits outside the physical product entirely.

There are several levels of complexity, and it is worth being clear about which one you actually need. A barcode that identifies your own product to your own warehouse is a straightforward internal matter. A barcode that will be scanned at a retail check-out must be a valid GS1 GTIN allocated through your own GS1 organisation membership, pointing at the correct item, and it must be printed to a scannable standard. A barcode that carries variable data — a serial number, a batch, an expiry — is a GS1 Serialized Shipping Container Code and a considerably more involved exercise.

Three practical problems arise regularly, and all three originate with the buyer rather than the mill:

Ownership of the number. A GTIN belongs to the entity that owns the brand, not to the mill. If your mill prints your GTIN on the label, the number is still yours, and the mill needs to be instructed to use it exactly. A mill that sources its own numbering will eventually print a number that is not yours, and the retailer's system will reject it.

Format and quiet zones. The printed symbol needs the correct symbology, module width, height, contrast and quiet zone to scan reliably, and the printing method has to produce a symbol that a retail scanner accepts. Printed labels on a reflective or textured spool flange are notoriously harder to scan than paper labels on a flat surface. A retail test scan of the actual production label, on the actual spool, is cheap insurance.

Variable data and the GS1 master data. If you need batch, serial or expiry to be captured, the mill must be able to apply the variable human-readable digits in a way that matches the barcode exactly, and the data must flow into your GS1 master data record correctly. This is a systems project rather than a packaging project, and it is far easier to design in than to retrofit.

The cost of getting this wrong is disproportionate to its apparent size. A barcode that fails at a retailer's till affects every unit rather than a delivery, and the fix requires replacing packaging across stock already in the distribution network.

Carton Configuration and Units per Carton

The carton is the unit that freight is priced on, that the warehouse receives, and that is usually picked from. Three numbers define it, and each one should be specified rather than inherited.

Units per carton. How many rolls, or how many folded spools, or how many cards go into a carton. The right number is determined by the weight a warehouse team can comfortably handle, the carton strength, and how the receiving operation breaks down cartons. A carton that is too heavy causes handling exceptions and injuries; a carton that is too light means more cartons for the same volume, more freight cost and more receiving labour.

Carton dimensions and gross weight. These determine cube-out, the freight rate, and whether the carton fits your customer's storage and shelf system. For retail distribution in Europe the pallet configuration matters too, and the choice between a 1200 by 800 footprint and a 1200 by 1000 footprint changes how many cartons fit on a pallet, which changes the effective freight cost per carton significantly.

Carton strength. Rated by a compression test standard, with a stated maximum stacking load and stack height. This is the specification that prevents the bottom-of-pallet damage described earlier, and it is the one most often left out.

Beyond those three, the details that cause friction in a warehouse are worth specifying: whether the carton is a regular slotted container or a tuck-end or hinged box, whether it is a double-wall construction, how it closes, and whether it can be re-closed after a partial pick. These sound trivial and each one costs a receiving operation time on every delivery.

There is also a commercial dimension. The units-per-carton figure feeds directly into the minimum order quantity arithmetic, and this is where packaging specifications quietly constrain commercial flexibility. It is discussed in the next section because it is the interaction buyers most often miss.

The MOQ Interaction Nobody Checks

Ribbon minimum order quantities are quoted per colour, per width, per decoration and sometimes per construction. Buyers accept them and move on. But the packaging specification determines whether a minimum order quantity is actually usable, and the two only connect at the point where the goods are counted, packed and shipped.

Consider a typical structure. Suppose your minimum order quantity is 500 metres of a given colour and width, but the mill's standard carton contains 2,400 metres. You cannot ship 500 metres in the standard carton. You will receive 500 metres in a part carton, or you will receive 2,400 metres and have 1,900 metres of excess stock sitting in your warehouse in a colour that may never be reordered.

This is not a minor packaging issue; it is a working capital issue. It is the reason so many programmes end up with a warehouse full of half-used colours, and it is the reason buyers who thought they had negotiated a low minimum order quantity sometimes discover they have not actually reduced their stock commitment at all.

Three ways to handle it well:

Negotiate a part-carton arrangement. Many mills will pack a partial carton for a first order, charging the full carton price but shipping only what you need. Ask explicitly.

Negotiate the minimum order quantity in cartons rather than in metres. This aligns the commercial unit with the physical unit and removes the mismatch at the root. If your programme reorders on a predictable cycle, buying in carton multiples also makes receiving and counting dramatically easier.

Negotiate a lower minimum on a specific colour. If one shade is a core colour with high forecast accuracy, it deserves a lower minimum than an experimental seasonal shade. A blanket minimum across the whole palette is a blunt instrument, and separating the palette into core and seasonal tiers is one of the more effective minimum order quantity conversations available.

The related arithmetic worth running on your own programme: for each colour in your range, compute the total metres you would need to fill a standard carton, and compare it with your expected consumption over a season. Any colour where the carton exceeds seasonal demand is generating inventory risk, and those are the colours where a part-carton arrangement or a lower minimum is worth the negotiation.

How Packaging Shows Up in Landed Cost

Packaging is frequently overlooked in cost analysis because it is bundled into the price per metre and never separated. Separated, it typically represents a low single-digit percentage of the product cost and a more significant percentage of the freight and handling cost. Both are worth understanding, because the levers are different.

Within the product cost. Spool or core, carton, packaging film or wrap, labels, and the labour to pack. The levers are straightforward: a cardboard spool instead of plastic, a lighter carton board, fewer labels, or a better carton fill rate. Each of these reduces cost without touching the ribbon, and they are usually worth pursuing if the mill is receptive — though buyers should be realistic that a mill with a fixed packing routine will resist changes that slow the line, and a change that saves a fraction of a cent per metre but adds ten minutes of labour is not a saving.

Within the freight cost. This is where packaging earns disproportionate returns. Freight is charged on the cubic volume and the weight of the shipment. A carton that is 15 per cent under-filled costs you 15 per cent of that carton's freight for nothing. The two highest-leverage moves here are increasing fill rate and choosing a carton footprint that cubes out efficiently on the pallet. Both are free in product terms and both are paid for directly in freight.

Within the handling cost. The cost of unpacking, counting, re-packing, dealing with damaged rolls, correcting stock record errors, and resolving barcode scan failures. This one is usually invisible to the manufacturer, which is precisely why it needs to be written into the specification rather than left to emerge as a complaint.

There is a fourth element that is easy to miss: packaging as a resale or disposal liability. Packaging that cannot be recycled in the customer's market, or that uses a material subject to an extended producer responsibility charge, carries a cost that appears in the customer's packaging data and eventually in your commercial relationship. If you sell into markets with packaging levies, this is worth discussing with your mill, and a mill that is already working with major brands will have a view on it.

A useful discipline is to ask for packaging to be shown as a line in the quotation, separately from the ribbon. A mill that can itemise packaging is a mill that has thought about it, and itemisation makes it possible to evaluate a change. A mill that cannot separate it is telling you that packaging is not a variable in their process, which is itself useful information.

Shelf Life, Storage Conditions and Stock Rotation

Packaging specification is closely tied to how long the goods can be stored and in what condition, and this matters more for ribbon than buyers often expect, because ribbon is a light, bulky, low-value-per-kilogram product that gets stockpiled and sits.

Three areas of concern:

Light exposure. Unbleached and dyed ribbon fades with light exposure, and the fade is uneven — the outer surface of a roll degrades faster than the core. If goods sit in a warehouse with natural light, in transit containers, or in a retailer's stockroom with a window, the front of the roll becomes visibly lighter than the back. Packaging that blocks light, plus a stated storage condition, protects against this.

Temperature and humidity. Standard polyester satin is stable in normal warehouse conditions. Cotton, rayon, and paper-core spools are less so, and extreme conditions can cause core softening, adhesive failure, or mildew risk in unlined cartons. A stated storage range, and a material choice that tolerates it, is the practical protection.

Rotation and dating. If goods will sit, they need to be identifiable by age so that the oldest stock moves first. A production date on the label is the minimum; a batch reference that lets you link a delivery to a production run is what lets you answer a question about which stock is affected when a quality issue arises. This is a small addition to the label specification with a disproportionate effect on how confidently you can run a stock rotation.

A practical note on shelf life: it is worth asking the mill what they consider a reasonable storage period for the specific construction and packaging combination, and recording that answer in the specification. Mills know their own products' behaviour over time, and the answer occasionally surprises buyers — some finishes yellow with age, some adhesives are not suitable for long storage, and some constructions are simply not intended for extended inventory holding.

Packaging Specifications in a Supply Agreement

Packaging is where specifications most often get lost, because it is not the ribbon and it is not the price, and both of those get attention. The fix is to give packaging its own section in the purchase order or quality agreement, with the following elements:

Winding format and direction. Stated explicitly, including face in or face out, and the number of layers if it matters for your equipment.

Spool or core specification. Material, flange and hub diameter, width tolerance, and the requirement that it be held constant across the programme.

Label specification. Content, placement, format, adhesive type and durability, plus an explicit statement that the label may not obscure the ribbon or interfere with mounting on machinery.

Barcode specification. Where relevant: symbology, GTIN ownership, print quality standard, variable data rules, and a requirement to provide the master data record.

Carton specification. Units per carton, dimensions, gross weight, board type, compression rating, maximum stack height, closure and re-closure.

Pallet configuration. Cartons per layer, layers per pallet, pallet type and footprint, and gross pallet weight.

Part-carton rules. Whether partial cartons are shipped, and how they are labelled and invoiced.

Labelling and documentation. What accompanies the shipment: packing list format, carton labels, barcodes, and any required compliance documents, with the numbers matching across all of them.

Written this way, packaging stops being an area where you find out what the mill does and start being an area where you specify what you need. The mill may propose changes, and some of them will be good ones — a better carton structure, a more reliable adhesive, a smarter carton fill. That is the right conversation to have, provided you are in it deliberately rather than discovering the outcome on a dock.

Common Packaging Failures and How to Prevent Them

A short catalogue of the failures that recur, with the specification change that prevents each:

Carton collapse in transit, deformed bottom rolls. Prevented by a stated compression rating and a maximum stack height, and by a defined transit stacking configuration.

Under-filled cartons inflating freight. Prevented by specifying a minimum fill rate or by specifying carton dimensions against a target metres-per-carton figure.

Loose winding causing edge waviness. Prevented by specifying a winding tension or a maximum roll hardness, and verified on receipt by comparing the roll edge profile.

Labels detaching or running in cold or damp storage. Prevented by specifying a permanent, freezer-grade adhesive and by stating the storage conditions the label must survive.

Label covering the ribbon face or blocking machine mounting. Prevented by specifying the label zone explicitly and by a sample approval of the labelled roll before production.

Barcode failing to scan at retail. Prevented by a retail test scan of the production label on the production spool, before the first bulk run.

Mixed deliveries where rolls cannot be identified. Prevented by requiring a roll sequence number on every roll and a carton-level list of the sequence numbers it contains.

Minimum order quantity that cannot be received in a usable quantity. Prevented by expressing the minimum in cartons, or by negotiating a part-carton arrangement in writing.

Label data disagreeing with the invoice or packing list. Prevented by specifying a single source of truth for the data and requiring that the mill's label data be generated from it rather than re-keyed.

The common thread is that every one of these is cheap to prevent at specification stage and expensive to fix after production. That is the argument for doing it properly the first time.

The Working Programme

A practical sequence, for a brand team that wants to fix this once rather than repeatedly:

This week — inventory the current state. For each ribbon programme, write down what packaging you actually receive: winding format, spool type, label content, units per carton, carton dimensions and gross weight. Most teams discover they do not know, which is the point.

This month — check the three-way match. For each programme, confirm that the label data, the packing list and the invoice all describe the same thing in the same units. Mismatches here are pure cost and pure friction.

Next quotation — ask for packaging as a line item. Request the spool, carton and labelling costs separately so you can evaluate changes. Ask about fill rate and pallet configuration, which are usually more valuable than the unit price.

Next order — pilot the specification. Specify the packaging requirement in writing for one programme, and check the result on receipt against a written acceptance checklist covering roll condition, label legibility, carton integrity and count accuracy.

Next agreement — write it in. Add the packaging section to the supply agreement, and review it at the same time as price and quality, not as a separate exercise three years later.

Ongoing — measure. Track damage rates on arrival, receiving time per delivery, and stock-record correction frequency. These three numbers make the business case for packaging improvements, and they are also the numbers that a customer or a distribution centre will be looking at.

The general principle is simple. Packaging is a specification, not a leftover. A ribbon that arrives correctly wound, correctly labelled, correctly counted and correctly stacked is a ribbon that costs almost nothing extra to receive, and it is the difference between your programme being an easy part of your customer's operation and being a small ongoing nuisance in it. That nuisance is invisible on your own dashboard, and it is the kind of thing that quietly decides who gets renewed when the sourcing is put out to tender.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the standard packaging format for ribbon supplied to factories?

Bulk ribbon is normally flat wound in multiple layers onto a cardboard or plastic core, with the width and the number of layers set by the mill's standard packing routine. The roll is then placed in a carton, typically in a regular slotted container, with the rolls laid flat and the carton closed and labelled. For retail programmes the common alternatives are center-wound spools on a card for hanging display, and ribbon folded to a set length on a header card. Which of these applies should be specified rather than accepted as default, because they suit different downstream operations.

2. How many rolls or spools should I put in a carton?

The right number is set by handling weight, carton strength, and how the receiving operation breaks down cartons. As a practical rule, cartons that a warehouse team can comfortably handle without an exception are the ones that work, and cartons that under-fill create freight cost with no benefit. There is a strong commercial argument for matching the units per carton to the minimum order quantity, because a part carton at the end of a production run is awkward to receive, to invoice and to count. Ask your mill for their standard carton configuration, then adjust it to suit your order pattern and your customer's warehouse.

3. Should minimum order quantities be expressed in metres or in cartons?

For anything that reorders on a predictable cycle, cartons are usually the better unit, because they align the commercial commitment with the physical unit. Metres-based minimums create a mismatch whenever the quantity you need does not fill a standard carton, which forces a part carton. Part cartons are workable but they are awkward to receive, to invoice and to count, and they are the origin of a lot of unnecessary handling. If your volume is predictable, ask to negotiate the minimum in carton multiples. If it is not predictable, ask instead for a written part-carton arrangement that states the price, how the part carton is labelled and how it is counted, so that the awkwardness is priced rather than discovered.

4. What should be printed on a ribbon roll label?

At minimum: your purchase order or internal style number, the mill's job number, a roll sequence number within the delivery, the product description including construction, width, colour and finish, the metres on the roll, the number of rolls in the carton and the total, a production batch or dye lot reference with a production date, and country of origin plus any required compliance marks. The roll sequence number is the item buyers most often omit and most often wish they had, because it is what allows a single defective roll to be identified and replaced without rejecting the whole delivery.

5. Does every roll need a barcode, or only the retail-ready ones?

Only the retail-ready ones need a scannable retail barcode, because only those are scanned at a check-out. Bulk ribbon going into a production line does not need a GS1 symbol, though many buyers still apply an internal barcode for their own warehouse. If your programme is retail-ready, note that the GTIN belongs to your brand rather than to the mill, that it must come from your own GS1 membership, and that a test scan of the actual production label on the actual spool is worth doing before the first bulk run, because labels on textured spool flanges frequently scan poorly.

6. How do I stop ribbon cartons collapsing and crushing the bottom rolls?

Specify a carton compression rating and a maximum stacking load and stack height, and confirm that the roll count and arrangement inside the carton are designed for that load. A carton that is nominally correct but over-packed will still transmit load to the bottom rolls. The other common causes are a carton whose board grade is too light for a long transit and a pallet configuration that puts the heaviest pallets on the bottom. It is worth asking the mill for their pallet stacking configuration, because that is where the load path is decided.

7. What information should accompany a ribbon shipment?

A packing list that states the same thing the carton labels state, in the same units, plus a packing list that identifies which roll sequence numbers are in which carton. A common and costly failure is a packing list in metres while the labels count rolls and the invoice bills cartons, which makes every delivery require a manual reconciliation. Ask for one source of truth for the data and require that the mill generate the labels and the packing list from it rather than re-keying them, and specify the format so that your warehouse system can parse it.

8. Can packaging specification affect the price per metre significantly?

The direct product cost impact is usually modest, often a low single-digit percentage. The larger effect is on freight and handling: an under-filled carton costs you the same freight as a full one, and poor fill rate or a poor pallet footprint can add several per cent to the delivered cost without appearing anywhere in the unit price. Packaging is therefore one of the few areas where a modest specification change produces a disproportionate financial return, which is why it belongs in the conversation with the mill rather than after it.
