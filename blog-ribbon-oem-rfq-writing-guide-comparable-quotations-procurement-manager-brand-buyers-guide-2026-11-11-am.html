Why Buyers Write Specifications and Then Get Incomparable Quotes

Most procurement managers in the ribbon and decorative trim category are surprised by how few comparable quotes they receive. They send a specification to eight suppliers, and get back eight responses that price five different things. One quote includes the decoration tooling and the first setup, another treats the same setup as free because the supplier assumes a repeat order will follow. One includes freight to a European port, another quotes ex-works and lets the buyer arrange shipping, which means the real landed cost is invisible in the comparison. One supplier has priced the same width and construction at a different base metric per metre, one at per hundred metres, and one has quietly quoted a narrower ribbon than the one specified, because narrower stock runs faster and the price difference is hidden in the width tolerance nobody wrote down.

None of this is bad faith. It is what happens when a request is written as a description of a desired outcome rather than as a set of comparable inputs. The supplier is not failing; the buyer has simply left too many decisions open, and each supplier has resolved them differently in their own commercial interest and then priced the result. The fix is not a better supplier. The fix is a better request.

An RFQ for ribbon OEM should be constructed so that a factory in Xiamen, a converter in another Chinese province, and a trading company in Europe are all answering the same question with the same units, the same scope and the same assumptions. That takes about two hours of careful writing. This guide sets out what to specify, what to leave deliberately open, how to structure the request, and how to read what comes back.

Separate the Product From the Commercial Terms

The most common structural mistake in an RFQ is mixing three different kinds of information in one document: the physical product, the commercial scope, and the delivery terms. When they are mixed, the supplier fills the gaps in whichever direction serves them, and no two responses can be compared line by line.

Split them into three explicit blocks. In the first block, describe the product as a builder would need to describe it, with no commercial language at all. In the second, list what is included in the price and what is quoted separately, with the assumption that anything not listed as included will be treated as excluded. In the third, state the commercial basis: the Incoterm, the named place, the payment terms and the currency, and the date your quotation is valid.

The second block matters most and is the one most often omitted. A clear statement like the following removes an enormous amount of ambiguity:

The quotation shall state separately: (a) yarn and base ribbon cost per metre at the stated width and construction; (b) the conversion cost including winding, cutting, bundling and packing; (c) decoration setup charges, itemised as artwork preparation, screen or plate setup, screen printing, foil stamping, heat transfer or jacquard; (d) tooling or plate charges and whether these are refundable; (e) the unit price for the decoration applied to the stated quantity; (f) packing cost per carton; (g) any test, certification or inspection charges; and (h) any quantity-break pricing. Any item not listed above shall be treated as excluded from the quoted price.

That paragraph is a little long, and it is worth every word. It converts a negotiation about who decided what into a comparison of numbers, which is the only kind of comparison that survives contact with a real purchasing decision.

The Four Blocks of a Ribbon RFQ That Actually Drive the Price

Ribbon prices are determined by fewer variables than buyers expect. If you specify these four blocks properly, the quotation becomes close to deterministic, and the differences between suppliers become meaningful rather than noisy.

1. Construction and Material

This is the substrate. State the material as a specific construction rather than a family name. Polyester satin in a 38 mm width, 100% polyester, 100 denier face with a satin weave and a selvedge edge, is a purchasable instruction. Satin ribbon in a gift box is not, because it leaves width, denier, weave, edge finish and surface treatment open, and those five choices move the price more than the decoration does.

The same discipline applies to the other constructions. For grosgrain, state the width, the rib density or the number of picks per centimetre, the polyester content and the finish. For velvet, state the pile height or the yarn type and the backing. For organza, state the sheer weight and whether the edges are wired, and if so, the wire material and gauge. For woven or jacquard, state the pattern repeat, the face and back construction and whether the pattern is registered to a selvedge. For metallic, state the base film or textile, the metallisation and the required hand-feel after burnishing.

Two details are worth the extra effort because they cause the most expensive disputes. First, specify the width tolerance and where it is measured, because width varies across a running bolt and the narrowest point of a ribbon is often what sets the trim allowance on the buyer's own line. Second, specify whether the ribbon is supplied on a specific core, at a specific roll length, or wound in a way compatible with the buyer's existing winding equipment, and state the winding direction. Ribbon wound in the wrong direction for the customer's winder produces scrap that nobody priced.

2. Dimensions and Dimensional Tolerances

Width, thickness, length per roll and piece length all need a figure and a tolerance. This sounds pedantic until you compare what two suppliers actually quoted: one quotes a nominal width with no tolerance, the other quotes the narrowest point of the ribbon and a maximum thickness. On a wide ribbon at high volume, the difference between nominal and minimum-width pricing can be several percent, and it lands entirely on your margin.

State tolerances as a band, not as a single value, and state the measurement method. Is width measured at 1 m from the start of the roll, or across a relaxed sample conditioned at a given temperature and humidity? Is thickness measured under a specified load? These questions sound academic until a claim arrives, and at that point the method is the entire defence.

3. Decoration

This is where most of the money moves, and where the RFQ needs the most precision. Specify the decoration method you want, not a category. If you want screen printing, say screen printing, and state the number of colours, whether the design is a solid, a halftone or a fine-line logo, and whether any colour must match a Pantone reference within a stated delta. If a metallic finish is required, state whether it is foil stamping, hot foil, metallic screen print or metallised film, because these have completely different cost structures and different durability.

Decoration also carries its own hidden inputs, each of which should be in the request:

  • Number of colours, and whether a colour is defined as a solid, a tint of another colour, or a gradient.
  • Registration tolerance, stated in millimetres, and what "acceptable" means where the design has fine detail or small text.
  • Repeat and position, for jacquard or printed continuous patterns: the repeat length, and whether the pattern runs continuously or repeats per metre.
  • Surface — whether the ribbon is decorated in the greige state and then finished, or finished first and then decorated, because the order of these operations changes the achievable quality and the cost.
  • Decoration on one or both faces, and whether a reverse-face print is required for a specific end use.
  • Adhesion and fastness requirements, including any wash, rub or light fastness the buyer intends to claim on the finished product.

If your design is not yet final, say so and attach what exists. A supplier who receives a concept image will either decline to quote, quote an assumption and bury it, or quote on the assumption that you will absorb the cost of a change. Stating "artwork is not final; target timing for final artwork is week X; if artwork is not received by week Y we reserve the right to requote" protects both sides and is entirely normal practice.

4. Packing, Quantity and Delivery

Specify the order quantity and, where possible, a set of volume breaks. A ribbon factory prices from a specific production lot, and the cost per metre genuinely falls as the lot grows, so asking for three points on the volume curve gives you both a quote and an understanding of the curve. Many suppliers will quote a single price at a single volume and treat the rest as negotiable, which makes it impossible to tell whether a lower quote at a higher volume reflects real economics or simply an opening position.

For packing, state the requirements that affect cost and that you actually care about: roll length or piece length, pieces per bundle, rolls per carton, carton dimensions and maximum gross weight, and whether cartons must be palletised. Also state the labelling requirement, since a retail-ready carton with a customer barcode and a specific label position is a different operation from a plain export carton with a shipping mark.

Finally, state the delivery basis and the destination precisely. "FOB Xiamen" and "FOB Ningbo" are different places with different freight economics. "DDP Rotterdam" and "DDP Antwerp" are different ports. "CIF Rotterdam" obliges the supplier to arrange carriage and insurance to that port, and the supplier will price it accordingly, which is often a useful benchmark even if you do not intend to buy on that basis, because it converts the whole comparison onto a landed basis.

What to Leave Open, and Why

Some things should stay open in an RFQ, and saying so explicitly is better than omitting them. If you are genuinely comparing approaches, say that you are open to alternative constructions that meet the functional requirement at a lower cost, and state the functional requirement. "We need a 38 mm ribbon with a soft hand, good drape, and enough body to hold a bow loop" is an open brief; "38 mm polyester satin" is a closed specification. Buyers who want comparison should write the first kind and ask each supplier to propose the construction, then compare the proposals against each other on the criteria that matter.

Other open items simply need a deadline rather than a specification: the final colour standard, the final artwork, the exact carton count for a given shipment, and the exact delivery date if it depends on a production slot you have not yet booked. For each of these, state what you will do if the information arrives late. In most cases, the answer is a requote, and saying so removes the temptation on the supplier's side to build in a large contingency and then present it as a fixed price.

How to Structure the Request So Responses Come Back Cleanly

The format of the request does as much work as its content. A request sent as a free-text email produces prose answers that must be re-keyed into a spreadsheet. A request sent as a structured table produces rows that can be compared directly.

The most workable structure for a ribbon RFQ is a single document with five sections in this order:

  1. Programme summary — two or three sentences on the product, the market, the target launch window and the expected annual volume. This is the part that lets a supplier understand whether they are being asked to make a prototype or to plan a supply programme, and it materially changes the quality of the response.
  2. Product specification table — one line per specification field, with the requirement and the tolerance. This becomes the sheet the quotation is priced against.
  3. Quotation table — a table with the line items from the commercial scope above, and columns for each requested volume break. Ask explicitly that unavailable items be marked as "not included" rather than left blank, because a blank is ambiguous and a zero is a claim.
  4. Evidence and documentation** — a list of what you need to see with the quotation: business registration, the certifications that apply to your market, and a description of the quality control process applied to your order. Ask for the scope and the expiry date of any certificate, not just the name of the certificate, because the scope is what determines whether it is useful to you.
  5. Terms and response deadline** — Incoterm and named place, payment terms, currency, the date the quotation is valid, and the date by which you need responses. Add a line asking suppliers to state any assumption they have made that is not covered by this document.

That last line is the single most valuable sentence in the whole request. It moves the burden of unstated assumptions onto the responder, where they belong, and it makes a quote with a hidden condition visible instead of buried.

How to Read the Responses

When the quotations arrive, resist the temptation to sort them by the headline price. Instead, normalise them. Take the itemised lines from each quotation and recompute the unit price per metre at each volume break, then recompute a landed cost using a single consistent basis across all suppliers. Two quotations that differ by twelve percent on the headline frequently differ by two percent on the normalised landed basis, and the second pair is the one you can act on.

Then look for the asymmetries. A quote that is materially below the others in the setup charge and materially above in the unit price is telling you that the supplier expects to be paid on repeat volume, and it is worth asking directly whether they would hold that price. A quote that is materially below in the unit price and above in the decoration charge may be assuming a different decoration method than you specified. A quote that excludes certification testing may simply not have included it in the scope you gave, which is a clarification rather than a red flag.

Finally, look at what the quotation does not contain. Every missing line is either an assumption the supplier made or an item that has quietly been dropped from the scope. Both are worth a question before the second round, because the second round is where you start negotiating with the supplier who already understands your requirement, and negotiating with someone who misunderstood you is just expensive correspondence.

Common Mistakes That Cost Buyers Real Money

Leaving the width under-specified. A nominal width with no tolerance lets the supplier price to the narrowest acceptable figure, and the buyer receives a ribbon that is correct on paper and short on every finished good that needs the full width.

Assuming decoration setup is free because it is not itemised. Setup is real work with a real cost, and a supplier who absorbs it in the first order is either losing money or planning to recover it later. If setup is not itemised, ask for it to be itemised.

Comparing ex-works with delivered prices. This is the classic error, and it produces the appearance that a cheap factory is dramatically cheaper than it is, because the freight, duty and insurance have been excluded from one side of the comparison.

Not stating the delivery date basis. A quote valid for ninety days with no commitment to lead time tells you nothing about whether the supplier can actually build your schedule, and holiday peak season in the ribbon trade is a genuine constraint rather than a negotiation position.

Accepting the first round without a second round. The first round is a data-gathering exercise. The second round, informed by what you learned about the market range, is where the negotiating happens. Suppliers know this, and they price their first response accordingly.

A Practical Sequence for Your Next Ribbon RFQ

If you are about to run an RFQ for a private label ribbon programme, the sequence below is a realistic working plan.

Days one to three: write the specification. Use the four blocks above as the checklist. Any field you cannot fill in is a field you do not yet understand well enough to buy on, and it is better to resolve that now than to receive eight different answers to it.

Days three to four: build the document. Write the programme summary, the specification table, the quotation table and the terms in a single file. Have somebody who is not the author read it and ask them to quote from it. If they cannot, the document is not ready.

Days four to five: shortlist the supplier panel. Send to a panel wide enough to give you a range and narrow enough that you can actually compare the responses. Six to eight suppliers is usually enough. Include at least one factory rather than only intermediaries, because you need a price reference that reflects the mill rather than the margin stack.

Days five to ten: read and normalise. Rebuild every quotation on a common basis. List every assumption and every excluded item. Prepare one clarification list that goes to all suppliers, so that each receives the same question and the answers are directly comparable.

Days ten to fourteen: second round and award. Take the clarification responses, narrow to two or three, and negotiate. At this point you are negotiating on price and terms with suppliers who already understand the specification, which is the only kind of negotiation that produces a durable agreement.

Two weeks from first request to award is a realistic cycle for a programme of this type, and it is faster than the alternatives. The usual alternative is not a longer cycle; it is a shorter one that ends in a sample that cannot be mass produced, or a quote that turns out to have excluded the thing that mattered most.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions procurement managers and brand owners most often ask us about writing and running a ribbon OEM RFQ: how long it should take, what to include, why quotations come back so far apart, and how to compare quotes that use different terms. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How long should a ribbon OEM RFQ take to write, and how much detail does it need?

For a straightforward custom printed ribbon programme, two to three hours is realistic if you work from the four blocks in this guide: construction and material, dimensions and tolerances, decoration, and packing, quantity and delivery. The detail that matters most is not length but precision. A request that states a width and a tolerance, a decoration method with a colour count, a packing format and a delivery basis will produce comparable quotations. A request that says a quality ribbon with your design, delivered to Europe, will produce eight responses that price five different products. If a field genuinely does not apply, say so explicitly in the document rather than leaving it out, because a stated omission is unambiguous and a silent omission is not.

2. Should I include artwork in the RFQ, or wait until the supplier is selected?

Include what you have and be explicit about its status. If your design is not final, attach the concept and state both the target date for final artwork and what happens if that date passes, which is usually a requote. This protects both sides. The supplier is not quoting on a guess, and you are not accepting a price built on a contingency for changes you have not yet made. Many buyers also benefit from asking each supplier to price two scenarios, one on your current design and one on a simplified alternative, because the difference between a six-colour print and a single-colour print is often large enough to change the product positioning, and seeing both numbers side by side is more useful than a single figure.

3. Why do quotes from different suppliers differ so much when the specification is the same?

It is usually not the product that differs but the scope. In our experience the same ribbon request gets priced five different ways because one supplier included the decoration setup in the unit price, another itemised it; one quoted ex-works and another delivered; one priced the nominal width and another the minimum width; one included the certification testing and another did not; and one treated the order as a repeat and discounted the setup accordingly. None of these suppliers is being dishonest. They are resolving genuine ambiguities in the request in the way that suits their commercial position, and the price spread you see is largely the cost of those silent assumptions. The remedy is not to hunt for the honest supplier; it is to write a request with no ambiguities left in it.

4. What is the single most useful line item to insist on seeing separately?

Decoration setup, and the tooling or plate charges that sit behind it. Setup is the line where the largest number of assumptions accumulate, and it is the one that is most often absorbed into a headline price and therefore invisible in a comparison. Ask for it as separate items: artwork preparation, screen or plate setup, and the decoration charge per unit at the quantity ordered. Also ask whether any tooling or plate charge is refundable and on what terms, because in some markets a brand owner expects to recover a plate when the programme ends, and in others it is simply a consumption cost. Both are defensible, but only one of them can be what you expected if you never ask.

5. How many suppliers should I include in the first round?

Six to eight is usually the right number: wide enough that you learn the real market range, narrow enough that you can read every response carefully. Include at least one manufacturer rather than only sourcing intermediaries, because you need one price reference that reflects the mill rather than a margin stack, and because that reference is what tells you whether an intermediary's quote is reasonable. If you are sourcing a large programme or entering a market with unusual compliance requirements, eight is the better number. If you are sourcing a small pilot, five is usually sufficient, provided at least one is a factory.

6. Should I negotiate the first round of quotations, or wait?

Treat the first round as data collection rather than as a negotiation, and expect the prices to be higher than the eventual outcome. Suppliers know a first round is a first round, and they price it accordingly. What matters in the first round is that the responses are normalised onto a common basis, because a price comparison that mixes delivered and ex-works terms is not a comparison at all and will lead you to the wrong award. Once every response is normalised and every assumption and excluded item is listed, the second round becomes a genuine negotiation with suppliers who already understand your specification, which is where the real value is found.

7. How long should I allow between sending the RFQ and placing an order?

Allow roughly two weeks for a standard programme, and treat that as a reasonable working estimate rather than a promise from any individual supplier. The time goes into three activities: your own preparation and internal review, the suppliers' pricing and clarification cycle, and the second round with a smaller panel. For programmes with more complex decoration, or where certification testing is required, allow longer. For a simple printed ribbon with a confirmed construction and a confirmed volume, a compressed cycle is possible, but compressing the specification stage is what causes the expensive mistakes, because a rushed specification is the one that gets reinterpreted by eight suppliers in eight different ways.
