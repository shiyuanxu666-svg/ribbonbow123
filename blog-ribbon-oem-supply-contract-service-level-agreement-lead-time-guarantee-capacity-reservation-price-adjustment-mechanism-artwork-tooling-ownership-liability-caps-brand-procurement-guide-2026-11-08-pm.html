Every ribbon programme has a purchase order. Very few have an agreement that governs what happens when the purchase order cannot be met, when the price of polyester moves, when a print plate is used for someone else's order, or when a lot of ribbon arrives with a shade you did not approve.

That gap is not a technical problem. It is a commercial one, and it is where the expensive disagreements live. A factory that has been reasonable for six years can still end a relationship badly, not through bad faith but because there was no rule to apply. Both sides then discover, at the moment of conflict, what their leverage happens to be — and the buyer usually discovers it too late to do anything with the answer.

This guide covers what to write into a ribbon OEM supply agreement and a service level agreement, and why each clause exists. It is written for brand buyers, procurement leads and owners who will eventually be the person signing, and it covers nine commercial areas: lead time guarantees, capacity reservation, price adjustment, artwork and tooling ownership, quality warranty, liability and insurance, compliance responsibility, document hierarchy and dispute resolution, and the review mechanism that keeps the agreement alive as your programme changes.

Why the Purchase Order Is Not an Agreement

A purchase order is a unilateral instruction to supply. It creates a commercial moment, not a commercial relationship. It says what, how much and at what price, and it is silent on everything that determines what happens next.

Consider the five most common commercial events in a ribbon programme, and what a purchase order says about each of them:

The eventWhat the purchase order saysWhat you actually needed
Delivery is lateNothingA defined lead time, a calendar basis that excludes the Lunar New Year shutdown, and a remedy
Material prices moveA fixed priceA formula, a threshold band and an annual cap
Your print plate is used on another customer's orderNothingAn identified tooling schedule with ownership and a return obligation
A lot arrives off-shadeA quantity and a priceA warranty tied to the approved sample, a claim window and a remedy clock
Your programme grows 40% for peakNothingA capacity reservation mechanism with a notice period and a minimum volume

Each of those gaps is a conversation that will happen at the worst possible moment: when the factory is busy, when you have a delivery date you cannot move, or when you have already paid for the goods. A written clause moves the conversation forward to the moment when both parties can still reason about it.

There is also a structural reason to write this properly. A supply agreement is a price. Not the unit price — the price of the risk you are carrying. A factory asked to hold a slot, carry your tooling, absorb a shade failure and guarantee a date is being asked to hold risk, and it will price that. The correct conclusion is not to avoid asking, because the risk does not disappear when it is not written down; it simply moves to whichever party happens to be weaker at the moment it is needed. The correct conclusion is to ask clearly, in a way that the factory can price, and to receive a supplier who is willing to commit because they have been given a corresponding commitment in return.

Clause One: Lead Time Guarantee With a Real Remedy

This is the clause buyers think is routine and write in one line, and it is the one that fails most often.

A usable lead time clause needs four components.

A defined start point. A lead time that starts when the order arrives is meaningless if the factory is waiting on yarn. The start point should be the moment the order is confirmed against a signed specification and the material is available, or a stated date if you need to commit to a specific despatch. If you are booking a delivery date with a customer, the start point should be fixed to a calendar date so that it does not slide when the factory has a queue.

A defined end point. Despatch, not production completion. A factory that has finished weaving your ribbon on Friday and still needs four days for slitting, inspection, packing and consolidation has not delivered, and if your clause says “production completed” you have no remedy for the four days your customer is waiting.

A calendar basis. This is the detail that makes the clause unusable for half the year. Chinese manufacturing has a Lunar New Year shutdown of roughly two to three weeks, plus travel days either side, and the effective loss to a factory is close to a month. If the lead time is expressed in working days from an unspecified start, a January order and a February order get the same number of days and completely different calendar outcomes. State the exclusions explicitly: public holidays, the Lunar New Year shutdown period, and any days on which the factory is closed, and state the shutdown dates rather than a general formula. A clause that names the 2027 shutdown window is worth more than a general exclusion clause, because it can be planned against.

A remedy. A guarantee without a remedy is a statement of intent. In practice, the workable remedy in this supply chain is a service credit for each day late, capped at a stated percentage of the order value, with a stated escalation for delay beyond a threshold. It is enforceable because it is arithmetically simple, it is proportionate because the cap keeps it smaller than the loss, and it is preferable to a right to cancel, which applies only where the delay is catastrophic and therefore rarely applies at all. Add a separate right to cancel for the specific case where the delay exceeds a stated number of days, because the credit will not rescue a launch date.

Two things to avoid. First, a lead time expressed as a range. “25 to 35 days” means you will receive the goods on day 35 and the factory will describe it as on time. Use a single number with a stated allowance for force majeure and nothing else. Second, a remedy that depends on the factory choosing to offer credit. Write it as an entitlement, not as a courtesy the factory may extend.

Clause Two: Capacity Reservation for the Months That Matter

Volume commitment and capacity reservation are different obligations, and most buyers write only the first and assume they have the second.

A volume commitment is your promise to buy. It gives the factory revenue certainty, it is the basis of a price discount, and it is the obligation most supply agreements already have.

A capacity reservation is the factory's promise to hold a specific slot. It is the only thing that protects a launch date, and it is the thing almost never written down.

The distinction matters in December. In peak season, a factory is oversubscribed and something has to give, and the default mechanism in almost every factory is first-come-first-served by purchase order date. Your programme's position in the December queue then depends on when you happened to place a purchase order, not on how much business you do or how important your launch is. A reservation changes that by naming a specific week or window and committing the factory to hold it.

A workable reservation clause specifies:

  • Notice period. How far ahead you must book. We advise ninety days for a base lot on a fixed launch, and longer for peak-season volume where factory scheduling is more loaded.
  • Reserved window. The specific week or date range, and ideally the line family or construction group, because a construction that only one line can run has no flexibility at all.
  • Minimum committed quantity. The floor the factory is holding capacity against, which protects the factory from reserving a slot against an order that never materialises.
  • Advance or deposit. Usually a percentage of the reserved value. This is what makes the reservation real for the factory and therefore enforceably real for you.
  • Deadline for confirming the balance. Without it, a reserved slot becomes a slot the factory cannot sell to anyone else, and the factory will not accept that.
  • Re-booking rule. What happens if the balance arrives late or the programme shrinks. This should be explicit, because it is the clause that decides whether a factory will agree to reserve in the first place.
  • A second window covering the restart period. If your programme runs January to June, a reservation that covers January to mid-February protects the pre-break production, but the goods you need in April are still exposed to the post-holiday queue. Booking both at once, in the same conversation, is the single most useful thing a buyer can do about seasonal delay.

One drafting point that is often missed: the reservation should be tied to a construction and a capacity commitment, not to a percentage of the factory's total capacity. A factory that commits to “5 per cent of capacity for your programme” has committed to nothing, because capacity for your construction may be one line and that line may be busy. Ask for the basis: lines capable of running your construction, the number of constructions on those lines per month, and the monthly output. Those three numbers let you test whether the reservation is physically possible, and they make the clause verifiable rather than aspirational.

Clause Three: A Price Adjustment Mechanism That Nobody Has to Negotiate

Polyester, nylon, rayon and viscose all move. The question is not whether your price will move but whether it will move by rule or by conversation.

A workable mechanism has five parts, and each of them matters.

The material. Name the specific input your product depends on and the proportion of the material cost it represents. “Raw materials” is unusable, because the factory will claim that a rise in electricity or labour is a raw material movement.

The index. Name the published index used to track it and where it is published. Using an unspecified market price means the party with the information advantage wins every time.

The base. State the base price and the base index value at the date of signature. This is the reference point against which every future adjustment is measured, and without it there is nothing to calculate.

The threshold band. Specify a band around the base within which nothing happens. This is the most important and most frequently omitted element. A band of plus or minus five per cent absorbs ordinary fluctuation with no paperwork, and it stops a small index move becoming a quarterly price discussion. Without a band, the agreement generates administrative cost on both sides and the factory, who controls the relationship, controls the timing.

The cap. An annual limit on total adjustment. This protects you from an extreme spike arriving in one invoice, and it is the element that makes a factory willing to accept a formula at all.

The shape of the adjustment should also be stated: whether the full movement beyond the band passes through, or a stated proportion of it, and whether it applies to the material component only or to the whole product price. The second formulation is much easier for a factory to accept and much easier for both sides to administer, and it usually ends up costing the buyer very little because the material share of a ribbon price is meaningful but not dominant.

Two drafting notes. First, define the review point: the date each year at which the base index is reset and the current price is restated. Without a named date, the reset happens when the factory is ready for it. Second, give yourself a price-protection floor: a mechanism that adjusts a price down as well as up, but with a slower or capped downward movement, so that an index collapse does not permanently reset your cost base at the low point. This is a small concession for the factory to make and it materially reduces the risk of the agreement to you.

If your agreement genuinely cannot carry a formula, the fallback is a fixed price for twelve months with a defined annual review point, not an open-ended escalator. A fixed price with a scheduled review is a far better position than a variable price with no formula, because at least the number you budget against does not change in the middle of the quarter.

Clause Four: Artwork, Tooling and Custom Equipment

This is the area where buyers lose assets quietly, and the mechanism is always the same: something custom is made, nobody records that it exists, and a year later it cannot be identified or recovered.

Artwork should pass to you on creation or on payment, and the clause should say so expressly, including your right to grant a sublicence to another factory. Also specify whether the factory may use your artwork, your designs or your brand marks for its own purposes — showing them at a trade fair, using them in a portfolio, or producing ribbon for a competing brand. This is not a paranoid request. A factory that shows your proprietary seasonal range at a trade fair has disclosed your launch plan, and a factory that has your artwork is a factory that can be asked by someone else whether it can make it.

Tooling is different from artwork, and must be handled separately. Custom printing plates and screens, dies and foil plates, embroidery and sewing frames, slitting blades set for your width, custom winding mandrels and any bespoke jig are all tooling. They must be:

  1. Identified in a schedule. A general clause covering “any tooling used in the production of the goods” is very difficult to enforce against a factory that has quietly repurposed your plate on someone else's order. A named schedule, signed by both parties, is enforceable.
  2. Owned by you, held as bailee by the factory. The factory has physical custody and a duty of care, and no right to use the tooling except to produce your goods. Say that expressly, because otherwise a factory that is between orders and under pressure on utilisation has an economic incentive to run your plate for someone else, and it may not even see this as dishonest.
  3. Marked and inventoried. Require a physical inventory with your asset reference on the tool itself, and an annual confirmation that the inventory is complete and in good condition. This is the cheapest insurance in the whole agreement: it is a one-page schedule and an annual signature.
  4. Returned on termination, with a timeframe. Usually thirty to sixty days, at your cost, including the case where you terminate for the factory's breach. Specify the condition standard, because a tool returned unusable is not a return.
  5. Handled if you leave the supplier. The cleanest position is that the tooling stays with you and the factory has a time-limited obligation to make it available for the final production run and the last set of approvals, and to return it thereafter. Where the factory funds the tooling as part of the unit price, an alternative is a depreciation schedule on the tooling value, which recovers the cost when the relationship ends.

Samples and the retained-sample archive also need ownership clarity, because they are evidence. Who owns the approved samples held by the factory, who owns the golden samples held by you, and what happens to both when the relationship ends.

Your own tooling, if you supply it — a print plate, a die, a pattern — needs a different clause: it remains your property, it must be returned on request, and it must not be used for any other customer. If you send a plate to a factory, assume it will be used on someone else's job unless the agreement prevents it, because on a busy line a plate sitting on the bench is an idle asset with a clear opportunity cost.

Clause Five: Quality Warranty, Remedy and the Clock

The warranty clause ties the goods to two documents: the purchase specification and the approved sample. Say that conformity to either is conformity to the contract. The sample matters because a specification alone cannot describe a shade or a hand, and the sample alone cannot cover the things that are better stated in words.

The warranty should then define:

  • The covered defect classes, named. Width deviation, shade deviation against the approved reference, print registration failure, print adhesion failure, fraying or yarn pull-out, wrong core, wrong roll length, contamination, incorrect labelling, short quantity. Naming them removes the argument about whether a defect is a “quality issue” or a “preference”.
  • The despatch inspection standard. For most programmes, ISO 2859-1 or ANSI Z1.4, general inspection level II, with an agreed AQL, plus the four to six performance tests relevant to the construction: colour difference, rub fastness, width, tensile or burst, print adhesion and shrinkage after wash or heat. State the standard so the AQL has a sampling plan attached to it; an AQL without a plan is not a test.
  • The failure procedure. A failed lot is quarantined and not shipped without a written concession signed by you. This is worth having even if you never use it, because its existence changes the behaviour of the despatch inspection.
  • The remedy. Replacement, rework or credit, at your election for the first two. Do not leave the factory to choose, because a factory offered a choice will always choose credit, and credit for goods that have already been consumed by your production line is not a remedy.
  • The clock. This is where warranties fail in practice. A claim window in days from arrival. Inspection within a stated number of hours of arrival, so that a factory cannot refuse inspection because the goods have already been cut up. Acknowledgement of the claim within a stated number of days. Resolution within a stated number of days.
  • The evidence position. The factory retains a counter sample from each approved lot, and provides the lot number, the AQL inspection report and the colour-difference readings on request. Without this, a shade dispute becomes an argument from memory, and memory is not evidence.

Two final points on the warranty. First, tie the warranty to a defined shelf life rather than to an open-ended period, and state it per construction, because a plastic woven tape and a printed organza have very different service lives and different failure modes. Second, carve out what the warranty does not cover in a way that is factual rather than a general exclusion: defects caused by storage outside the stated temperature and humidity range, by dyeing or cleaning with solvents not approved for the construction, by mechanical damage after delivery, or by use inconsistent with the stated end application. A narrowly drawn exclusion on genuinely buyer-caused damage is easier to agree than a blanket one, and it protects you when the factory tries to apply it broadly later.

Clause Six: Liability, Insurance and the Compliance Question

Do not attempt to exclude liability for everything, because a factory that has accepted unlimited liability for a defect in a polyester ribbon will price that risk into every quote, and the resulting price is worse than the exposure you were trying to avoid. The workable structure is tiered, and the tiers reflect how different the risks are.

Tier one: commercial commitment. The factory is responsible for goods failing to conform to the agreed specification and must replace, rework or credit them. This is the remedy that actually solves your commercial problem and should be your primary protection. It needs no cap, because it is bounded by the value of the goods.

Tier two: consequential loss. Cap this at a stated multiple of the order value or a fixed figure, and expressly exclude the categories that make agreements unnegotiable: lost profit, lost sales, production downtime and business interruption. These are the exclusions the factory will ask for, and they are the right ones to give, because the exposure they describe is unbounded and largely outside the factory's control.

Tier three: third-party liability and product safety. This is the tier where you should not accept a low cap, and where the real risk sits. A ribbon going into a food or cosmetic gift set, a pet accessory or a child's product can carry a chemical compliance failure, and the consequence for you is not the value of the ribbon; it is a batch withdrawal, a retailer suspension and a regulatory file. This tier should require the factory to warrant that materials comply with the chemical safety requirements applicable in the destination market, to provide test reports on request and not only on file, and to maintain product liability insurance with the certificate available for inspection.

On that last point, one thing to establish explicitly for your own business: whether your retailer or brand customer requires you to hold product liability cover as well. In many consumer programmes the answer is yes, and it is cheaper for you to hold it than for the factory to hold it, because the factory's policy may not cover your market or your product classification. Find out who the contract requires to be the insured, and do not discover the answer at the audit.

Also allocate responsibility for regulatory and chemical compliance explicitly. The factory warrants the materials it supplies; you warrant how the finished product is labelled, marketed and used. That allocation is not a formality, because it determines who answers the question when a test report is requested by a retailer at 48 hours' notice.

Clause Seven: Document Hierarchy and Dispute Resolution

The great majority of ribbon supply disputes are factual disagreements about what was agreed. They resolve quickly when the agreement contains an agreed order of precedence, because the argument collapses to a question of which document governs.

Specify the hierarchy explicitly. A workable order, from highest to lowest, is:

  1. The signed purchase specification and the signed sample approval record, together forming the product standard
  2. The agreed quality and inspection standards, including the AQL and the performance tests
  3. The production release note and the despatch inspection report for the specific lot
  4. The purchase order and the delivery schedule
  5. Formal correspondence, and last informal messages, chats and unapproved email exchanges

That last line matters more than it looks. In a dispute about a shade or a width, the material that actually settles it is the approved sample, the retained counter sample and the release paperwork for the lot. A written agreement that puts the informal correspondence last, explicitly, tells both sides that evidence is what counts. That is a cheap way to protect yourself against a decision made on a verbal assurance.

For the formal route, the practical options are arbitration at a named institution under rules both parties can use, or the courts of an agreed jurisdiction. The choice matters less than three details: the language of the agreement and of any evidence is unambiguous; there is a mechanism for interim relief if goods are held or payments are disputed; and there is a stated venue that is genuinely accessible rather than nominal.

But put an escalation ladder in front of the formal route, because almost nothing should reach the formal route in a ribbon supply relationship. A named contact at each company, a defined period to resolve at management level, and only then the formal process. The purpose of the ladder is not to be nice; it is that the questions that reach the formal stage are almost always questions of fact that could have been answered by a document, and the people who can answer them are the ones who know the programme.

Clause Eight: Review, Amendment and the Programme That Changes

An agreement that never changes is an agreement that will be quietly ignored. Programmes move: the range expands, a construction is discontinued, a peak becomes permanent, a new market adds a labelling requirement, and the factory changes a machine or moves a process to a second site.

Three mechanisms keep it alive.

A scheduled review. An annual review of price basis, forecast volumes, the quality record, the list of constructions and the tooling inventory. Hold it at a defined time of year, before the peak booking window closes, because that is when you want the reservation terms refreshed for the following year.

A change control clause. When either party wants to change something, specify how: notice in writing, an impact assessment covering price, lead time and any tooling impact, and a signature. Crucially, include a re-approval trigger for manufacturing-side changes: a change of production site or line, a material substitution, a change of decoration method, or a change of dye or yarn source must be notified and, where it affects the product, re-approved against a new sample. Without this, the factory can make a change that is perfectly reasonable on its side and quietly alters your product.

A termination and survival clause. What happens on either side ending the relationship. Notice periods, the effect on open orders, the return of tooling and documents, the survival of the warranty for goods already delivered, and the survival of the confidentiality and intellectual property terms. The clause people most often forget is the survival period for the warranty: without a stated period, a claim made in year four against goods delivered in year one falls outside the term, and the goods may well still be on a shelf.

Building the Agreement Into Practice

An agreement only earns its keep if it is usable. Five habits make the difference.

Keep a contract register. One row per agreement: supplier, product scope, start date, review date, price adjustment basis, tooling location, reservation windows booked, and the current claim status. This is the single most useful document in a multi-supplier ribbon programme, because the failure mode is not bad clauses, it is forgetting what was agreed.

Have one owner. A named person who holds the master agreement, the amendments, the tooling schedule and the claim log. Distributed ownership of a contract is the same as no ownership.

Read it with the factory, not at them. The first time you go through the clauses with a supplier, frame it as making the relationship predictable for both of you. A factory that understands that a lead time remedy is cheaper for it than a renegotiated relationship will agree to it. A factory handed a legal document without context will price it and resist it, and you will get a worse agreement for less protection.

Tie the agreement to the specification. Cross-reference it. Every clause should point at a document that exists and is version-controlled, and every document should point back at the agreement. An agreement that references a specification that has been superseded is worse than no agreement, because it creates a false impression of clarity.

Review the claim log quarterly. Four numbers: how many claims were raised, how many were upheld, how many days each took to resolve, and what they were about. Claims clustered on shade across several factories usually indicate an approval problem rather than a supplier problem, and claims clustered on one factory's width usually indicate a control plan gap. These four numbers will tell you where your programme is losing control, and they are the same four numbers that make a factory relationship worth keeping.

What to Ask a Factory

These eight questions will tell you quickly whether a factory takes its commercial relationships seriously, and they are all reasonable to ask:

  1. Will you accept a lead time clause with a defined start point, despatch as the end point, and a service credit for each day late?
  2. How do you allocate capacity when oversubscribed, and what would a reserved production slot require from us?
  3. What raw materials does this product depend on, and would you accept a price adjustment formula with a threshold band and an annual cap?
  4. Do you accept a tooling schedule with our asset references on the tools, an annual inventory, and a return obligation?
  5. What is your standard warranty position on off-specification goods, and what claim window do you work to?
  6. Do you hold product liability insurance, and can we see the certificate and its territorial and product scope?
  7. How do you handle a dispute about a shade or a width, and what evidence do you need from us to resolve it?
  8. What happens to our tooling and our documents if we end the relationship, and how would we recover them?

Note what these questions reveal. A factory that asks you what remedy structure you prefer, and then proposes one, is a factory that operates on contracts. A factory that answers them with “we always handle our customers well” is telling you that its commercial agreements are oral, and that in the year it stops going well, you will have nothing to point at.

The Payoff

None of this makes the ribbon better, and much of it will never be tested. That is the point.

Consider what the agreement buys you in three situations. When polyester moves eighteen per cent in a quarter, the price adjustment formula settles it in an afternoon instead of a month, and neither side is spending the relationship on it. When a delivery is late by nine days against a launch date, the service credit is what pays for the expedite, and the fact that the remedy was agreed in advance is what makes the factory act rather than explain. And when a print plate is needed for a last-minute order, the tooling schedule means it is on the shelf with your reference on it, rather than on another customer's line.

There is a fourth effect, and it is the one that matters most in a category like this. In ribbon, the product is visually trivial, the switching cost for your customer is nearly zero, and the competitive set is willing to be cheaper than you at any moment. What is genuinely hard for a competitor to copy is a supplier relationship where both sides know exactly what happens when something goes wrong. That is worth more than a price concession, and it is the reason we would always rather a programme be run on a written agreement than on goodwill.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand buyers, procurement leads and owners most often ask us about ribbon supply agreements: what a contract should cover beyond price and quantity, how to write a lead time guarantee the factory will honour, how price adjustment and capacity reservation actually work, who should own artwork and tooling, how to limit liability without making the deal unusable, and how disputes about shade and width get resolved. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What should a ribbon OEM supply agreement actually cover beyond price and quantity?

A usable ribbon supply agreement covers six commercial areas that a purchase order never does: lead time and its remedies, capacity reservation for peak periods, how price may change over the life of the agreement, ownership of artwork, tooling, samples and any custom-made equipment, quality warranty and remedies, and the limits of each party's liability. The purpose of each clause is the same: to convert a question that will otherwise be answered by whoever has more leverage at the moment, into a rule that was agreed when neither party was under pressure. In our experience the clauses that cause the most conflict are the ones buyers skip because they feel like legal formalities: the lead time remedy and the price adjustment mechanism. Those two are the ones that decide who absorbs a delay caused by a material shortage or a spike in polyester or nylon price.

2. How do I write a lead time guarantee that the factory will actually honour?

A lead time clause needs four components, not one. A defined start point, because a lead time that starts when the order arrives is meaningless if the factory is waiting on yarn; use the point at which the order is confirmed against a specification and the material is available. A defined end point, ideally despatch rather than production completion. A calendar basis, because ribbon production in China has a Lunar New Year shutdown of roughly two to three weeks that must be excluded explicitly, otherwise the clause is unusable from December to March. And a remedy, because a guarantee without a remedy is a statement of intent: a fixed credit for each day late, capped at a stated percentage of the order value, is enforceable and common in practice, whereas a right to cancel applies only to the orders where delay is actually catastrophic. Write the remedy so it costs the factory less to hit the date than to miss it.

3. What is a capacity reservation clause and how is it different from a volume commitment?

A volume commitment is about how much you promise to buy. A capacity reservation is about how much capacity the factory promises to hold for you. They are separate obligations and they protect different things. Your volume commitment gives the factory certainty of revenue and is what justifies a price discount. Their reservation gives you certainty of a slot in a specific week, which is the only thing that actually protects a launch date. The reservation typically specifies a notice period, a minimum committed quantity for the reserved window, a deposit or advance, a deadline for confirming the balance, and a re-booking rule if the balance arrives late. The most useful practical addition is a second reservation covering the restart weeks immediately after the Lunar New Year break, because the post-holiday queue is where spring deliveries actually die.

4. How should price adjustment work in a long-term ribbon supply agreement?

Use a formula, not a negotiation, and define the indices precisely. A workable mechanism names the specific raw material the product depends on, names the published index used to track it, sets a base price and the base index value at the time of signing, specifies a threshold band around which no adjustment occurs, and then adjusts by a stated proportion of the material movement beyond that band, subject to a stated annual cap. The threshold matters as much as the formula: it absorbs normal fluctuation without paperwork, and it stops a small index move becoming a price conversation every quarter. The cap protects you from an extreme spike arriving in a single invoice. The common failure is a clause with no threshold and no cap, which means every quarter becomes a price negotiation and the factory, holding the delivery relationship, effectively holds the price. If your agreement cannot carry a formula, insist on a fixed annual price with a defined annual review point instead, because a fixed price with a review is better than an open-ended escalator.

5. Who should own the artwork, tooling and custom equipment used to make my ribbon?

You, and the clause should say so in that many words, with a trigger for transfer and a schedule for it. Artwork ownership should pass to you on creation or on payment, and it should expressly include the right to grant a sublicence to a competing supplier. Tooling is different from artwork: custom printing plates, dies, embroidery frames, slitting blades set for your width and any bespoke winding mandrel should be identified in a schedule, and the agreement should state whether the factory owns them and licenses their use, or whether you own them and the factory holds them as bailee. The schedule matters more than the principle, because tooling is only protectable if it is identifiable, and a general clause covering &ldquo;any tooling used in the production of the goods&rdquo; is very difficult to enforce against a factory that has quietly put a plate to work on someone else's order. Include a return obligation with a reasonable timeframe if you terminate, and specify what happens to the tooling if you leave the supplier: it stays with you, and the factory has a time-limited obligation to make it available for the last run of the programme.

6. How do I limit a supplier's liability without making the agreement commercially unusable?

Do not attempt to exclude liability for everything, because a factory that has agreed unlimited liability for a defect in a polyester ribbon will price that risk into every quote and the resulting price is worse than the exposure. The workable structure is a tiered one. Commercial commitment: the factory is responsible for the goods failing to conform to the agreed specification and is required to replace, rework or credit them, which is the remedy that actually solves your commercial problem and should be your primary protection. Consequential loss: cap this at a stated multiple of the order value or a fixed amount, and exclude categories such as lost profit and production downtime, because these are the categories that make agreements unnegotiable. Third-party liability and product safety: this is the one area where you should not accept a low cap, because a ribbon used in a food or cosmetic gift set that carries a chemical compliance failure can expose you to a regulatory claim and a retailer suspension that dwarf the value of the order. Require the factory to warrant that materials comply with the chemical safety requirements applicable in the destination market, to provide test reports on request, and to maintain product liability insurance with the certificate available for inspection, while confirming whether your customer requires you to carry it as well.

7. What should the quality warranty clause cover and what should happen when a lot fails?

The warranty should tie the goods to two documents: the purchase specification and the approved sample, and it should say that conformity to either is conformity to the contract. It should define the defect classes that are covered, so that width deviation, shade deviation, print registration failure, fraying, wrong core, wrong length and contamination are all named rather than left to a general standard of quality. It should state the inspection level and the acceptance standard used at despatch, which for most programmes should be ISO 2859-1 or ANSI Z1.4 general inspection level II with an agreed AQL, and it should specify that a failed lot is quarantined and not shipped until a written concession is signed. Then it needs the remedy and the clock: replacement or credit within a stated number of days of a documented rejection, inspection within a stated number of hours of arrival, and a claim window in days from arrival. Without the clock, the warranty is unenforceable in practice, because the factory will always be waiting for a resolution on a lot that has already been consumed by your production line.

8. What dispute resolution and governing law terms are appropriate for a Chinese ribbon supplier?

Keep it simple and keep it enforceable where the assets are. Specify the governing law, and specify the forum, and make sure the forum is somewhere the dispute can actually be heard. For a supply agreement with a Chinese factory, the practical options are arbitration in a named institution under rules that both parties can use, or the courts of the agreed jurisdiction, and the choice is less important than two practical details: that the language of the agreement and of any evidence is unambiguous, and that there is a mechanism for interim relief if goods are held or payments are disputed. Whatever you choose, write an escalation ladder before the formal route: a named contact at each company, a defined period to resolve at management level, and only then the formal process. The great majority of supply disputes are factual disagreements about what was agreed, and they resolve quickly if the agreement contains an agreed document hierarchy, because the argument collapses to a question of which document governs. Specify that hierarchy explicitly, and note that in a dispute, correspondence and the approved sample register together with the release paperwork outrank any informal message, which is exactly the evidence set that actually settles a ribbon quality dispute.
