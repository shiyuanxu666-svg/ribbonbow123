The core insight: sampling is the part of ribbon sourcing where the logistics is almost never specified, and it is the part where a programme most often breaks. Not because the ribbon was wrong - because a sample arrived crushed, because a courier delivered on a Friday afternoon and sat in a depot over the weekend, or because a duty and VAT invoice was presented to the recipient on arrival and the package was refused.

Each of those failures is avoidable and cheap to prevent. Each of them is also invisible in the quotation, because sample shipping is usually quoted as an afterthought, an included courtesy or a line item the buyer does not examine. This guide is about the layer between the mill and your desk: how samples are packed, who chooses the courier, what the shipping terms mean, what customs paperwork a ribbon sample requires, and how to build a sampling workflow that scales without turning into an expense the programme cannot carry.

It is written for brand owners, product developers and procurement managers who run sampling programmes with offshore manufacturers, and for the sourcing managers who need to control the logistics without turning every sample into a formal procurement exercise.

The Most Undermanaged Layer in Ribbon Sourcing

Compare how carefully a buying team specifies a bulk order against how carefully it specifies a sample. The bulk order gets a specification sheet, a lead time, an incoterm, a quality standard, a packing list and a payment term. The sample gets a courier account and a note saying "please send samples, DHL account 12345678".

That asymmetry is understandable. A sample is small, cheap and provisional. It is also, in many sampling programmes, the single most information-dense object in the entire sourcing cycle - it may carry the colour approval, the print registration approval, the jacquard strike-off approval, the handle assessment, the width measurement and the construction verification all at once. When a sample is lost or arrives damaged, the buyer usually does not simply re-order it. They re-order it, wait again, and by then the development timeline has slipped by a week with no record anywhere of why.

Three things make sample shipping more particular than people expect.

Sample value is asymmetric to its shipping cost. A twenty-metre sample of a jacquard ribbon may have cost the mill several hundred dollars of setup to produce, and it may represent hours of development work that cannot be recovered. The courier bill to move it might be forty dollars. The ratio means that express shipping is frequently the correct economic decision even for small samples, because the cost of the sample exceeding a certain value is essentially the entire programme cost of that stage.

The recipient is usually not the buyer. Samples frequently go to a designer, a product developer, a studio, an agent or a factory in another country entirely. Every additional destination adds a customs question, a delivery question and a chance that the person receiving it does not know what to do with it.

Samples cross borders far more often than bulk shipments do. A bulk order moving monthly from one country to another gets a customs routine established. A sample moving to six different studios across three countries, at irregular intervals, does not - and every one of those shipments is a standalone customs event with its own paperwork, its own duty treatment and its own chance of a surprise.

What a Sample Actually Does in Your Process

Before optimising the shipping, be clear about what the sample is for, because the purpose dictates the handling standard and the courier class.

Colour and finish approval samples need to arrive in a state where appearance is judgeable. That means protected from light, heat, crushing and moisture, and it means they should not be folded or creased. For a colour approval, the sample arriving flat and unmarked is not a nicety - a crease creates a highlight and a shadow that a buyer will read as a shade difference.

Construction and specification samples are cut up, tested, measured or put through a machine. These need to arrive with enough length and width to work with, and they are the least sensitive to appearance, which means they can travel by a slower and cheaper service without any real loss.

Production strike-off samples are the most critical object in the whole programme and the most frequently damaged. They carry the approved colour, the finished construction and the pattern positioning, and they are frequently the physical evidence used in a dispute months later. These need the best protection, the fastest reliable service, and a record of what was shipped and when.

Market or customer samples - the ones you pass to a retailer, a stylist or a customer as a sales tool - carry a different requirement again. These need to look good on arrival, which means presentation quality matters more than anything else.

The mistake is treating these as one category and choosing one courier service for all of them. The right answer is usually two or three service levels matched to sample purpose, with a documented rule for which sample gets which treatment.

Why Sample Shipping Is a Real Cost, Not a Rounding Error

Let us put a number on it, because "small" is a relative term and sampling programmes accumulate.

A brand running a normal development cycle might request twenty to forty samples per programme across two to four sample rounds. At forty dollars a shipment with a domestic courier surcharge, that is eight hundred to sixteen hundred dollars per programme in freight alone, plus the courier account setup fees, plus the labour of packing, labelling and tracking each one. On a programme that nets perhaps fifty thousand dollars over its first year, that is a meaningful percentage - and it is invisible because it never appears on the factory quotation as a production cost.

There are also the costs that are harder to count but larger.

The cost of a lost sample. A lost colour approval sample does not just cost the courier fee. It costs the production time, the material, the setup, and the delay - typically a week or more. On a tight seasonal calendar that delay can be the difference between being on the shelf for the season and missing it.

The cost of a sample that arrives unusable. A crushed or creased sample that fails to be approved is a full round of the sampling cycle repeated. In many programmes the second round is more expensive than the first, because the mill has now produced the sample twice.

The cost of a duty surprise. If a sample arrives collect and the recipient cannot or will not pay, the courier returns it to the sender. The sender then pays return freight, and the sample arrives having travelled twice. Some couriers charge a handling or clearance fee on top, and that fee lands with the sender.

The counter-argument is that fast, tracked, insured shipping reduces total cost, because it removes the loss and damage rate from the programme. The right comparison is not courier cost against zero. It is courier cost against courier cost plus the expected cost of the failures it prevents. On strike-off samples, express is almost always the correct answer.

Choosing a Courier: Express, Air Mail and Economy

Three service classes cover nearly all sample shipping, and they differ on two axes that matter: transit time and the number of times the parcel is handled.

Express courier - DHL, FedEx, UPS, TNT and their regional equivalents. Airport-to-airport in a hub network, with tracking, and usually the option to declare a monetary value for insurance. This is the default for samples whose value or deadline matters: strike-offs, colour approvals going to an external party, and anything going to a customer-facing event. It is also the only class that reliably handles a Friday dispatch for Monday arrival in most international lanes.

Air freight and postal air mail - slower, cheaper, fewer handoffs, and tracked to a varying degree. Suitable for routine specification samples and larger sample quantities where the content is not time-critical. The risk profile is different rather than simply worse: fewer handling points means less crushing risk, but a longer transit means more exposure to delay, and postal services vary enormously in tracking quality and insurance handling.

Consolidated and economy services - a group shipment or a slower surface service, chosen for cost. These are appropriate for bulk sample kits, for shipping a range of stock options to a buyer at once, or for very early-stage development where the buyer has not yet committed to a direction. They are not appropriate for anything with an approval deadline attached.

Three practical criteria should drive the choice, in this order.

Deadline behaviour, not average transit time. A courier quoting three to five days is describing an average that includes customs clearance delays. What matters is the service that meets the date you need if customs is straightforward, and what the fallback is if it is not. Ask for the guaranteed transit for the specific lane, and ask what happens on a delay - whether the courier reroutes, and whether the delivery is time-definite or best-effort.

Handling count. Every handoff is an opportunity for damage. A parcel that passes through five facilities has five chances to be dropped, stacked or crushed. This is why a slower service with fewer handoffs is not automatically worse for fragile goods, and it is the reason ribbon samples should never travel as loose items in an outer carton with void fill.

Traceability and claims. Tracking should be event-level, not just a current-status ping, because you need to know where a parcel was when a claim is made. Insurance terms matter enormously: many services limit liability to a per-kilogram maximum, which for a dense roll of ribbon can be less than the sample's actual value. Check the declared-value limit against the real value of a strike-off before you ship one, and retain the invoice or cost evidence of the sample.

One more practical point: use the same courier for the whole programme. Switching couriers between sample rounds introduces variables that are hard to isolate. If a colour is rejected on round three, you want to know whether the colour changed or the handling changed. Consistency in carrier, in packing method and in the person approving on your side removes a whole category of false signal.

Why Ribbon Samples Are a Special Shipping Problem

Ribbon does not travel like a flat swatch card, and a buyer who has never received fabric samples overseas will underestimate this.

Ribbon is light and voluminous. A ten-metre length of 38 mm satin ribbon is small in weight but occupies a surprising volume when wound. Parcels of this shape get crushed by parcels that are heavier, and a courier network handling thousands of cartons does not have a way of knowing yours is delicate.

Rolled ribbon is protected by its own geometry, but only if it stays on its core. A ribbon wound on a reel core resists crushing far better than one wound loose or on a thin cardboard tube. Off-core or re-wound ribbon flattens, and a flattened ribbon develops a permanent crease pattern that will not recover - which means the crease is present in a sample that a buyer is about to judge for surface quality.

Colour samples need light and heat protection. Saturated shades fade under strong light, and heat in a closed vehicle or a depot during summer can affect a dye. This matters most for approval samples, where the sample is the reference. If a sample has sat in a hot warehouse, the shade you approve may not be the shade the mill produced.

Satin and lustre finishes show handling damage permanently. A handling mark on a matte grosgrain is a minor cosmetic issue. The same mark on a high-shine satin reads as a defect and, because it catches light, can be mistaken for a colour difference.

Some ribbon is genuinely fragile. Metallic, pearlescent and certain novelty constructions are easily creased or rubbed. So are some elastic and fringed products, which can tangle or pull if the package is opened carelessly. Elastic ribbon in particular may be damaged by being folded to fit a smaller carton, so it should be shipped on its core in a box that is the right size rather than compressed into a smaller one.

Width and length combinations create awkward parcels. Very wide ribbon on a short core makes a fat, heavy parcel for its weight. Very long ribbon on a small core makes a long, unstable one. Both are handled less well than a compact parcel, and both need a rigid outer box sized to them.

DDP and DDU: Who Actually Pays

The shipping terms on a sample are where surprise costs live. Two terms account for most international sample shipments.

DDU - Delivered Duty Unpaid. The sender pays the carriage. The recipient pays duty, tax and clearance fees, which the courier collects on delivery. The practical effect is that the recipient must be present, must be able to pay, and must agree to pay. If they cannot, the parcel is returned, and the sender usually ends up paying the return carriage plus a handling fee anyway.

DDP - Delivered Duty Paid. The sender arranges and pays for carriage, duty, tax and clearance. The courier delivers without asking the recipient for anything. For samples this is almost always the right term, because the alternative puts a surprise payment in front of a designer or a studio who has no relationship with the freight, no customs knowledge and no way to respond quickly.

For DDP to work correctly, the sender must supply accurate information: a correct declared value, an accurate description, and the correct commodity code and country of origin. When those are wrong, the consequence is not a surprise for the recipient but a surcharge, a delay or a return for the sender.

There is a middle ground - DAP, delivered at place with duty and tax payable by the recipient but carriage paid by the sender - that behaves very much like DDU in practice. And there is a distinction worth noting between express courier DDP and DDP freight forwarder: the courier collects duty at the door and handles it simply, while a forwarder's DDP arrangement is an account-level deal that can be cheaper at volume but requires more administration from the sender and offers less visibility of the clearance process.

The rule for sampling programmes is straightforward: for anything going to an external recipient, ship DDP and put the cost inside your own logistics budget. The one exception worth making is samples going to a buyer who routinely imports - a customer with its own freight account, its own customs broker and its own procedures. In that case, shipping on the buyer's terms can be simpler for them and cheaper for both sides, and it is worth asking how they prefer to receive samples rather than assuming.

Customs, Duty and VAT on Ribbon Samples

Customs treatment of samples is a genuine grey area, because the legal definition of a sample depends on intent, and different jurisdictions draw the line differently. The practical facts are more useful than the theory.

Samples are frequently eligible for duty exemption or deferral, but eligibility is not automatic. Most customs authorities provide for samples that are not intended for commercial use to be entered under a simplified or exempt procedure. The usual requirements are that the goods are clearly marked as samples, are not intended for resale, and are accompanied by a proper commercial or customs document stating their nature. A parcel of ribbon labelled "gift" or with no marking at all is not claiming a sample exemption and will be assessed as ordinary merchandise.

Declaring the true value matters even where duty is exempt. Some exemptions are conditional on a declared value being present, and some are only for low-value consignments. Understating a value to avoid duty creates a different problem: it can make the parcel subject to inspection, it can invalidate the courier's liability cover, and it is a false declaration in a customs document. The correct approach is to declare the actual value and claim the exemption properly.

Low-value consignments are handled differently in every country. Many jurisdictions have simplified regimes below a threshold - typically a few hundred US dollars - that allow duty and VAT to be collected at checkout or on delivery rather than requiring full formal entry. These regimes usually mean the courier collects the tax, and they are considerably more predictable than full customs entry. Most sample shipments fall into this band, which is one reason DDP works so smoothly.

VAT treatment differs from duty treatment. Duty is a tax on the import of the goods; VAT is a consumption tax. For a genuine sample that is not for resale, a zero-rating claim may be available in some jurisdictions, while in others the sample is simply consumed within the business and treated as a recoverable cost. This is jurisdiction-specific, and the practical answer is to work with the courier's DDP team, who handle these regimes routinely, and to describe the shipment accurately as a sample rather than as merchandise.

Two pieces of advice are worth more than the detail above. First, build the sample shipping workflow around DDP with accurate documentation, and let the courier handle the customs mechanics - they do this many thousands of times a day and it is not where your competitive advantage lies. Second, have your own customs or trade compliance adviser confirm the treatment for your main destinations once, and write it down. Sample duty handling is a small cost, but it is a recurring cost, and recurring costs are worth one hour of professional attention.

The Paperwork That Has to Be Right

On domestic shipments, paperwork is trivial. On international shipments, a single incorrect field can cause a clearance delay of days, and the information required is short but the consequences of getting it wrong are disproportionate.

Every international sample shipment needs at minimum:

A commercial invoice showing the description of the goods - "polyester satin ribbon, 38 mm, plain, 20 metres" - with a clear sample value, the country of origin and the incoterm. The description should describe what the goods actually are, because a vague description is one of the most common reasons for a parcel being held for inspection.

Correct origin declaration. If the ribbon is woven in one country, decorated in another and packed in a third, the origin is not the packing location. Getting origin wrong on a low-value parcel is unlikely to be noticed, but it is a false declaration and it will be noticed if a parcel is ever inspected for another reason.

A clear sample marking. Marking the parcel itself as a sample, and stating "not for resale", costs nothing and materially improves the odds of a smooth, quick and unfeeed clearance. It also removes any doubt at the receiving end about what the parcel is and what to do with it.

A packing list that matches the contents. Ribbon samples are frequently multiple items in one parcel - a colour set, a construction set, a finished swatch plus a length. A packing list that names each item lets the recipient check they received everything without opening and searching, and lets a courier verify the contents against the invoice. This matters more than it sounds when a parcel arrives after a customs inspection that has been opened and resealed.

A contact route. The invoice should carry a telephone number and an email address for the sending party. If the parcel is held and the courier cannot reach anyone, it will be returned or stored and billed for storage. Almost all customs delays involving small parcels are communication failures rather than documentation failures.

The right commodity code. For a ribbon sample, the tariff classification is normally in the woven textile heading rather than the decorated article heading, and the binding of trims and accessories is relevant only if the ribbon is presented as a trim. It is worth confirming the classification once with a broker and then applying it consistently, because inconsistent classification across a shipment series is the kind of thing that eventually triggers a query.

How to Write a Courier Brief That Protects the Sample

A good sample shipping brief is short and specific. It should cover five things, and a competent freight forwarder or mill can work from it without asking questions.

What the sample is and how sensitive it is. State the type - colour approval, construction sample, strike-off, market sample - and whether appearance is critical. If it is a colour approval, say so explicitly, because that single word changes how it should be packed and how fast it should travel.

How it must be packed. For a colour approval: on the original reel core, wound without compression, in a rigid outer box with no void fill pressing on the ribbon, protected from light, and with the sample marked as a sample on the outside. For construction samples: rolled on a core, boxed, and protected against bending. Specify that the parcel must not be crushed under other items, and that no adhesive tape may contact the ribbon itself - tape residue on a satin surface is a rejection.

What must not happen to the inner packaging. For an approved strike-off, specify that the inner bag must not be opened and resealed, and that the outer carton must not be used as a shipping carton. This is the requirement that protects an approval from looking like a sample that has been handled, and it is the requirement most often forgotten.

Shipping terms and documentation. DDP for external recipients, the invoice contents, the origin, the marking, the declared value and the contact route - as set out above.

What happens on the other end. Tell the recipient what is inside, what to do with it, and who to contact if it arrives damaged or incomplete. A parcel that arrives and sits unopened in a corridor because nobody knew what it was, is a failed shipment that nobody will report.

Two further details that materially improve outcomes. Insure to the real value. Check the courier's per-parcel liability limit and declare above it where the sample value justifies it. And photograph the sample before it is packed for any approval-stage shipment, in case a later dispute needs evidence of the condition at dispatch. It takes a moment and it is the difference between a claim that succeeds and one that does not.

How Samples Get Damaged or Lost

The recurring failures are few and each has a straightforward countermeasure.

Crushing. The dominant physical failure. A light, voluminous parcel is loaded with heavier freight and survives. Countermeasure: a rigid outer box sized to the sample so it cannot be crushed, a rigid inner tube, and no loose filler pressing on the ribbon surface.

Creasing from folding. Ribbon folded to fit a smaller carton will hold the crease permanently. Countermeasure: ship on a core, rolled, in a box that fits the roll rather than one that requires folding.

Water damage. A sample in a sea port or a depot in bad weather can arrive stained or with a tide line. Countermeasure: a sealed inner bag for every approval sample. This is the cheapest protection in the workflow and it prevents one of the most annoying outcomes, because a water-marked colour sample cannot be approved and the cause is invisible.

Colour shift from light and heat. Countermeasure: opaque inner packaging, no seasonal storage in a hot vehicle, and dispatch timing that avoids a parked van over a weekend in summer. The last one sounds trivial and is genuinely a cause of rejected samples.

Loss or misdelivery at customs. Countermeasure: complete and accurate documentation, a contact route on the invoice, and tracked service.

The returned parcel. The recipient refuses or cannot pay the duty. Countermeasure: DDP. This alone removes a large share of sample logistics failures.

The sample that arrives but was never logged. In a busy development cycle, a parcel arrives and nobody records it. It gets used, it gets folded into a swatch board, and three weeks later the buyer asks the mill for the strike-off to be re-sent because they cannot find it. Countermeasure: a simple register - date, description, courier tracking number, recipient, purpose. A spreadsheet is entirely sufficient and it takes two minutes per shipment.

Building a Sample Request and Shipping Workflow

A workable sampling workflow is not complex. It has six steps, and the value of each one is in making the next one possible.

Request. A structured sample request captures what is needed and why, in one place: the programme, the material and construction, the colour or colourway, the finish, the length, the width, the pattern or artwork reference, the stage of approval it is for, the destination, the required-by date and the recipient's contact details. A free-text email request produces errors, and the errors surface three weeks later as a sample of the wrong thing.

Confirm. The mill confirms what it can produce and when, and raises anything unclear. This is where the ribbon specification gets tightened - a sample request is often the first time a buyer realises they have not specified the width or the finish or the selvedge treatment.

Sample production. Against the request, with the sample marked and logged before packing.

Packing and dispatch. Against the brief, with the marking, the paperwork and the tracking reference captured in the register at the moment of dispatch.

Receipt. The recipient confirms arrival and condition. This is the step that turns sample logistics into a controlled process, and the one most often skipped. A one-line confirmation that the parcel arrived undamaged and matches the request closes the loop and gives both sides a shared record.

Disposition. Approved samples are retained as standards, sealed and labelled with the programme version and the approval date. Rejected samples are retained until the issue is understood. Samples that are no longer needed are disposed of deliberately - which also matters for compliance, because the fabric samples a company retains can carry a small but real inventory of fibre content and chemical compliance information, and uncontrolled accumulation of them in a studio is a documentation liability as well as a clutter problem.

A Sample Shipping Policy That Scales

As the number of sample requests grows, the logistics start to dominate the cost, so it is worth having a written policy rather than handling each request ad hoc. Six rules cover most programmes.

Rule one: every sample is logged before dispatch. Date, programme, description, purpose, destination, carrier, tracking number, sender, recipient. No exceptions, including samples sent to your own office.

Rule two: samples are classified by purpose, and purpose determines the service. Appearance-critical samples travel express and DDP. Specification samples travel on the standard service. Market samples travel with presentation packaging. A classification is recorded in the register so it can be audited afterwards.

Rule three: DDP for every external recipient. Unless the recipient has a stated preference and a capability to handle freight themselves. No collect shipments to anyone outside your own company.

Rule four: a standard packing format. One documented method for colour approvals, one for construction samples, one for strike-offs. This is what makes damage rare rather than lucky, and it is what allows a new colleague to pack correctly without asking.

Rule five: a standard request form. One form, used by everyone, so the mill receives consistent information and a buyer does not have to remember nine attributes every time.

Rule six: a monthly reconciliation. Count the samples shipped, the freight spend, the damage and loss rate, and the number of shipments that resulted in a repeat sample because the first was rejected or damaged. The damage and loss rate is the number that tells you whether the packaging standard is working, and it is the number that justifies any investment in better packaging.

None of this is glamorous, and none of it differentiates the product. That is rather the point. The ribbon differentiates the product. The sample logistics is what determines whether the buyer's experience of your programme is that it is precise and well-run, or that it is a series of small avoidable delays. For a mill competing on programme quality rather than unit price, this layer is quietly one of the most differentiating things available.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Should ribbon samples be shipped DDP or DDU?

Ship DDP for anything going outside your own company. DDU means the courier collects duty, tax and clearance fees from the recipient on delivery, which puts a surprise payment in front of a designer, studio or agent who may not be able to pay it. If they cannot, the parcel is returned and the sender ends up paying the return freight and usually a handling fee as well. The one reasonable exception is a recipient who imports regularly and has a stated preference and the capability to handle freight themselves.

2. How much does it typically cost to ship ribbon samples internationally?

The courier bill for a small parcel is usually modest, often in the region of thirty to eighty US dollars depending on the lane, the weight and the service level, and this is frequently charged to the sender rather than the buyer. The costs that matter more are the indirect ones: a lost colour approval costs a week of development time and a full resampling cycle, a damaged strike-off can mean repeating the sample round entirely, and a refused delivery costs the sample, the freight in both directions and the delay. Once those are included, express shipping on approval-stage samples is usually the cheaper option overall, and for specification samples a slower economy service is entirely reasonable.

3. How should ribbon samples be packed to avoid damage?

Wind the ribbon on its original reel core, without compression, and place it in a rigid inner tube or box. Use a sealed opaque inner bag for any sample whose appearance is being approved - this prevents water marks, light exposure and handling contamination. Use an outer carton sized to the roll so the parcel cannot be crushed under heavier freight, and never fold ribbon to fit a smaller box, because a fold leaves a permanent crease that satin surfaces show immediately. Avoid void fill pressing directly against the ribbon face, and never allow packing tape to contact the ribbon, since adhesive residue on a smooth surface is a rejection reason.

4. Do ribbon samples need customs paperwork and do they attract duty?

Most international sample shipments require a commercial invoice with a description of the goods, a true value, the country of origin and the incoterm, plus a packing list that matches the contents and a contact route for the sender. Many jurisdictions provide a simplified or exempt procedure for genuine samples, and the usual low-value consignment regimes let duty and VAT be collected by the courier rather than through formal entry. Marking the parcel itself as a sample and stating that it is not for resale materially improves clearance. Eligibility for exemption depends on the jurisdiction, so confirm the treatment for your main destinations once with a broker and document it.

5. What is the difference between DDP, DAP and DDU for sample shipments?

DDU means the sender pays carriage and the recipient pays duty, tax and clearance costs collected on delivery. DAP means the sender pays carriage with duty and tax payable by the recipient, so it behaves much like DDU in practice. DDP means the sender arranges and pays for carriage, duty, tax and clearance, and the courier delivers with nothing to pay at the door. For sampling, DDP is the practical choice for external recipients because it removes both the surprise payment and the risk of a refused parcel, provided the sender supplies accurate information about the goods, their value and their origin.

6. How long should a sample shipping and approval cycle take?

A well-run programme runs a few working days for transit, one to two days for receipt and internal review, and then the time the reviewer needs to actually evaluate the sample, which for a colour or construction judgement is realistically one to three days rather than an hour. So a sample round is typically five to eight working days end to end, excluding the time the mill takes to produce the sample. The number worth managing is the second round - the sample that has to be re-made because something was wrong with the first. That number is where sample specification quality shows up, and it should be tracked per programme.

7. Should we keep approved samples as standards for future orders?

Yes. An approved strike-off or colour approval sample is the physical reference against which subsequent production is judged, and without it every future dispute becomes an argument from memory. Retain it in an opaque, sealed bag, labelled with the programme, the approval date and the approval version, and store it protected from light, heat and humidity. Keep the retained standard separate from working samples, and never let it become the sample that gets passed around a studio for a week and comes back creased. A retained standard is an archive piece; working samples are consumables, and confusing the two is how a programme ends up with no reference at all.

8. How can we reduce the number of sample requests without slowing development?

Reduce requests that were never necessary in the first place, rather than rationing the ones that matter. The three usual sources of waste are requests that omit a specification detail and therefore produce a sample of the wrong thing, duplicate requests for colour options that were never properly shortlisted on screen, and requests for physical samples of constructions that were never candidates. A structured request form fixes the first, a documented colour and construction screening step before any sample is made fixes the second, and a pre-brief to the mill on what is actually in contention fixes the third. The result is fewer samples and a faster development loop, because the samples that do get made are the ones that can be approved.
