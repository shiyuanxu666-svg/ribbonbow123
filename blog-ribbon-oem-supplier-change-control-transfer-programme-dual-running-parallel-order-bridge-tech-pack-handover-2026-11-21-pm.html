Most ribbon supplier transfers do not fail because the new factory cannot make the product. They fail because the brand did not transfer the programme itself. A factory can be handed a sample, a drawing and a purchase order and will produce something entirely reasonable. What cannot be reconstructed from those three items is the accumulated knowledge that made the product good: which dye recipe held the shade, which yarn supplier's filament behaved, which finishing route avoided shrinkage, which defect was acceptable to the retailer and which was not. That knowledge lives in people, not documents, and it is the first thing lost in a transition.

This guide covers how to transfer a ribbon private label programme from one supplier to another, or how to bring a second supplier live alongside an incumbent. The subject is narrower than general supplier management, but it is a specific piece of work with a specific failure mode, and the difference between a smooth transfer and a botched one is almost entirely in the preparation.

Why Programmes Move

It is worth being clear about the reasons, because each one implies a different amount of risk and a different approach.

Capacity or lead-time failure. The incumbent cannot hold the dates the brand needs. This is the least risky transfer, because the specification is proven, the artwork file exists and the colour is established. The risk is concentrated in timing.

Commercial renegotiation. The incumbent's pricing has moved beyond what the programme can carry. Here the danger is that the brand is tempted to accept the new supplier's sample on price and quality rather than on equivalence, and quietly changes the product in the process - a different hand-feel, a slightly different width tolerance, a shade that is close but not the same. The programme is now not what was launched, and the difference will surface as a customer complaint rather than as a specification argument.

Compliance or certification failure. A certification lapses, an audit finding is unresolved, a social compliance requirement changes. This has the least schedule tolerance of the three, because the replacement supplier must already hold or be able to obtain the required credentials, and credentialing takes calendar months, not weeks.

Risk diversification. The brand wants a second source for a product it currently single-sources. This is the reason this is now routine in most programmes, and it is the case with the longest runway and the best economics, because there is no deadline forcing a decision.

Whatever the trigger, the mistake is treating the transfer as a sourcing exercise rather than as a project. It is a project with defined phases, a critical path and a handover that can fail.

What Actually Breaks During a Supplier Transfer

Across transfers, the failures cluster into five groups. Recognising them in advance is most of the defence.

The shade drifts. The new factory starts from the same colour reference and produces a shade that is technically within tolerance of the standard, and the brand accepts it because the difference is small. But small differences at the level of the ribbon are large at the level of the product - a gift box ribbon against a printed card, a hair bow against a photographed model. The transfer then becomes a slow drift across several production runs, because each run is accepted against the previous rather than against the original standard.

The specification arrives incomplete. The brand transfers a description written for the incumbent rather than a specification written for production. Details that were never contested with the incumbent - because both sides knew them - are not written down: the acceptable width band, the winding direction, the maximum number of knots per roll, the carton stacking limit, the acceptable edge condition. The new factory does different things on all of them, reasonably, and the brand discovers the differences as complaints.

The approval is re-done badly. The new factory's sample is approved in a hurry, by someone who has not seen the product before, using a screen to compare against a physical standard. Approval under time pressure, without the original reference in hand, produces an approval that will not hold up later.

The order lands at the wrong time. A transfer planned around a production gap, with no overlap, produces a period where the programme has no supplier. This is the most common purely commercial failure, and the cheapest to prevent.

The knowledge is never captured. The incumbent's production manager knows which finishing route works on this construction, which yarn lot was problematic, what the retailer's tolerance really is in practice. None of that is in the tech pack. If the transfer is done because the incumbent relationship is ending, that person is not going to be available for consultation a year later.

The Tech Pack Handover

The tech pack is the document that carries the programme across the supplier boundary. It is more than a specification: it is the accumulated definition of the product plus the history of how it is made. A complete handover pack for a ribbon programme contains the following.

  1. The full specification - material and fibre content, construction, width with tolerance, finish, edge treatment, decoration method, colour reference and tolerance with the measurement method, performance requirements, and the packing format.
  2. The production file - the artwork at the correct repeat, the print specification, separations where applicable, and a written statement of who owns the artwork and under what terms it may be used.
  3. The colour master standard - a physical reference cut from production material, labelled with its reference number, plus the specification of the measurement method and tolerance. This travels with the programme. It does not stay behind.
  4. The bill of materials - the yarn and fibre suppliers, the dye house, the decoration method in detail, the trim and accessory sources, and the consumables. This is what lets a new factory tell you honestly whether it can replicate the product or only produce something similar.
  5. The defect classification and acceptance criteria - what counts as a critical, major or minor defect in this product, and the sampling plan and inspection standard applied at the incumbent.
  6. The history - the changes made over the programme's life and why, the defects that occurred and their root causes, and the concessions that were granted. This section is what separates a handover from a specification, and it is the section most often omitted.
  7. The commercial and operational parameters - lead time in working days from confirmed order to ready-to-ship, the packing configuration, the label content, the document set required on arrival, and the contact and escalation structure.

Two practical notes on the handover itself.

The pack should be assembled and signed off before the new factory is asked to quote, so that the quotes are comparable. A quote against a loose description and a quote against a complete pack differ by more than the price difference - they differ in whether they are for the same product. If the incumbent is cooperative, ask for the history section explicitly; most factories will supply it and most brands never ask.

Hold a live session to walk through the pack with the new factory's production and quality staff, not only with the sales contact. The purpose is to surface the items that are ambiguous in writing. A specification that reads cleanly can still contain an assumption that only the person who wrote it could see - and the new factory's production manager is exactly the person who will find it, if you ask them directly.

Bridging Colour Between Two Mills

Colour is the transfer's hardest control point and deserves a method of its own rather than being folded into the general sample process.

The method that works is a three-way comparison rather than a two-way one. Take the approved master standard, the incumbent's current production output and the new factory's first production. Measure all three under the same conditions and compare them against the standard. The point is to separate two different questions that are usually collapsed into one.

Can the new factory hit the standard? If the new factory's output is measurably outside the agreed tolerance of the master standard, the answer is no, and the response is to work on the recipe before anything else. This is a technical problem with a technical solution - a different dye house, a different yarn source, a different finishing route, or a genuinely different shade standard that the brand adopts.

Does the incumbent's current output still sit inside that tolerance? This question surprises people. A programme that has been running for two years has almost certainly drifted. If the incumbent's current output has moved 2.5 Delta E from the original standard while the new factory's output is 1.2 away, then the new factory is closer to the original approved colour than the incumbent is. Bridging to the incumbent's current output in that situation is the wrong move - it institutionalises a drift that the brand has never actually approved.

Where the two suppliers genuinely cannot be brought within a tight tolerance of each other, the honest answer is that the programme now has two shades. That is a legitimate commercial outcome - it happens when the base material differs and the dyes are not fully interchangeable - but it must be a decision, taken explicitly, with the consequences priced in. A programme with two shades needs either a separation between the two lots in the packaging and labelling, or a written acceptance that the two are the same product. Leaving it undocumented produces a complaint you cannot answer.

Running a Parallel Order

The parallel order is the mechanism that makes a transfer safe, and its absence is the single most common cause of a botched supplier change. The principle is simple: for at least one full production cycle, the incumbent and the incoming supplier both produce the same specification, from the same artwork and the same colour standard, and both sets of goods are evaluated against the same criteria.

What the parallel run actually establishes is worth being precise about, because "the new supplier's output looks fine" is not what is being tested.

It tests the specification. The new factory produces from the written specification alone. Where its output diverges from the incumbent's, the divergence identifies either a gap in the specification or a legitimate material difference. Both are important to know before the incumbent contract ends, and the second is only visible because the two outputs exist side by side.

It tests the tolerance. A tolerance agreed in the abstract is not the same as a tolerance the incoming factory can hold. The parallel run is where the real achievable band becomes visible.

It tests the delivery. The incoming supplier's actual lead time on a real order, with real packing and real documents, is known after one cycle and only estimated before it.

It protects the brand's customers. This is the commercial reason the parallel run exists. A single-customer product with two visually identical lots in one season is a quality complaint waiting to happen. If the brand cannot physically prevent mixed supply, the parallel run should be planned so that the incoming supplier's first lots are used in a lower-visibility application or a later delivery window - which usually conflicts with the urgency of the change and therefore needs to be negotiated deliberately rather than wished away.

Three practical points on structuring it.

Size the parallel order to the evaluation, not to the economics. A run large enough to be statistically meaningful on defect rate and lead time, small enough that it can be scrapped if the outcome is bad. For a retail trim, that is often a fraction of the first production order.

Evaluate against the standard, not against the incumbent's output alone. The evaluation sheet should record measurements against the master standard for both suppliers. Where a human judgement is required, use more than one judge and present the samples in randomised order without identifying which supplier made which, because the knowledge of which is which changes the perception.

Run the full document and packing chain. A parallel run that skips the retail-ready packing, the label verification and the document set is not a parallel run. The failures that matter in a transfer are the ones that appear at the receiving site, and the only way to find them is to ship the full configuration.

Change Control After the Transfer

The transfer is not the end of the project. The period after is where a transferred programme either stabilises or quietly diverges, and the difference is usually a change-control regime that was set up for the first order and never updated for the new supplier.

Four elements of change control need to be re-established rather than inherited.

The approval authority. Who can approve a change to the specification, and who does not need to be consulted? On a transferred programme there are two plausible approvers - the brand's original approver and the incoming supplier's account manager - and ambiguity here is what allows the specification to drift one small change at a time until nobody can say what the product is.

The re-approval triggers. These should be restated in the new supply agreement, and they are the defence against silent substitution: a change of base material, a change of dye house, a change of decoration method, a change of yarn source, a change of sub-supplier for a trim component. Each of these is a legitimate business decision for the supplier to make on cost or availability grounds, and each of them changes what the brand is selling. If they are not trigger events, the brand learns about them when a customer does.

The change request record. A simple log - date, what changed, why, who approved, effect on cost and lead time, effect on specification and colour - maintained by the brand and shared monthly with the factory. The value is cumulative: a programme with a two-year change log can be handed to another supplier, or taken to market for a licence, with a defensible account of its specification history.

The review of the drift. Shade, width and defect rates should be re-measured at defined intervals rather than only when a complaint arrives. Quarterly review of the colour against the master standard catches a drift of 0.5 Delta E per quarter at a point where it is still correctable; the same drift discovered at a retail audit is not correctable at all.

Exit Terms and Continuity Planning

When the transfer is driven by the end of the incumbent relationship, the exit terms and the continuity plan are negotiated at the same time and rarely by the same person. The commercial negotiator handles the contract; the continuity plan is a manufacturing and inventory question. Both are needed, and a contract that resolves the commercial dispute while leaving the operational continuity unresolved produces a supplier who is willing but unable to help.

Three things to settle before the incumbent relationship ends.

How long the production capability is held. How long will the incumbent keep the dye recipe, the production file and the physical standard available, and at what price will it be re-issued if the brand returns? Without a defined holding period, the recipe is retired at some arbitrary point after the last order - often at the end of a peak season - and re-development then costs both a fee and a full approval cycle.

What happens to the physical standards and samples. Retained standard, retained samples from each production batch and the records that connect them must be transferred and remain traceable. This is a physical transfer of custody, and it is forgotten more often than it is negotiated.

How the final order is phased. The last order from the incumbent should be sized so that it carries the brand through the point at which the incoming supplier's first production is proven and in the distribution channel - not merely to the point at which the first order was placed. Getting this wrong produces a stock-out in the transition window, which is the most visible failure a supply change can produce and the least forgivable by the customer who sees it.

None of this makes the transfer complicated. It makes it a project with a defined sequence: capture the programme in a handover pack, bridge the colour against the original standard, run a parallel order, establish change control under the new supplier, and secure the exit terms and the continuity position before the incumbent goes quiet. Programmes that follow that sequence transfer cleanly. Programmes that treat it as a purchase order with a new supplier's name on it usually discover the missing months later, at retail, with no reference to compare against.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How long does a ribbon supplier transfer normally take?

For a colour and construction already proven with an incumbent, three to four months from kick-off to a fully live second source is realistic: a month for the handover pack and initial samples, a month for the parallel order to run and be evaluated, a month for the incoming supplier's first production to be approved and shipped, and a month of overlap before the incumbent is released. Where the programme involves a new certification, a new construction or a compliance-driven deadline, add the credentialing calendar - typically three to six months - and expect the compliance track, not the manufacturing track, to set the schedule.

2. Is a parallel order really necessary, or is it an unnecessary cost?

It is necessary whenever the brand's customers can physically see the difference between the two suppliers' output, which is most of the time for ribbon trim. Its cost is one part-size order and a few weeks of schedule, and it buys the two things a transfer without it does not: evidence about whether the incoming supplier can actually hold the specification, and a fallback that remains live until the new source is proven in the distribution channel. The programmes that treat the parallel order as optional are the ones that end up with mixed lots in the same season and no defensible answer for the customer who complains.

3. How do we handle colour when the new supplier cannot match the approved shade exactly?

Measure the incoming output against the original master standard rather than against the incumbent's current output, because the incumbent has usually drifted from the original standard over the life of the programme. If the new supplier is closer to the original approved colour, bridge to the original standard. If a genuine material difference makes that impossible, treat it as a decision rather than a problem: confirm whether the programme can carry two shades, price the consequences, and either separate them in the packaging and labelling or record a written acceptance that they are the same product.

4. What information should we demand from the outgoing supplier during a transfer?

The handover pack described in this guide, and within it the history section in particular: the changes made during the programme and the reasons for them, the defects that occurred with their root causes, and the concessions that were granted and why. Most factories will supply this and most brands never ask for it. This history is what stops the incoming supplier from rediscovering a problem the programme already solved two years ago, and it is the hardest part to reconstruct later if it is not captured during the transition.

5. Should we give the incoming supplier our existing purchase order history and pricing?

No. Share the technical specification, the standards, the artwork under the agreed ownership terms and the acceptance criteria, but keep the commercial information with the incumbent. Price history is commercially sensitive to the incumbent and gives the incoming supplier a benchmark it will quote against without any obligation to match it. Share volume information in a form that is useful for capacity planning - such as annual metres per specification - rather than as a full order ledger.

6. How do we prevent the specification from drifting after the transfer?

Four controls, applied consistently: name a single approval authority for changes; restate in the new supply agreement the changes that require re-approval, including material, dye house, yarn source, decoration method and sub-supplier changes; keep a written change log with the date, reason, approver and cost and lead-time effect; and review the colour against the master standard at defined intervals rather than only when a complaint arrives. The drift is almost never a single large change - it is a sequence of small approvals, each individually reasonable, that is only visible in the log.

7. If the incoming supplier fails mid-season, what continuity plan should exist?

Keep the outgoing supplier's production capability on hold - the dye recipe, the production file and the physical standard - for a defined period, so the brand can place a bridge order rather than negotiate from zero. Agree that holding period and the terms for using it in the supply agreement before the relationship ends, because it cannot be arranged at the point of failure. In parallel, maintain enough stock of the ribbon in the distribution channel to cover the real bridging time, which is longer than the production lead time once approvals and freight are added.
