The Gap Between Product Approval and Retail Approval

A private label ribbon programme has two separate approval processes, and brands routinely confuse them. The first is product approval: the factory and the brand agree a specification, a sample is approved, and everyone agrees what the ribbon is. The second is retail approval: the retailer decides whether this supplier, this product and this packaging can enter its distribution network and onto its shelves. The first is a conversation between two parties. The second is a documented, multi-department process with fixed forms, fixed systems and fixed lead times, and it is usually run by people who will never see your ribbon or speak to your factory.

The distinction matters because the factory controls the first and barely participates in the second. The ribbon factory is expert in producing to a specification, sampling it and inspecting it. Retailer onboarding requires expertise in a different set of areas: legal entity documentation, GS1 barcode registration, EDI transaction formats, packaging dielines and label content, advance shipping notice discipline, and a compliance evidence file organised in a way that a compliance auditor can navigate. These are not skills a ribbon mill has, and a mill that has never done them before will under-price the work, because the work does not appear on the mill's own production schedule.

This guide is written for the brand owner or procurement manager who has decided to sell private label ribbon into retail, and for the factory team supporting them. It covers what is actually required, in what order, how shelf-ready packaging differs from the shipping carton, how the data contract with the retailer works, and what changes about unit economics when you ship directly into a retailer's own distribution centre instead of shipping to a single consolidated destination.

What a Retailer Asks For Before You Ship a Single Carton

Every retailer has its own supplier agreement, but the substance is remarkably consistent, and the reason is that the systems are shared. Large retailers use the same kinds of onboarding portals, the same electronic data interchange networks and the same compliance vendor frameworks, so a supplier that has onboarded with one large retailer usually finds the next one recognisable. This is helpful, because it means a supplier that treats onboarding as a capability rather than a one-off project can reuse the work.

Before anything ships, expect to be asked for the following, and expect the list to arrive in stages rather than all at once.

  • Legal and financial identity — articles of incorporation or the equivalent, a certificate of tax registration, a completed tax form for the relevant jurisdiction, bank account verification, a proof of address, and a signed supplier agreement with the retailer's own terms rather than yours.
  • Insurance and indemnity — general liability, product liability and, depending on the category, cargo insurance, each naming the retailer as an additional insured.
  • Social compliance — a current social audit report. For most large retailers this is a BSCI, SEDEX or SMETA audit, and a report that has expired is treated as no report at all.
  • Product and chemical compliance — material declarations, and depending on where the product lands and who the end consumer is, chemical compliance statements, restrictions documentation, and sometimes a third-party test report for the specific construction being supplied.
  • Product specification and test data — the construction, material, dimensions, tolerances, colourfastness, shrinkage and abrasion data for the SKU in question.
  • Quality process evidence — a written incoming inspection procedure, a sampling plan, a non-conformance handling process and a commitment to corrective action with response times.
  • Technical and data setup — supplier portal registration, GS1 company prefix and barcode registration, EDI connection with agreed transaction types, a routing guide, and an agreed carton and label specification.
  • Packaging approval — artwork files against the retailer's dieline, a physical pre-production sample, and sign-off that the label content is in the right place with the right wording.
  • Ship-from and routing information — the origin address, the carrier, the container and carton plan, and the advance shipping notice protocol.

Two points about this list are worth stating plainly. First, several items have external lead times you do not control. A social audit that has lapsed cannot be produced quickly. A barcode registration is a database entry that some retailers process in batches. An EDI test cycle requires the retailer's systems team to schedule a window with you, and that window is often the longest single item on the critical path. Second, the list is not a one-time cost. Most of these documents expire or need refreshing, and the retailers that are most demanding about submission are also the ones that will audit you on whether the evidence was current at the time of shipment.

The Four Document Groups That Gate Onboarding

Organising the requirement set into four groups makes it manageable, because each group has a different owner and a different failure mode.

Identity and financial

This group is mechanical and, once complete, almost never changes. The failure mode here is a document expiring or a bank detail being amended without notification, which is a surprisingly common cause of a payment hold. Two practical habits pay for themselves: keep a single master record of legal entity details and notify every trading partner in writing whenever any field changes, and confirm with the retailer which entity they expect to be the contracting party, because in a private label arrangement that is sometimes the brand owner and sometimes the trading entity.

Social and environmental compliance

This group has the longest external lead time and the strictest expiry logic. A current report means one that is within its validity window for the specific audit type and the specific facility. Treat the expiry date as a calendar item with a renewal trigger at sixty days, not a quarter end. If the ribbon is being produced at more than one site, be clear in the paperwork about which site is supplying which retailer, because a retailer will not accept a report for a facility that is not making the goods.

Product quality and chemical

For ribbon, this group is mostly about demonstrating that the specific product you are about to supply has been tested for the things your customer cares about. In practice the recurring questions are colourfastness to washing and to light, shrinkage after washing or cleaning, crocking, and whether the product is safe under the chemical frameworks that apply in the destination market. The mistake to avoid is having a test report for a different construction. A report on a woven polyester ribbon does not cover a velvet ribbon with a different backing, and a report on a plain ribbon does not cover a printed one. If you are supplying several colourways of the same construction, one report for the construction with the colour data attached to each colourway is usually sufficient, and it is worth asking the retailer what they will accept.

Operational and technical

This is the group that surprises people, because it is the group where the retailer's systems meet your factory's systems. It includes the EDI transaction set, the ASN process, the barcode and label specification, the routing guide and the packing format. It is also the group where the cost of getting it wrong lands hardest, because these are also the systems that generate chargebacks when the data is wrong. We will come back to this in the section on EDI below.

Shelf-Ready Secondary Packaging for Ribbon and Trim

Ribbon and decorative trim occupy an awkward position in packaging. The product is a consumable, often bought on impulse, frequently used as a finishing element rather than as the primary reason for a purchase, and in the case of a spool or a bow, physically small but with an unusual shape that does not stack well. That shape drives most of the packaging design decisions.

The industry-standard structure for a spool-based ribbon is a polybag or header card carrying the spool, sometimes with a second and third spool in a multi-pack, sealed with a sticker or a heat seal, and then a printed master carton containing a defined number of those consumer units. For bows and for pre-made decorative sets, the pack is often a backing card with a blister or a paper band. For cut lengths sold in bundles, the pack is typically a band or sleeve around the bundle, sometimes with a printed insert carrying the brand and the product story.

What matters to the retailer is not the aesthetics of the pack but a small set of functional and legal requirements.

  • Barcode in the exact format, symbology and size the retailer specifies, with a contrast and a quiet zone that survive their scanning environment. Many retail environments use older fixed-mount scanners, so a barcode that tests fine on a handheld in a bright office can fail on a poorly lit dock.
  • Country of origin statement, in the position and wording the retailer requires. For a Chinese-origin product entering the US this is a marking requirement on the product or its container, and retail policy frequently requires it on the consumer pack as well.
  • Net quantity or count — for a multi-pack of spools, the number of spools; for a length sold by the metre, the actual length.
  • Material or fibre content where a fibre certification mark is used, and the licence number if the retailer wants it displayed.
  • Care and use instructions where the product will be washed with the garment or the gift, and warnings where the product carries a foreseeable risk such as small parts or a sharp wire edge.
  • The retailer's own specification elements — many large retailers have proprietary pack requirements covering dieline, substrate, approved inks and adhesives, recyclability expectations, and mandatory artwork elements such as a recycling instruction, a country-of-marking note or a specific graphic.

The practical sequence that avoids rework is to start the retail packaging design only after three product decisions are fixed: the spool or reel geometry, the finished consumer pack count, and the master carton configuration. All three change the dieline. If the ribbon is later changed to a wider width or a different core, the carton changes, the pack count changes, the barcode position changes and the artwork is redone. This is one of the most common sources of avoidable cost in private label packaging, and it is entirely self-inflicted by sequencing.

Carton Design, Barcode and the Label Content That Must Be Right

Retailers receive goods by the pallet and open them by the carton, so the master carton is an interface with a machine rather than with a person. Four parameters drive most receiving errors.

Carton dimensions. These must match the routing guide exactly, within tolerance, because the retailer calculates how many cartons fit on a standard pallet and plans labour from that number. A carton that is fifteen millimetres over a dimension can push the case count down by several units per pallet, and the difference across a full container is significant. Get the outer carton dimensions from the retailer and have them verified against the actual packed carton with a tape measure, not from a drawing.

Maximum gross weight. This is a handling limit, and exceeding it produces manual handling at the dock and a chargeback. Weight also varies with the construction of the product, so a carton that is compliant with one ribbon weight can breach the limit with a heavier velvet or a wired product in the same carton. If your range spans constructions, the case pack should be calculated for the heaviest, not the average.

Case pack and pallets. The number of consumer units per carton and the number of cartons per pallet must be consistent across the programme and must match what the purchase order and the ASN will say. When the case pack changes after artwork approval, the artwork must be revised, because the case pack usually appears on the carton panel.

Label placement and legibility. The label must be in the position the routing guide specifies, on a substrate the retailer's scanners read, at a size that the label printer produces consistently, and it must not overlap a seam where it will wrinkle. Test the barcode on a physically packed carton with the actual scanner if you can, and at minimum with a scanner from a comparable environment. Barcode failure is detected at receiving, which is the worst place to detect it.

Alongside the functional marks, the label typically carries the retailer's own vendor and item identifiers. The point that causes the most trouble is a mismatch between the item code printed on the carton, the item code on the packing slip, the item code in the purchase order and the item code in the advance shipping notice. Four copies of a six-digit code with one digit transposed is a routine cause of a receiving rejection, and it is avoidable with a single controlled source for the code.

EDI, ASN and the Data Contract With the Retailer

For a supplier that has only ever shipped export wholesale, EDI feels like an unnecessary imposition. In practice it is the mechanism by which a retailer's system knows what is arriving before it arrives, and the modern retail network simply cannot operate without it. A supplier that cannot transmit an advance shipping notice electronically is, at best, manually keyed, and manual keying is where both cost and error live.

The transaction types that matter for a ribbon supplier are a small subset of the full retail EDI set.

  • Purchase order — received from the retailer, carrying the item, the quantity, the price, the ship-to location and the required delivery date. This is the document that should drive your production plan and your packing list.
  • Advance shipping notice — sent by you before the goods leave, carrying the carton count, the pallet count, the carrier, the tracking reference, the ship-from location and the bill of lading number. The retailer's dock labour and receiving appointments are planned from this, so the notice carries real weight.
  • Invoice — sent by you, matching the purchase order and the goods received, and, where the retailer works on deduction, matching the deduction report.
  • Receiving advice — received from the retailer once goods are checked in, which is the document that drives the payment clock.
  • Inventory or status reporting — required by some programmes for consignment or vendor-managed stock arrangements.

Three practical points about implementation. First, agree the transaction types and the version of the specification in writing before development starts; the differences between implementations are mostly in field naming and code sets, and discovering them late means re-testing. Second, insist on a real end-to-end test rather than a file exchange test. The value of an EDI test is in the responses: the retailer rejecting a document with a specific error code, and the supplier learning to read that code. A transaction that is accepted on the first attempt has not been tested. Third, agree the error-handling process explicitly, because in most implementations the harder part is not sending the document but knowing what to do when the response is a rejection, and who is watching for it.

For a brand that cannot operate an EDI connection directly, the usual arrangement is a third-party EDI service provider or a 3PL that translates between the retailer's network and the brand's order system. This is a legitimate and common solution. It does add a layer, so agree who monitors for rejections, who owns the mapping when the retailer's specification changes, and who is accountable for an invoice that goes unpaid because a rejection was never seen.

Direct-to-DC Shipping and What It Does to Your Economics

Shipping into a retailer's own distribution centre, rather than to a consolidator or a retailer's single receiving warehouse, is a significant operational change. It is also where many brands get their landed-cost model wrong, because they continue to think in per-container economics when the retail network thinks in per-carton economics.

What changes in your favour. Freight is usually charged at a contracted retail or parcel rate rather than a full container rate, and for a mixed retail order with several destinations the per-carton rate is typically lower than the equivalent cost shipped as a wholesale container. Transit is shorter, which reduces the cash sitting in goods on the water and improves the availability figure the retailer sees. Inbound appointments are scheduled, so the goods are off the truck quickly rather than sitting in a yard.

What changes against you. Retail receiving is unforgiving. The advance shipping notice must be accurate and timely or the delivery is refused, and refused deliveries cost a lot. Cartons must match the routing guide on dimensions, weight, pallet pattern and label position. Mixed retail orders often need splitting across several DC destinations on different delivery dates, and each split is a separate carton handling event that does not exist in a single-destination export. Chargebacks apply at the carton and pallet level rather than the order level, so a small error on a large volume is proportionally more expensive.

The right model for this is a per-carton landed cost that includes the retail-specific elements: the freight at the contract rate, the split handling cost per destination, the expected chargeback rate derived from your own history, and the working capital cost of the shorter but more frequent cycle. Comparing that against a wholesale container model on a per-metre basis, as most brands do, will produce the wrong answer in one direction or the other depending on the retailer's chargeback rate.

One more practical point. A direct-to-DC model works best when the order structure is predictable enough to plan cartons and pallets, and the ribbon itself is one of several components in a mixed order that a brand is already managing this way. For a brand making a first large retail commitment with an unfamiliar retailer, it is worth modelling the first year conservatively, including a higher assumed chargeback rate, and then refining the model against actuals.

Chargebacks: Where the Money Actually Leaks

Chargebacks are the most predictable cost in the entire retail onboarding process, and almost all of them are caused by a short list of things, and almost none of them relate to the ribbon itself.

In our experience of supporting private label programmes into large retail networks, the recurring causes are these:

  • Late or inaccurate advance shipping notice. The notice arrives inside the receiving window, or the carton count does not match what physically arrives.
  • Packing slip does not match the purchase order line for line, including the item code. Retailers reconcile at the line level and reject the whole delivery on a mismatch.
  • Carton dimensions or gross weight outside tolerance, which cascades into an unexpected pallet count and manual handling.
  • Barcode that does not scan at the dock, because of print contrast, label material, size or placement, or because the code was generated for a different environment than the one it is being read in.
  • Case pack or pallet count inconsistent with the approved artwork and the routing guide.
  • Ship-from location on the label or the notice not matching the registered supplier location.
  • Missing country of origin marking in the position the retailer requires, on the consumer pack rather than only on the master carton.
  • Carrier not on the approved routing list, or a carrier whose appointments the retailer does not take.

The defence against all of these is a short, repeatable pre-shipment check. We run one before every retail-bound container, and it takes about twenty minutes.

  1. Print the purchase order and the final packing list side by side and verify item code, quantity, case pack and ship-to against each other.
  2. Verify the carton label against the routing guide for dimensions, gross weight, label position and the ship-from address.
  3. Scan every barcode with a handheld scanner on the actual packed carton, not on a proof, and check that it resolves to the right item.
  4. Measure and weigh a random sample of packed cartons, and confirm the pallet pattern and count against the routing guide.
  5. Check the country of origin marking, net quantity and any required certification mark against the approved artwork.
  6. Confirm the advance shipping notice is generated from the same data as the packing list, and send it within the retailer's required window.

Two of these checks catch the majority of real problems, and they are the two most often skipped: scanning the barcode on the packed carton, and reconciling the packing list against the purchase order line for line. Both take minutes and both prevent a chargeback that costs several times more than the check.

How to Structure the Work Across Your Factory and Your Brand Team

Retail onboarding blurs the boundary between the brand and the manufacturer, and it is worth being deliberate about who owns what, because the work is different in kind and the failure mode of getting it wrong is a delay that neither party can see coming.

The brand side typically owns the commercial relationship, the legal and tax entity, the retailer agreement, the GS1 prefix, the retailer item codes, the barcode assignment, the EDI relationship, the compliance evidence file and the artwork approvals. The factory side typically owns the product specification, the physical production, the pack-out, the carton manufacture and packing, the label printing, the packing list, the advance shipping notice content, the container loading and the export documentation.

The overlap area is where projects stall, and it is always the same three things: the item code, the barcode artwork and the case pack. Each of these appears in documents owned by the brand and in physical materials owned by the factory, and each must have exactly one source of truth. The practical rule is that the retailer-facing identifiers live in one master record owned by the brand, and every physical and digital artefact is generated from that record rather than retyped.

A second practical point about the factory's role. When a ribbon mill packs retail cartons directly, the carton artwork becomes part of the product specification and belongs in the same change-control process, with the same version discipline. A carton change made without approval, a case pack that changed without the artwork being revised, a barcode regenerated with a new GS1 application identifier, these are all change-control failures, and they produce chargebacks in the same way that an unapproved change to the ribbon construction produces quality disputes. If your supply agreement already has a specification freeze and a change notification process for the ribbon, extend it to the packaging rather than creating a second, looser process.

A Twelve-Week Onboarding Sequence That Works

The following sequence has repeatedly produced a first approved shipment within roughly twelve weeks, and the important feature of it is that the long-lead items start on day one rather than after the product is ready.

Weeks one to two, parallel start. Submit the identity, tax, banking and insurance documents. Confirm the supplier agreement with the retailer's legal team. Establish whether the company GS1 prefix exists and request the barcode range. Book the social audit or confirm the current report is valid for the supplying site. These items have the longest external lead times and none of them depend on the ribbon.

Weeks two to four, specification freeze. Finalise the ribbon specification, the reel geometry, the pack count and the master carton configuration with the factory, and freeze them. Send the product specification, test reports and quality process documentation to the retailer for product approval. This is the point at which the packaging work becomes safe to start, because the dimensions are no longer going to move.

Weeks three to seven, parallel packaging and systems. Develop the secondary pack and the master carton against the retailer's dieline and label specification, produce a physical pre-production sample and submit it for approval. In parallel, build the EDI connection, agree the transaction set and code lists in writing, and run a full test cycle with at least one rejection deliberately processed so the error-handling process is proven.

Weeks six to nine, first production and pre-shipment review. Produce the first retail pack, run the pre-shipment checklist against real packed cartons, and complete a self-audit against the onboarding checklist with the retailer before the shipment is booked.

Weeks ten to twelve, first shipment and post-shipment review. Ship, monitor the advance shipping notice and the first receiving advice closely, and record every rejection with its error code. The first shipment produces more onboarding learning than the previous eleven weeks of preparation, and the value of that learning depends entirely on whether somebody is capturing it.

The sequence has one organising principle: nothing that has an external lead time starts after something that depends on it. Ribbon specification before packaging. Barcode range before artwork. EDI test before the first shipment. It sounds obvious written down, and yet the reverse order is what happens most often, because the instinct is to wait until the product looks finished.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement managers most often ask us about getting private label ribbon approved by a retailer: what documents are required, how shelf-ready packaging differs from a shipping carton, how direct-to-DC shipping changes the economics, how to avoid chargebacks, and how long the whole process takes.

1. What does a retailer require before a private label ribbon supplier is approved?

The list is longer than most first-time brand owners expect, and it is usually divided into four groups. Identity and financial: legal entity documents, tax registration, W-8 or equivalent, bank verification, insurance certificates and a signed supplier agreement. Compliance and social: social audit results such as BSCI, SEDEX or SMETA, and depending on the category, chemical and product safety documentation such as REACH or CPSIA statements and Prop 65 assessment. Product and quality: a current product specification, test reports for the specific colour and construction being supplied, colour fastness and shrinkage data, and an incoming inspection and corrective action commitment. Operational and technical: a supplier setup pack in the retailer's own portal, an EDI connection with agreed transaction types, an ASN and invoice format, GS1 barcode registration and a routing guide, plus packaging and labelling requirements including country of origin marking. The practical point for buyers is that several of these have long lead times. A social audit report that has lapsed cannot be produced in a week, and a barcode registration that has not been created will stop a shipment even if the ribbon itself is perfect.

2. What is the difference between a shipping carton and a shelf-ready secondary pack for ribbon?

A shipping carton is the master case that protects goods in transit and is typically opened at the distribution centre. A shelf-ready secondary pack is the consumer-facing pack that goes straight onto the shelf from the case, so it has to survive being handled once at the store, present the product clearly, and carry the label content the retailer requires. For ribbon and decorative trim, the common pattern is a polybag or header card carrying one or more spools, bows or tassels, inside a printed master carton that contains a fixed number of that pack. The distinction matters commercially because the secondary pack is where the retailer's requirements concentrate: barcode format and placement, country of origin statement, fibre certification marks, net quantity, care instructions, warnings where relevant, and the retailer's own packaging specifications including dieline templates, approved substrate and restricted ink and adhesive chemistry. A brand that develops the retail packaging before the product specification is signed off usually has to rework it, because the pack count, the reel diameter and the finished dimensions all depend on decisions that were made later.

3. How does drop shipping direct to a retailer DC change ribbon unit economics?

It changes the cost structure more than it changes the price. Shipping to a retailer's own distribution centre removes the retailer as a physical intermediary, so freight is usually charged at a retailer's rates rather than a parcel rate, which is typically cheaper per carton. It also shortens the transit, which reduces the working capital sitting in goods on the water and improves in-stock availability at the DC. The costs that move the other way are the packaging ones. Retail DC receiving is strict about carton count, pallet configuration, label legibility and advance shipping notice accuracy, so a supplier that misses the ASN or ships a non-compliant pallet pays the cost twice through chargebacks. Mixed retail orders also mean a single factory shipment may need to be split across several DC destinations with different dates, and each split carries a handling cost that does not exist in a single-destination export. The right way to model this is on a per-carton landed basis including the retailer's receiving rules, not on a per-metre or per-piece basis.

4. How do you avoid chargebacks and compliance penalties when onboarding with a retailer?

Chargebacks in this category are overwhelmingly caused by paperwork and data rather than by ribbon quality. The usual triggers are a late or inaccurate advance shipping notice, a packing slip that does not match the purchase order line for line, a carton that exceeds the maximum gross weight or has incorrect dimensions on the label, a barcode that does not scan because it was printed at the wrong size or contrast, and a pallet that does not match the routing guide. The practical control is a pre-shipment checklist that verifies the data on the label against the routing guide and against the ASN, a barcode scan test on the actual carton rather than on a proof, a weight check on a random sample of packed cartons, and a reconciliation step between the packing list and the purchase order before the truck is sealed. For compliance specifically, keep a single evidence file per SKU with the current certificate, the most recent test report and the date of the next expiry, so that an audit request can be answered in hours rather than weeks.

5. Should the ribbon factory handle retailer packaging, or should the brand use a separate packaging supplier?

Both are normal and the right answer depends on volume and complexity. A factory that already packs ribbon into consumer units can pack to a retailer's dieline, print the required marks on the carton and ship a single consolidated case, which removes a handling step and a second international freight leg. This is usually the better option for a brand with modest volume and a straightforward pack, because the alternative of a separate packaging supplier plus a separate freight forwarder adds two handling points and two places where a date can slip. A separate packaging supplier becomes the better option when the pack is complex, when the retailer requires a specific print process or substrate the ribbon factory does not run, or when the pack is shared across several product lines and needs to be produced in its own larger volume. The practical caution with the factory option is that carton artwork, barcode and dieline changes then need to go through the same change-control process as the ribbon specification, because a packaging change made without approval is exactly how a chargeback happens.

6. How long does retailer vendor onboarding take for a private label ribbon programme?

For a new supplier with no existing relationship, a realistic planning assumption is eight to sixteen weeks from first submission to first approved shipment, and much of that time sits outside the ribbon factory's control. Identity, tax and banking verification is usually one to three weeks if documents are complete. Supplier agreement and portal registration is typically two to four weeks including legal review. EDI connection and transaction testing, including at least one full transaction cycle of purchase order, invoice and advance shipping notice, commonly takes three to six weeks because it requires coordination with the retailer's systems team. Barcode and GS1 setup depends on whether a company prefix already exists. Compliance documentation can dominate the schedule if a social audit is not current. Packaging artwork and dieline approval is typically two to five weeks and includes a physical sample review at the retailer. The practical recommendation is to start the compliance and identity work in parallel with product development, not after it, and to treat the EDI test cycle as a fixed calendar item rather than something that happens once the ribbon is ready.

7. What mistakes do brands make when they first ship private label ribbon into a retail DC?

The most common first-shipment failures are data errors rather than product errors. Using the factory's own item code instead of the retailer's item code on the carton label and the advance shipping notice. Booking a carrier that is not on the retailer's approved routing list. Getting the master carton dimensions or maximum gross weight wrong, which then cascades into pallet configuration and detention charges. Missing the ship-from location on the label. Printing a barcode that scans poorly because of insufficient contrast or a label material the retailer's scanners cannot read. Shipping an incorrect number of cartons per pallet because the case pack changed after artwork approval. Failing to include the country of origin statement in the position the retailer requires. Not sending the ASN far enough in advance for the retailer to plan dock labour. And assuming the purchase order and the packing slip are identical when the retailer expects specific field names and formats. Each of these is cheap to prevent with a pre-shipment checklist and expensive to discover at the port, because a compliance hold on a container is measured in weeks rather than hours.
