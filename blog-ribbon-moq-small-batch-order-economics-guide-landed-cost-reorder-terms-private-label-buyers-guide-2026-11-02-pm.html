Why the Minimum Order Quantity Feels Arbitrary

Every ribbon quote arrives with a minimum order quantity, and every buyer has the same reaction: why so many? It is a reasonable reaction, because from the outside a minimum order quantity looks like a way for a factory to protect its margin. The more useful way to read it is as an honest disclosure of where the setup cost sits.

Consider what actually has to happen before the first metre of a custom ribbon exists. For a printed ribbon on a stock construction, the factory has to set up the screens, one per colour, and register each one to the ribbon. It has to run a test length and check the registration against the artwork. It has to approve the ground colour against a lab dip. If the ground colour also has to be dyed, there is a dye lot, a recipe, a dipping run and a drying cycle. For a jacquard ribbon there is a weaving programme, a card or paper, a set-up sample and a run at slow speed before the loom can run at production speed.

All of that setup is essentially fixed. It does not get cheaper because you order fewer metres; if anything, the cost per metre of setup gets worse as the volume drops. So the minimum order quantity is not the factory being greedy. It is the point at which the fixed setup cost per metre stops being indefensible.

Understanding this changes how you approach the conversation. Instead of asking why the minimum is 1,000 metres, the productive questions become: what is driving this specific minimum, what would change it, and what is the actual cost per metre across different volumes? Those questions can be answered, and the answers are frequently more useful than the number on the quotation.

What Actually Sets the Minimum

Four things determine the minimum order quantity for a ribbon programme, and they pull in different directions.

Setup amortisation. The larger the fixed setup, the higher the minimum. A one-colour screen print on a stock construction has a small setup, so the minimum can be low. A four-colour jacquard with a custom weave has a large setup, so the minimum is high. This is the single biggest driver.

Material purchasing. Yarn and base fabric are bought by the kilogram or by the cone, and dyeing works in lot sizes. If your programme uses a colour that is not in stock, the mill has to buy and dye a minimum quantity, and that minimum may exceed your entire programme volume. Sometimes the most effective way to lower a minimum is simply to choose a ground colour that the mill already holds.

Colour count and decoration method. Each additional colour in screen printing adds a screen, a pass and a registration variable. This is why a one-colour ribbon can be quoted at 500 metres and the same design in four colours cannot. Foil adds a die. Heat transfer reduces the per-colour setup but constrains the material. Jacquard adds a whole class of setup.

Finishing and packing requirements. Cut-to-length ribbon, pre-tied bows, header cards and individual polybags each add a handling and packing cost that has a minimum batch below which it is not worth running the line. A programme with heavy individual packing requirements will have a higher minimum than the same ribbon supplied on bulk rolls.

When you ask a factory to explain a minimum, this is what a good answer sounds like. "The 1,000 metre minimum is because we have two colours to set up and the base colour is a stock ground, so the screen setup covers roughly 400 metres of cost at your target price. If you went to 500 metres on a single colour with the same ground, we could probably run it." That is a different conversation from a number on a price sheet, and it is the conversation that produces a workable programme.

The Order Quantity Decision: A Working Method

Before you contact a factory, do the arithmetic that determines whether your volume is even viable for the construction you want. This takes twenty minutes and it will save you weeks.

Step one: work from consumption, not from aspiration. Take the number of units you expect to sell in your first planning cycle, multiply by the ribbon length per unit, and add the length needed for set-up waste, trimming and sampling. Ribbon consumption per unit varies enormously by category: a single bow on a gift bag might use 250mm, a wrapped gift box might use 1.5 metres, and a ribbon used across a full packaging set with a bow and a belly band might use three metres or more. Be honest about the trim allowance, which is typically five to ten per cent, plus a metre or two for the factory's own set-up and sample lengths.

Step two: subtract what you already have. If you are already buying an unprinted or differently branded ribbon and are switching to a custom one, your effective new volume in the first year is only the incremental quantity. This is a real and frequently overlooked lever: a programme that looks uneconomic at full volume may be very economic as an incremental add-on to an existing buy.

Step three: compare against the minimum, and read the gap carefully. If your calculated need is 700 metres and the minimum is 1,000, you have three sensible options. Order the full 1,000 and carry the balance as inventory, which is the simplest and usually the right answer if the leftover is a colour or construction you would use again. Redesign to hit the minimum, for example by consolidating two colours into one construction or reducing the number of SKUs. Or change construction to one with a lower minimum, such as a stock ground with printed banding.

Step four: model the unit cost at three volumes. Ask for the price at the minimum, at double the minimum, and at your realistic annual volume. The curve is almost always concave, meaning unit cost falls as volume rises, and the size of that fall tells you whether it is worth waiting to grow into a better price or whether you should take the smaller run now. A useful rule of thumb is that the price difference between the minimum and five times the minimum is typically in the region of fifteen to thirty per cent, which is meaningful but rarely the deciding factor for a launch.

Step five: check the reorder economics before you commit. The first order and the reorder are priced differently in a well-run factory, and the difference is often larger than buyers expect. Reorders avoid screen setup, often avoid the colour lab dip, and sometimes avoid minimum quantity entirely because the material is already dyed and in stock. Understand the reorder terms before the first order, because that is what determines whether the programme is viable at scale.

Strategies for Getting Below the Minimum

When the minimum genuinely does not fit your volume, there are a number of legitimate routes. None of them are compromises in the way that "we could get it cheaper if you order more" is a compromise. Each of them changes the economics of the programme in a different direction, and they are worth understanding before you assume the minimum is immovable.

Use a stock ground colour

The most effective and least compromising lever. If you use a ground colour the mill already holds, you eliminate the dyeing setup and the material minimum, and the minimum becomes a function of printing setup alone. The brand cost is that your ribbon ground is not your exclusive colour, which matters in some categories and not in others. For many buyers this is entirely acceptable; for a luxury gift programme where the ground colour is part of the brand signature, it is not.

Reduce the colour count

Each screen is a setup. Moving from four colours to two typically moves the minimum down by a meaningful proportion and reduces both the unit price and the registration risk. There is also a design argument here: a ribbon carrying one strong brand colour is frequently more effective at shelf distance than one carrying four competing ones. Many buyers who consolidate during a MOQ negotiation keep the consolidated design permanently.

Consolidate SKUs rather than reduce volume

If you need four colours, consider whether you need them as four separate minimums or as four colourways of one construction. Four colourways of a single base ribbon often carries a lower combined minimum than four separate constructions, because the base is set up once.

Choose a decoration method with lower setup

Heat transfer has a much lower per-colour setup than screen printing, so for short runs with many colours it can be the more economical route despite a higher per-metre rate. It does constrain you to suitable materials, so this is a joint decision with the factory rather than a lever you can pull alone.

Apply branding outside the ribbon

A branded hang tag, a printed belly band or a printed card inside the box carries the brand message at a fraction of the setup cost of a printed ribbon. This is not a failure of the programme; it is frequently the better design decision, particularly where the ribbon is a supporting element rather than the primary brand statement. Many successful gift programmes pair a stock ribbon with a printed band.

Batch with another brand

This is not something you would ask a factory to arrange for you, but it is worth knowing that some programmes naturally consolidate: a retailer ordering several brands from one supplier, a distributor placing a shared order, or a brand that already has an annual volume on the same construction. If you have an existing relationship with a factory, the economies may already be available to you.

What the Per-Metre Price Is Actually Made Of

Buyers often compare quotations on the headline per-metre price alone, and that is the wrong comparison, because two quotations at the same headline price can differ enormously in what they contain. A useful per-metre figure is built from a small number of identifiable components, and a good quotation lets you see them.

Base material. The yarn and the weave. This is usually the largest single component and it varies most with material choice. Polyester satin, grosgrain, velvet and organza are different price points for structural reasons, not because anyone is charging more for the same thing.

Colour. Dyeing a base fabric to a specific colour is a material cost when the colour is standard and a setup-plus-material cost when it is custom. A custom colour carries the dyeing cost for the whole lot even where it is not repeated in the price line, which is why custom grounds are quoted differently from stock grounds.

Decoration setup, amortised. Screens, dies, weave programmes. This is the part that changes most between a first order and a reorder, and it is the reason a first order is priced higher per metre.

Decoration run cost. The actual application of the colour or foil.

Finishing. Slitting to width, edge treatment, winding, cut-to-length, bow assembly if applicable.

Packing. Bulk rolls, spools, header cards, polybags, cartons.

Quality and inspection. Setup and sampling costs, plus any third-party testing you have specified.

Overhead and margin. The factory's cost of running the business, plus its profit.

Terms and logistics. Payment terms, Incoterm, and anything included in the price by agreement such as pre-shipment inspection or freight.

Two further points on comparison. First, confirm what quantity the price relates to. A quotation at 1,000 metres and a quotation at 10,000 metres look like a pricing difference but are mostly a volume difference. Second, compare on the same scope. A quotation including a lab dip, an inspection report and freight to your door is not comparable to a quotation that is ex-works and excludes testing. When quotes differ by more than about ten per cent, the difference is nearly always scope rather than margin.

Total Landed Cost: The Number That Actually Matters

The per-metre price is a factory number. Your business runs on a landed number, and the two diverge more than most buyers expect. The gap between them is where programmes quietly lose money.

Moving from the quoted price to your landed cost adds a set of costs that should be visible in your model from the start. Freight and insurance depend on the mode and the volume density. Duty depends on the HS classification and the country of origin, and it is worth checking whether the classification has been considered correctly rather than assumed. Brokerage and customs clearance have a fixed component per shipment that is significant on small volumes. Inspection and testing costs are per-shipment as well as per-order. Financing the lead time is a real cost: if the factory takes eight weeks and pays thirty days, that is roughly a quarter of a year of your cash sitting in ribbon, and the cost of that cash is a legitimate line in the model. Packaging waste, damage in transit and the scrapping of stock that does not sell are costs that rarely appear in a quotation but do appear in the accounts.

Add to that the commercial reality of the carry. If your minimum forces you to buy 1,000 metres and your first-year need is 700, you have 300 metres of inventory. That inventory has a storage cost, it ties up working capital, and in the case of a seasonal colourway it may be dead stock that will not sell next season and will eventually be written down. This is very often the largest single cost of a minimum order quantity, and it is invisible in the per-metre quote.

The practical consequence is a simple rule: the minimum order quantity should be judged against your landed unit cost, not against the factory's per-metre price. A construction that looks expensive per metre can be cheaper landed if it eliminates a scrapping risk or a sub-scale colour, and the reverse is also true.

Small-Batch Strategy: Building Volume Without Building Inventory

The strategic question behind MOQ is how to grow a programme without letting inventory outrun sales. There are a few approaches that work, and they are worth combining rather than choosing between.

Consolidate the launch. Launch one construction with one colourway and one decoration method, and add the second colourway only once the first has sold through. A narrower launch has a smaller minimum, a lower cash commitment and a much lower obsolescence risk. Most programmes that die do so from an over-broad range, not from a low price.

Stage the colour approval. Approve the ground colour with the factory first, in a plain construction, at a small quantity. Use that to confirm the colour and test the market. Then order the branded version once you know the colour sells. This converts one large risky order into a small colour test and a later confirmed order.

Keep the programme open across seasons. If a colourway is not seasonal and you can reorder at a lower minimum, the MOQ problem largely disappears after the first order. This is the argument for choosing a construction that reorders flexibly over one that looks marginally cheaper at higher volume but locks you in.

Negotiate the reorder minimum explicitly. Many factories will quote a higher minimum for a first order and a lower one for a reorder of an approved design, because the setup is already done. This is a normal thing to ask for and it should be written into the quotation so it is binding.

Align with a demand calendar, not a budget cycle. Peak seasons have different minimums, different lead times and different freight economics. A programme that is planned around the selling calendar rather than the financial calendar will usually come out better on both cash and availability.

Reading a Quotation So You Can Negotiate It

Given a quotation, the useful exercise is to reconstruct it. Take the quoted unit price and the quantity, multiply them, and compare the total against what the construction and decoration should plausibly cost at that volume. If the total looks high, the reason is usually in the scope rather than in the margin: the ground colour is custom rather than stock, the quotation includes a lab dip and testing that you did not need, the packing is individual rather than bulk, or there is a second colourway priced in. Each of those is negotiable or removable.

Then work the levers that actually move a price, in roughly the order of their impact.

Quantity. The largest lever, and the one you should use only if you are confident the volume is real. Committing to volume you cannot use converts a price problem into an inventory problem.

Colour count. Each screen removed saves setup and improves registration.

Ground colour choice. Moving to a stock ground removes the dyeing setup entirely.

Decoration method. Screen printing versus heat transfer versus jacquard changes both setup and unit cost, and the right answer depends on volume and material.

Packing specification. Bulk rolls against individually bagged lengths is often a meaningful difference and is frequently overlooked entirely.

Payment terms and Incoterm. These do not change the unit price but they change the landed cost and the cash cycle, and they are usually more available than buyers realise, particularly on a repeat order.

Two things to hold onto. Do not negotiate a price without a specification, because you will get a lower price for a different product and then argue about quality later. And do not negotiate only price. Lead time, capacity reservation for peak season, the right to reorder at the minimum, quality remedy terms, and the definition of acceptable quality are all worth more to you than a small percentage on unit price, and they are usually granted more readily because they cost the factory less.

A Working Approach to Minimum Order Quantities

The buyers who handle small and medium volumes well share a habit: they treat the minimum order quantity as a planning input rather than a wall. They calculate their real consumption before they order, they understand which levers actually move it, they model the landed cost rather than the quoted price, and they negotiate the reorder terms at the same time as the first order because that is when the factory has the most reason to agree.

None of this requires a large volume. It requires knowing your own numbers before you open the conversation, which is the difference between negotiating a programme and accepting one.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, category buyers and procurement managers most often ask us about ribbon minimums, quotations and order economics. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the minimum order quantity for custom ribbon?

For a fully custom ribbon with your own construction and colour we typically start at 1,000 metres per design and colourway, and we run small batches from 500 metres on selected constructions, single-colour designs and stock ground colours. The most effective lever on the minimum is usually the ground colour: choosing a colour the mill already holds removes the dyeing setup entirely and often takes the minimum down substantially. If you tell us your actual annual volume and your preferred ground colour, we can tell you honestly whether custom manufacture is the right route or whether a stock construction with a printed band would serve you better.

2. Why is the minimum order quantity so high on custom ribbon?

Because of setup, and the factory is usually telling you the truth when it quotes it. Before the first metre exists, a printed ribbon needs screens cut and registered for each colour, a ground colour lab-dipped and approved, and a test run checked against the artwork. A jacquard ribbon additionally needs a weaving programme set up and run at slow speed first. That cost is fixed and does not get cheaper with fewer metres, so the minimum is the point at which the setup cost per metre stops being defensible. Ask any factory to break down the minimum for your specific programme and a good one will tell you which setup is driving it and what would change it.

3. Can I get a custom ribbon order below the minimum?

In most cases yes, by changing something other than the quantity. Moving to a stock ground colour removes the dyeing setup. Reducing the colour count removes a screen per colour. Consolidating four colourways of one construction into a single base with four print options often carries a lower combined minimum than four separate constructions. Applying branding to a hang tag or belly band instead of to the ribbon carries the brand at a fraction of the setup cost. And on a second order of an approved design, most factories will quote a lower minimum because the setup is already done. Ask for the reorder minimum in writing at the time of the first quotation.

4. Is it cheaper to order more metres or to run a smaller batch?

It depends on whether you will use them, which is the only question that matters. Unit price almost always falls as volume rises, typically by somewhere in the region of fifteen to thirty per cent between the minimum and five times the minimum. But every metre beyond your requirement is working capital, storage, and a risk of obsolescence if the colourway is seasonal. For a growing brand the usual right answer is to take the minimum on the first order, use the unit price saving to buy down a second construction later, and let the reorder terms improve as volume proves out. Buying volume you cannot sell to reach a better price is the most common way buyers lose money on a minimum order quantity.

5. What should a ribbon quotation actually itemise?

A quotation that lets you negotiate should break out the base material, the colour, the decoration setup amortised across the run, the decoration run cost, finishing, packing, quality and inspection, overhead and margin, and the payment and Incoterm terms. If you cannot see the components, you cannot tell whether a price difference is margin or scope. The most common reason two quotations differ by more than about ten per cent is not greed on either side; it is that one includes a lab dip, third-party testing and freight to your door while the other is ex-works and excludes them. Always compare like with like, and always check what quantity the quoted price actually refers to.

6. How do I work out what quantity I actually need?

Work from consumption, not from aspiration. Take expected unit sales for your first planning cycle, multiply by the ribbon length used per unit, then add set-up waste, trimming allowance and sampling. Typical consumption ranges from about 250mm for a single bow on a gift bag to 1.5 metres for a wrapped gift box and three metres or more for a full packaging set with bow and belly band. Add five to ten per cent for trim plus a metre or two for factory set-up. If you are switching from an existing ribbon buy, your incremental new volume is only the difference, which is often enough to change which construction makes sense.

7. What does landed cost include that the factory's price does not?

Your landed unit cost adds freight and insurance, duty based on the HS classification and country of origin, brokerage and customs clearance including its per-shipment fixed component, inspection and third-party testing, and the financing cost of the lead time, since cash sits in ribbon for roughly a quarter of a year on an eight-week production run plus thirty-day terms. It also includes the cost of the carry, which is often the largest single item and never appears in a quotation: inventory above your requirement is working capital, storage, and, for a seasonal colourway, eventual write-down. Judge the minimum order quantity against landed cost rather than against the factory's per-metre price.
