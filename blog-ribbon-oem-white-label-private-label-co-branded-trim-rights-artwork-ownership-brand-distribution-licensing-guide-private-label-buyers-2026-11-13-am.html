Three words buyers use interchangeably

Ask five brand owners how they got their ribbon made and you will get five different answers, most of them describing the same commercial arrangement. The vocabulary has drifted because the people using it are not the people negotiating the contracts. A sourcing manager says "white label" because the supplier's catalogue page says so. A finance director says "private label" because that is the line item in the budget. A lawyer drafts the agreement and, if she is good, writes down what actually matters — which is almost never the vocabulary at all.

What actually determines your risk, your cost structure and your exit options is a small set of concrete facts:

  • Who owns the brand identity that appears on the product.
  • Who owns the artwork and who licensed it to whom.
  • Who owns the physical tooling — the moulds, dies, screens and plates.
  • Who may sell the finished product, where, and through which channels.
  • Who bears the cost and the risk of a change after the first order.

This guide works through the three arrangements you will actually encounter, then goes clause by clause through the rights that sit underneath all of them. It is written for the brand owner or procurement manager who has been handed the word "private label" in a brief and needs to know what to actually ask for before signing.

One framing point before we start: all three routes are legitimate, and none of them is inherently better than the others. A white label route is right for a retailer who wants a fast-moving consumable line with low creative overhead. A co-branded route is right for a brand with licensed demand. A true private label route is right for a brand building an identity asset. The mistake is not choosing the wrong route — it is choosing one route and contracting as though you had chosen another.

Route one: white label — buying a generic product

Under a white label arrangement, the factory sells you a product that already exists in its catalogue. It may carry a neutral or unbranded appearance, or a brand name that the factory is willing to replace with yours at no meaningful cost. The construction, the decoration method and sometimes the colour range were decided by the factory. You are choosing from a menu.

What you are buying. A proven article, already tooled, already specified, often already in production with other customers. The lead time from order to delivery is usually the shortest available, because the material may be in stock and the line is set up for that article. The unit price is usually the lowest available, because the setup cost is amortised across many customers.

What you give up. Differentiation. If five retailers buy the same white label bow from the same factory, the only thing that distinguishes you on the shelf is your packaging, your price and your marketing. That is a perfectly legitimate business, but it is a different business from building a brand on the product itself.

What you should still protect. Even in a white label arrangement, you are buying a physical product, and two things are worth writing down. First, a quality specification and tolerance framework — you should know what constitutes a defect on this article regardless of who designed it, because when it fails you cannot simply return it to another retailer. Second, a confidentiality term about your packaging artwork, packaging artwork is frequently the only thing that is actually yours.

White label is genuinely under-rated as a route for certain categories. If you are a gift shop chain, a party supplies business, or a subscription box operator buying a consumable trim, the speed and unit economics of an off-the-shelf article will often beat the economics of a bespoke programme, and the difference will never be visible on the shelf.

Route two: private label — owning the brand on the product

A private label programme means the finished product carries the buyer's brand. You decide the construction, the width, the colour, the decoration, the packaging, the barcode, the instructions — everything the customer sees. The factory supplies the manufacturing capability and, frequently, some level of design support.

This is the most common route for a brand owner building a recognised line, and it is the route with the most moving parts, because it involves the most assets: artwork, colour standards, tooling, packaging design, brand guidelines, and the documentation that ties them together.

The critical thing to understand is that private label describes the commercial outcome, not the legal structure. Behind "private label" you will usually find an OEM arrangement (the factory builds to your specification), sometimes combined with ODM elements (the factory proposes the construction and you adjust it). The agreement needs to specify which of those is happening at each stage, because it determines who pays for a change and who owns the result.

What a private label programme genuinely requires that a white label one does not:

  • A written specification covering construction, material, width, weight, finish, decoration and tolerances.
  • A colour standard approved against a defined reference, not an informal "close enough" approval.
  • An artwork file with a revision number, and a rule that only that revision is production-authorised.
  • Tooling, where the product is moulded or cut, documented with ownership stated.
  • Packaging artwork, barcodes and labelling handled as your IP, not as a supplier asset.
  • Inspection criteria that both sides agreed before production rather than after a complaint.

The cost difference between a white label article and a private label article is usually dominated by setup — new colour development, new screens or dies, new packaging — rather than by unit price. That is why private label economics improve sharply across a programme's lifecycle and why the reorder rate matters more than the first-order unit cost. It is also why a private label programme should be planned as a multi-season relationship rather than a single purchase.

Route three: co-branded — two brands, one product

Co-branded product carries two separately owned brands. Usually one is a licensor — a character, an entertainment property, a sports mark, a fashion house — and the other is the manufacturer or the retailer. You see it constantly in gift packaging, seasonal decoration and licensed children's products.

Why brands do it. The economics are straightforward. A licensed property has proven demand that you would spend a great deal of marketing money to create. The licence fee is a cost, but it buys demand you could not otherwise buy. Co-branding also lets you reach an audience that already trusts the licensor, which shortens the trust-building curve considerably.

Why it is structurally riskier than it looks. Every unit in the programme depends on a licence. Licences can be terminated, can be time-limited to a season or a year, can be restricted by territory, by channel, by product category, or by volume threshold. A co-branded line that reaches meaningful revenue share in your business is a dependency that needs to be managed like any other critical supplier dependency — and arguably like any other brand dependency, because the licensor is in a position to dictate price at renewal.

The practical protections are unglamorous and effective. Keep the co-branded line at a share of your programme that you can absorb without it. Maintain a parallel unbranded or house-brand version of the same article so the shelf position is not empty if a licence lapses. Read the termination terms before you commit volume, not after. And record the licence reference on the purchase order and the artwork file, so that when artwork is revised, the revision is checked against the current licence scope rather than a licence that expired last March.

From a manufacturing standpoint, co-branded ribbon is unusual in one specific way: the licensor's brand guidelines will usually impose decoration and quality constraints stricter than the buyer's own. That is usually good — a more demanding specification produces a better product — but it does mean the artwork approval loop involves a third party, and you should build that third party's response time into the schedule rather than discovering it as a delay.

Artwork ownership: the first question to settle

Artwork is where most brand protection is won or lost, and it is also where the most avoidable disputes originate. The principle is simple: the party that created the artwork owns it, unless the contract says otherwise. Everything else is detail.

If you supply the artwork. You own it. The file you send is your property. What the contract must then add is a set of promises from the factory: that the artwork is treated as confidential, that it will not be used for any other customer or product, that it will not be shown to or discussed with other buyers, and that it will not approach your named competitors with a comparable product. Without those promises, you have handed your design to a company that could, in principle, produce a near-identical article for somebody else — and for a brand whose visual identity is the product, that is the whole business.

If the factory designs the artwork for you. Ownership is genuinely ambiguous unless you say so. Three workable outcomes exist, and they are not equivalent:

  1. Assignment. The designer assigns you all rights on payment. Cleanest, but it requires a contract between you and whoever designed it, and the factory must be able to give you that assurance about its own designers and any freelancers it used.
  2. Licence. You receive a licence to use the design for ribbon products, tied to your brand, potentially with an exclusivity window. Less clean, because you do not control modification and cannot enforce against a third party.
  3. Ownership retained by the factory. You have a licence to use it, and the factory may use it elsewhere. This is common and sometimes perfectly reasonable, particularly for a generic motif. It is only a problem if the design carries your brand identity, which it would in most private label programmes.

What to ask for in any case. A written statement of who owns the artwork; a modification right, because a design that cannot be adapted when your packaging changes becomes a constraint rather than an asset; the file format you receive, and whether you receive the working file or only a flattened print-ready file; and confirmation that any third-party elements in the design — fonts, stock photography, licensed clip art — are cleared for commercial printing on your behalf. The last point catches more brands than it should, and it is entirely preventable.

For our own part, we ask buyers to confirm in writing that the artwork they supply is theirs to use, and we confirm in return that we will not use customer artwork for any other customer or product. We will decline to quote the same branded artwork for a different buyer. Where a design is genuinely generic and carries no brand identity, this protection is less relevant, and we say so rather than pretending otherwise.

Tooling, moulds and dies: who owns the metal

Tooling is the asset buyers most often leave undocumented, and it is also the one that most clearly determines whether you can leave.

Print tooling — screens, plates, dies, cylinders. These carry the artwork. Their ownership effectively follows the artwork ownership question, and it should be stated explicitly rather than inferred. If you own the artwork, you should own the screens and plates made from it, and the contract should say so, along with whether you want them returned on exit or destroyed on your instruction. A factory holding your screens is holding a reproducible copy of your artwork, which is a leverage position you probably did not intend to create.

Shape tooling — moulds, dies, cutting forms for bows, flower trims and formed products. Here the money is bigger and the exit question is sharper. If you fund a mould for a specific bow or flower, the agreement should identify it, give it a reference number, and state that it is your property. Critically, it should include a buy-back or transfer right: if the relationship ends before the mould is retired, you should be able to take the mould, or to have it transferred to your next supplier.

Without a buy-back right, you have paid several thousand dollars for an asset that is stuck in someone else's warehouse and can only be used by the company you just stopped working with. This is a genuinely common trap, and it is entirely preventable by a single clause.

Two further points on tooling. First, the amortisation question: who pays for tooling, and does the unit price embed it, or is it a separate one-off? A good agreement makes that explicit, because it changes how you negotiate the second order. Second, maintenance and ownership: if a mould is used heavily, it wears. Who maintains it, who pays for refurbishment, and what happens if it fails mid-production are all questions that are much cheaper to answer at the start than during a peak season.

Confidentiality and non-use: what the contract must say

Confidentiality clauses are often written so broadly that they are meaningless, or so narrowly that they do not protect the asset you care about. What you want is a clause that covers four specific things.

Definition of what is protected. Not "all information" but a defined list: artwork and design files, colour standards and lab dips, packaging artwork, the brand name and marks, your forecasts, volumes and pricing, your customer's information if you are producing to a retailer's specification, and tooling. Naming it is faster than arguing later about whether something was covered.

Duration. Confidentiality should survive the end of the commercial relationship, and it should be stated to survive for a defined period. A clause that expires the moment you stop placing orders protects nothing during the period that matters most — the period after you leave, when the artwork is still in the factory's hands.

Permitted use. The clause should state positively that the artwork may be used only to manufacture the buyer's product, and only for the buyer's brand and the agreed territories and channels. A permitted-use statement is stronger than a prohibition because it defines the boundary, and boundaries are easier to enforce than prohibitions.

Non-solicitation of the design. For a recognisable branded article, a clause stating that the factory will not produce the same or substantially similar decorated product for any other customer, and will not approach the buyer's named competitors. We think this is fair and we accept it for branded work. For a generic article with no brand identity, it is not something a supplier could agree to, and it is not something you need.

A note on the sub-supply chain. If any part of the process — printing, converting, assembly — is subcontracted, your confidentiality protections have to reach that subcontractor too. If the factory will not confirm who it uses for a sensitive decoration, that is worth knowing before you disclose your artwork, not after.

Territory, channel and exclusivity

Most ribbon is sold in a global market with a handful of real chokepoints. The commercial question is who may sell what, where, and for how long.

Your territory rights. If you have invested in a private label design and built a brand around it, you will usually want exclusivity in your home market and possibly in specified regions. You should be clear that exclusivity without a minimum volume commitment is rarely granted, and without a term it is fragile. The workable structure is reciprocity: exclusive territory for you in defined markets against a stated minimum annual volume, with the exclusivity reviewed on an annual basis.

Channel rights. If the factory may sell the same branded article to a retailer that competes with you in the same channel, you have a conflict. If it may not, you should have it written down. The common pattern for a brand owner who sells through retail is that the factory is prohibited from selling your branded article direct to retailers in your channel, while remaining free to sell the generic version to others. That distinction is worth making explicitly, because it is easy to write "no competition" and accidentally create a supplier who feels obliged to restrict their whole business.

Duration and renewal. Exclusivity that has no end date is a liability as much as an asset. Set a term, set a review point, and make sure that if the relationship ends the exclusivity ends with it — rather than continuing to bind you while no longer protecting you.

The ten clauses a private label ribbon agreement needs

Here is the structure we work to when a buyer asks us to put a private label ribbon programme on paper. It is deliberately short, because a short agreement that is actually followed beats a long one that is not.

  1. Artwork and IP ownership. Who owns the artwork, who may use it, modification rights, file formats delivered, third-party elements cleared.
  2. Tooling ownership and buy-back. Each tool identified by reference, ownership stated, buy-back or transfer on exit, maintenance responsibility.
  3. Confidentiality. Defined protected information, duration, permitted use, and the obligation to flow down to subcontractors.
  4. Non-use and non-competition. No reuse of your artwork or branding for other customers, no approach to named competitors, reasonable duration and territory.
  5. Territory, channel and exclusivity. Where the article may be sold, by whom, the exclusivity term, the volume conditions and the review mechanism.
  6. Specification and tolerances. The technical specification, the decoration and colour standards, the tolerance framework, and what counts as a defect. Referenced by revision number so there is never a question about which version applies.
  7. Inspection and defect remedy. AQL and inspection method, pre-shipment inspection, the remedy ladder — replacement, rework, credit — and the evidence the buyer must supply to invoke it.
  8. Price adjustment and volume tiers. What triggers a price change, how a change is notified, and what volume breaks apply.
  9. Lead time, capacity and continuity. Standard lead time, notice period for schedule changes, peak-season capacity reservation, and what happens if a lead time is missed.
  10. Exit. What happens to tooling, retained samples, artwork files and open orders when the relationship ends, and the notice period on both sides.

Two clauses are absent from that list on purpose, and their absence is deliberate. There is no clause about "mutual benefit" or "long-term partnership", because such language is unenforceable and creates an expectation without a mechanism. And there is no clause making the buyer responsible for the factory's financial health, because a clause cannot make a supplier solvent — only diligence and order-book visibility can give you early warning.

What we would tell a buyer who can only add one clause: if you can only add one thing to an existing agreement, make it tooling ownership with a buy-back right. It is the clause most often missing, it is the clause that determines whether you can leave without losing an asset, and adding it costs nothing if the supplier has nothing to hide.

Choosing a route: a practical decision guide

Choose white label if the article is a functional or consumable trim where the customer's choice is driven by price and availability; you are building assortment breadth quickly and differentiation comes from elsewhere; the category is commoditised and creative differentiation has little commercial value; or you are testing a category before committing development spend.

Choose private label if the brand identity is a meaningful part of the value proposition; you are building a range you expect to repeat across seasons; the competitive set differentiates on design and finish; or you intend to develop this line into an asset with multi-year shelf life.

Choose co-branded if you have access to a licensed property with genuine, demonstrable demand; the licence economics beat what you would spend on marketing to reach the same audience; and you have an exit plan for the event that the licence does not continue.

Consider a hybrid if you want private label differentiation on a construction the factory already runs well — your artwork and branding on a proven article, with the factory's standard construction underneath. This is frequently the best value route and it is under-used. It gives you brand distinctiveness with a much lower development cost than a fully bespoke article, and it is far easier to reorder because the underlying construction is already in production.

Whichever route you choose, the sequence is the same: settle the rights before you settle the price. Once artwork has been transmitted and samples have been made, the leverage shifts, and it shifts against you. The cheapest negotiation you will ever have is the one you have before you have disclosed anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement teams most often ask about private label and white label ribbon: who owns the artwork, who owns the moulds, what a supplier's refusal to sign an IP clause means, and what an agreement actually needs to cover. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between white label and private label ribbon?

The difference is who owns the brand identity, not who makes the product. In a white label arrangement the factory sells you a product that already carries a generic or neutral identity, and you resell it largely as supplied. In a private label arrangement you supply the brand name, artwork and packaging and the factory manufactures to your specification under your brand. A middle ground exists where the factory's own product line carries your name on the packaging but the construction and decoration are theirs. These three routes carry very different IP, tooling and exit costs, which is why the distinction should be settled before the first purchase order rather than during a dispute.

2. Who owns the artwork on private label ribbon?

You should own it, and it should be written down. If you create the artwork and you supply the file, you own it. But you also need the contract to confirm that the factory does not retain a right to use it, that the factory does not reuse the design for another customer, and that the factory holds any third-party licences needed to reproduce it. We ask for written confirmation that artwork supplied by the buyer remains the buyer's property, and we do not reuse customer artwork for other accounts. Where the factory designs the artwork on your behalf, the contract should say who owns the design, whether you may modify it, and whether exclusivity applies.

3. Who owns the moulds and tooling for custom ribbon bows and trims?

This is the clause buyers most often leave out of a first agreement and most often regret. Any physical tooling or mould paid for by the buyer as a specific capital item should be identified in the agreement, listed with a reference number, and stated to belong to the buyer. For moulds, you normally also expect a buy-back or transfer right if the relationship ends before the tooling is retired, otherwise you have paid for an asset you cannot take with you. Dies, plates and screens used in printing are a slightly different case because they carry the artwork, and their ownership follows the artwork ownership question. We document tooling ownership in the agreement rather than leaving it to custom.

4. What is co-branded ribbon and when does it make sense?

Co-branded means two separately owned brands appear on the same product, usually a licensor such as a character, entertainment property or a fashion house, and the manufacturer or retailer. It makes sense when you have access to a licensed property with genuine demand and you want to capture the margin that a license would otherwise absorb. The trade-off is that every unit in that programme depends on a licence that can be terminated, time-limited or restricted by territory or channel. If the co-branded line is a meaningful share of your revenue, you need an exit plan that does not assume the licence continues.

5. Can a factory sell my branded ribbon to other customers?

Not without your permission, and the permission should be explicit. A well-run factory will not approach your competitors with your artwork, your colour references or your packaging. But the protection is contractual rather than cultural, and the clause should state three things: that the artwork is confidential, that the factory will not use it for any other customer or product, and that it will not approach your named competitors. For a decorated ribbon carrying a recognisable brand, we would also decline to quote the same artwork for another buyer. For a generic construction with no branding, that protection does not apply and does not need to.

6. What should a private label ribbon agreement actually cover?

At minimum: artwork and IP ownership, tooling ownership and buy-back, confidentiality, non-use of your brand or artwork for other customers, your territory and channel rights, minimum order volumes and any exclusivity, the technical specification and tolerance framework, the inspection and defect remedy process, price adjustment triggers, and a clear exit clause covering what happens to tooling, retained samples and artwork on termination. Nine or ten clauses, written once, and done properly, will save considerably more than they cost.

7. How does private label differ from OEM and ODM in practice?

OEM means the factory manufactures to the buyer's supplied specification, using the buyer's artwork and construction. ODM means the factory designs the product and the buyer adopts it, often with modification. Private label describes the commercial arrangement rather than the design relationship: the product carries the buyer's brand. A programme can be private label and OEM at the same time, which is the most common combination. What matters for the agreement is not the label but the specifics: who supplied the design, who owns it, who owns the tooling, and who bears the cost of a change.

8. What should a supplier's refusal to sign an IP clause tell me?

Treat it as a serious signal and ask why. A supplier that refuses a straightforward confidentiality and artwork-ownership clause may be running a practice of reusing customer designs, may not own the underlying rights for its own standard products, or may not control its sub-supply chain. Any of those is a risk to your brand, because your artwork and your brand name are on the product and your customer's shelf. If we cannot agree a fair clause, the sensible move is to walk away rather than to proceed and hope. We are happy to have the clause reviewed by your counsel.
