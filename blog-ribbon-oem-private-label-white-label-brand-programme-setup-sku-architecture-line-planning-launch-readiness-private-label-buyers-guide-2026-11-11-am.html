Private label is often described as simply putting your own name on someone else's product. For ribbon and decorative trim it is not that at all. A ribbon programme is unusually exposed to the buyer's taste, because ribbon is visible, tactile, colour-critical and frequently the point at which a customer forms an impression of the whole product. A candle in a plain box with a beautiful ribbon is remembered. The same candle in a plain box with a cheap ribbon is not. That means the supply chain decisions in a ribbon programme are brand decisions, not procurement decisions, and treating them as the latter is the most common reason private label programmes in this category disappoint.

This guide covers how to set up a private label ribbon programme from the beginning: what you need to decide before contacting a factory, how to architect a range that a factory can actually run and a retailer can actually sell, where to spend colour development money, how to write a brief that gets comparable quotes, how to structure sampling and approval so that the standard is a physical object rather than a memory, how to plan a launch around real capacity, and how to keep hold of the brand once it is on sale.

The audience is brand owners and procurement leads building a private label range in ribbon, bows, tassels or packaging trim, typically for gift, beauty, home fragrance, confectionery, pet or apparel categories, and typically working with a factory in Asia for the first time.

What a Private Label Ribbon Programme Actually Is

Before the practical work, it is worth being clear about what a brand is buying. Ribbon is not a commodity in the way that a standard woven tape might be. Even at the simplest construction — a plain polyester satin of a given width and colour — the visual result is determined by the yarn, the weave, the dye, the finish and the winding, and each of those has a range of specifications that produce visibly different outcomes. A brand that treats ribbon as a commodity specification is asking for a quote that will be met with a substitute, and the substitute will be visible at exactly the moment the customer is deciding whether to re-order.

That means the real work of setting up a private label programme is specification. The factory relationship matters, but a good factory with a vague brief will give you a mediocre result, and a careful factory with a precise brief will give you something you can scale. The brief is the asset; the factory is one of several ways to realise it.

Three things are worth deciding before you approach anyone, because each one narrows the supplier field significantly and none of them can be changed later without cost:

  • Which product category the ribbon serves. Gift packaging, hair accessories, home fragrance, confectionery, apparel trim and pet accessories have almost nothing in common in terms of width, construction, hand-feel, certification requirements and price point. A supplier who is excellent in one is frequently mediocre in another. Decide the category first.
  • Who actually buys it. The end consumer, or a professional trade buyer in a craft store, a florist or a corporate gifting buyer. This determines everything from width to price to packaging.
  • Whether the ribbon is the product or the accompaniment. A brand selling bows is selling a product with a material cost. A brand selling gift boxes is buying an accessory that has to disappear into the presentation. These lead to entirely different specifications and, frequently, to different suppliers.

Starting From the Customer, Not the Factory

The most reliable way to build a range is to write the customer-facing description before you think about products. Not a mood board and not a colour palette — a sentence that describes what the range is for and who it is for, and a short list of the occasions it must work for.

"Soft, luxurious ribbon for gift packaging" is a starting point but not a specification. "Premium matte satin in muted seasonal shades for corporate gift hampers and subscription boxes, where the ribbon is seen but not handled much and must not shine under photography" is a brief that a factory can act on and a designer can build against. The second sentence also implies the exclusions, and the exclusions are usually where the money is saved.

Two practical tools make this more concrete.

The first is an occasion matrix. List the occasions the range must serve — birthday, wedding, Christmas, Mother's Day, Easter, Valentine's, corporate gift, seasonal retail — and mark each one against the constructions and colours you plan to carry. This quickly reveals where you have coverage and where a gap sits. It is normal for a first range to serve three or four occasions well and the rest not at all, and that is a better outcome than a range that nominally covers everything and has no depth anywhere.

The second is a competition teardown. Take the ten products you most admire in your category and record, for each: the material family, the width, the finish, the colour count, the visible decoration, the packaging format, the price point and the target customer. This is not about copying — copying is both a legal and a commercial mistake. It is about establishing the conventions of the category so that your range reads as competent to someone who buys this stuff professionally. A private label range that ignores the conventions confuses customers; one that reproduces them exactly has no reason to exist.

Building the SKU Architecture

A SKU architecture is the set of rules that decides what you will and will not make. Without one, a range grows one request at a time until it is a collection of unrelated products with no commercial logic, an unmanageable inventory position and a factory that is running far too many set-ups.

For ribbon, three tiers work well.

Tier 1 — The core

The construction(s) that carry the brand and appear in every season. Typically one or two: a satin or grosgrain in your primary width, in a set of core colours. This tier should account for the majority of your volume, and it is the tier where consistency of colour and quality matters most, because a customer who buys a core colour once and has a bad experience will not try again. Core colours should be chosen so that they can be stocked year-round and reordered without a colour match problem.

Tier 2 — The seasonal story

A defined colour family tied to a season or an occasion — a Christmas palette, a spring palette, a wedding palette. These are developed each year but follow a repeatable structure, so the factory can plan dyehouse time and you can plan marketing. This tier carries the visual identity of the range in the months when it matters most, and it is where the merchandising effort goes.

Tier 3 — The signature

One or two items that are unmistakably yours: a specific printed design, a distinctive width, a wired or wired-edge construction, a particular bow. These sell at higher margin, get photographed more, and define the brand. They are also the most expensive to develop, so the number should be small and the decision should be deliberate.

A workable arithmetic for a first launch: eight to twenty SKUs total, distributed roughly as four to eight core, six to ten seasonal, one to three signature. The exact number matters less than the fact that every SKU can be assigned to a tier and defended. If you cannot assign a SKU to a tier, it is a special request, and special requests are where range discipline goes to die.

The rules that keep the architecture intact

Write the rules down before the range starts, because they will be tested. Useful ones include: every new SKU must replace or extend an existing one, never sit beside it; a new colour must be distinguishable from every existing colour in the range under the lighting of your photography; a new construction must differ from an existing construction in a way a customer can see in their hand, not only on a specification sheet; and any SKU that has not sold through within a defined period is reviewed for removal rather than reordered automatically.

That last rule is the one most brands omit and most need. Ranges decay because nothing is ever removed. A SKU that sits at low velocity does not merely consume its own inventory; it consumes the shelf space that would sell a better one, the factory set-up time that could have made a fresher colour, and the attention of the buyer who now has to choose between two similar things. Decide in advance what happens to slow sellers, because deciding it later, with a warehouse full of the thing, is a different and much more expensive conversation.

Colour Strategy: Where to Spend Development Money

Colour is where private label programmes differentiate, and it is also where the money disappears. A bespoke colour match is not free: it typically requires a lab dip, one or more revision rounds, a colour standard approval, and the creation of a dye recipe that has to be re-matched for every subsequent reorder. Across a range of twenty SKUs with six bespoke colours, the development cost and the calendar time become a serious part of the programme.

The useful principle is that development money should be concentrated where the customer sees the difference, and removed where the customer cannot tell.

Across a core palette, most shades read as the same to a customer holding a finished product. The difference between two very similar pinks is a laboratory fact and a warehouse fact, not a shopping fact. But the difference between a well-chosen seasonal sage and a generic green is instantly visible in a photograph and on a shelf. So: develop bespoke in the signature items, in the anchor shade of the seasonal palette, and in any colour where a retailer or customer has specifically asked. Take the rest from a proven stock range.

There is a commercial advantage to this beyond cost. Stock colours and stock constructions are usually produced in larger quantities, which means better consistency, shorter lead times and lower minimum order quantities — all of which matter enormously for a first order. The colour you develop from scratch also becomes your colour, which is exactly why you should develop it deliberately rather than by accident, one SKU at a time.

Two practical warnings. First, ask the factory to hold a colour standard from production and to state the dye recipe reference for each approved shade, because "sage green" is not a specification and two factories will make two different sages. Second, define your tolerance in a form both parties can apply, whether that is a delta-E limit with a stated illuminant and observer, or a controlled physical comparison against a sealed reference under a defined viewing condition. This is covered in more detail in our guide on colour management, but the principle applies here: the standard must be an object, not a description.

Deciding What You Manufacture and What You Buy

Not every element of a private label ribbon range needs to come from the same factory, and treating it as a single procurement question is how programmes end up with a mismatch nobody planned.

Manufacture at the mill the things that are the product: printed ribbons, bows, wired edges, specific constructions that carry your identity. These are where your development brief, your sample ladder and your quality relationship matter.

Source from a specialist the things that are standard and where a specialist is clearly better: a particular trim type, a metal accessory, a specific box or carton format, a particular kind of paper or card. Ribbon factories are good at ribbon and indifferent at, for example, printed rigid boxes or metal eyelets, and the reverse is true. Asking a ribbon mill to subcontract a carton for you is possible but adds a coordination layer for a part that is not their strength.

Keep some things standard where the brand does not depend on them. Generic tissue, void fill, a standard shipping carton — these should be unremarkable and cheap. Deciding consciously to leave something unbranded is different from letting it happen by default, because a branded ribbon in a generic box reads as an unfinished programme, while a considered unbranded element reads as restraint.

The sequencing matters more than the mapping. Ribbon is the element most likely to determine whether the programme works, so it should be developed first and most carefully — the ribbon development timeline, not the packaging timeline, should set the launch date.

Writing the Development Brief

The brief is the document that determines whether you receive comparable quotes. A supplier who understands what you want can price it; a supplier who has to ask questions will price it differently from every other supplier, and the comparison becomes meaningless.

A workable brief contains the following, and nothing less if you want quotes you can compare side by side:

Product definition — construction, material family, width in millimetres with the tolerance you intend to live with, finish, and the decorative method if any (printed, jacquard, foil, embossed, wired). State whether the material is virgin polyester, RPET, paper-backed, or undecided.

Colour — named references with a standard, not descriptions. Either Pantone codes with an agreed tolerance, or named shades with a physical reference and a stated comparison method. Say whether the shade is from stock or to be developed, because the two are priced and scheduled completely differently.

Quantities by SKU — with the units stated. Metres per roll, metres per carton, pieces per unit. Include the first order and a stated reorder horizon, because a quote that assumes a one-time order is not a quote for a programme.

Decoration and artwork — the number of designs, the number of colours per design, whether repeat registration matters, whether artwork will be supplied by you or developed by the factory, and who owns the resulting files.

Packaging — roll format, core size, individual poly-bagging or vacuum-packing, carton dimensions and pieces per carton, carton markings, and the barcode requirement for each consumer unit.

Compliance and documentation — the certifications you require, the test reports you need per lot, the labelling requirements for each destination market, and the country-of-origin marking.

Delivery terms and destination — Incoterm, named place, port or airport, and the required arrival date rather than the required ship date. Most factories quote to a ship date, and the difference between the two is the difference between a supplier who understands your planning and one who does not.

Two things to avoid. Do not include a target price in the brief — it constrains the quotes in a way that makes them incomparable and teaches the factory to work to your number rather than to cost the job honestly. And do not omit the quantities, even approximately: a brief without quantities will come back priced at the minimum order quantity of every line, which is not a number you can compare across suppliers.

Samples, Approval and the Reference Standard

Sampling in this category goes through several stages and it is worth being precise about what each one proves, because buyers routinely approve something that does not mean what they think it means.

The stock swatch proves that the base material exists and roughly what it looks like. It proves nothing about your colour, your decoration or your construction in combination.

The lab dip proves the dye recipe can hit your target shade on your chosen material. It is a small piece of cloth. It does not prove that the colour will hold through weaving, that the hand-feel is right, or that the material will behave under a printed design.

The yarn-dyed or greige sample — the ribbon woven but undecorated — proves construction, width, hand and colour continuity across a real production quantity. This is the first sample that tells you whether the ribbon is right, and it is the point at which you can still change the construction without wasting decoration cost.

The production sample proves the decoration on real production material, with real registration, real repeat and real edge behaviour. This is the sample that should be signed, and it is the last point at which changes are cheap.

At each stage, ask the question that matches the sample. At the greige stage, ask about width, selvedge and shade continuity across the length. At the production sample stage, ask about print registration against the approved artwork, repeat length over at least ten full repeats, edge quality, and how the material handles being handled.

The critical step is what happens after approval. Approval should produce a sealed reference standard: a length of actual production material, signed by both parties, stored away from light, and used as the reference for everything that follows. When a reorder arrives nine months later, that sealed standard is what both sides measure against. Without it, the reorder is negotiated against a memory and a photograph, and the shade you receive will be the shade the dyehouse produced that week, which may not be the shade you approved. This is the single most common cause of the colour complaint on a third order in a private label programme, and it is entirely preventable.

Planning the Launch: Capacity, Timing and the First Order

New ranges launch badly when the launch date is a marketing date and the supply date is an assumption. Ribbon is seasonal, and the seasonal capacity in this industry is genuinely constrained — the same looms, the same dyehouse slots and the same freight capacity are all competing through the same peak months, and the factories that are best prepared are the ones that plan for it early.

The sequence that works:

  1. Confirm the supply position before the launch date is fixed. Ask the factory for a realistic lead time for your specific constructions and decoration method at your specific volume, and ask for it in weeks rather than in a range. Then work backwards from your required arrival date. If the maths does not work, change the launch date, not the plan.
  2. Book capacity for the seasonal period, not just for the launch. If your range peaks in November and December, the factory will want to know your re-order expectation in September, not in January. A capacity conversation in the spring, covering the whole year, is worth more than an urgent order in September.
  3. Make the first order deliberately smaller than the forecast. You do not yet know your sell-through, your returns rate, your retailer reorder behaviour or your true landed cost. A first order that is half the forecast costs less in dead inventory than one successful reorder cycle saves.
  4. Agree the reorder trigger in advance. Base it on actual sell-through over a defined period, with a defined reorder quantity and a price you have already fixed. This removes the most common failure mode in this category, which is a good month followed by a missed reorder because nobody decided who would place it or at what price.

One further point on timing. The ribbon should be the last thing to arrive before launch, not the first. Ribbon is light, bulky relative to its value, and can be shipped by air if necessary; the boxed product, the printed carton and the assembled sets cannot. Sequence the supply chain so that the most schedule-critical and least flexible elements are committed last, and you give yourself a recovery window when the ribbon is late.

Retail Readiness and Packaging

Most private label ribbon launch delays are packaging and logistics problems found in the final week, not manufacturing problems. Four things need to be right.

The consumer unit. Whether it is a spool, a carded bow, a tag-mounted piece or a set in a bag, it should be visible in a photograph, carry the brand clearly at shelf distance, carry a scannable barcode, and state the material and any care or safety information required in your market. For a spool, the presentation — band label, printed core, window card — does as much work as the ribbon itself at the point of sale.

The carton. Configure it so the store can open it and display it without damaging contents. Ribbon rolls that are over-compressed in a tight carton flatten and will not recover, which is one of the most common complaints in the category. State the permitted compression, the pieces per carton, and whether rolls are individually poly-bagged or vacuum-packed. Specify the carton markings exactly: product code, lot or batch number, colour code, quantity, production date, country of origin, and the format and language the receiving team can read without interpretation.

The documentation. Decide what travels with the shipment and what stays with your records: packing list, certificate of origin, the test report for the lot, and the certification evidence your retailer or customer will eventually ask for. A certificate you cannot produce within an hour is a certificate you do not have.

The artwork chain. Make sure the printing on the consumer unit, the carton and the shipping documentation all carry consistent artwork, and that the barcode resolves to a live listing before the first shipment leaves rather than after.

Protecting the Brand After LaunchThree protections are worth arranging deliberately, because all three become harder once the programme is trading.

Trademark. Register your brand name and your distinctive product names in your main markets before launch. Trademark rights acquired later are more expensive, slower and weaker, particularly against a distributor or marketplace seller who has already built equity in the name.

Ownership of artwork and tooling. Your print files, dies, screens, embroidery programmes and any physical tooling remain your property. State this explicitly in the supplier agreement, define what happens to them at the end of the programme — returned, destroyed with a certificate, or sold with your consent — and put it in the contract rather than in an email. The clause is one of the most commonly negotiated terms in this category, which is precisely why it is worth agreeing early, before both sides are invested in the relationship and before the tooling has any strategic value.

Distribution discipline. Decide how the brand will be distributed before it is launched, because most private label programmes that lose control of their brand lose it through the second tier in the first eighteen months rather than through copying. If you sell to a retailer who sells to another retailer who lists online under a different title, your brand is now attached to a product you did not make and cannot warrant. Put a channel policy in place: which channels carry the range, which do not, what happens if you find unauthorised listings, and how you will respond. Deciding this later, when you are already in the second tier, means unwinding relationships rather than setting rules.

From the Factory Side

It is worth understanding how a ribbon factory actually experiences a new private label programme, because it changes what you can reasonably ask for and how quickly.

A new programme is a scheduling problem before it is a manufacturing problem. The factory has to allocate loom time, dyehouse capacity, printing or finishing equipment, and inspection labour for something it has never made before, and it has to do so against committed volume from existing customers. A new programme that arrives with a clear brief, a realistic volume profile and an honest forecast is straightforward to schedule. A new programme that arrives with a vague brief, an aggressive launch date and a forecast that will double by the third order is genuinely difficult, and the factory will protect its committed volume rather than risk it.

This is why precise briefs and realistic first orders get treated better. It is not a favouritism; it is a scheduling decision. A factory can plan around a known commitment in a way it cannot plan around an aspiration, and the buyers who supply that certainty are the ones whose programmes get priority when capacity tightens in November.

At our own factory in Xiamen, which has been producing ribbon, bows, tassels and decorative trim since 2004, we run about 15,000 m² with more than 200 employees and daily output of 100,000 metres, and we hold OEKO-TEX, FSC, BSCI, SEDEX/SMETA and ISO 9001 certification. We also support OEM and ODM customisation from 1,000 metres with small-batch runs from 500 metres, which means a brand can develop a private label range at genuine scale without committing to a volume that would be wrong for a new programme. The structure of the relationship matters as much as the capability: a retained colour standard, a documented specification and a forecast we can plan against are what let a factory build your programme into the line rather than run it as a special job between other work.

Where to Start

If you are beginning now, this sequence is achievable without a formal programme document at the outset.

Write three sentences: what category this serves, who buys it, and whether the ribbon is the product or the accompaniment. Then build the occasion matrix and a ten-item competition teardown, and you will have more clarity about the range than most brands have after a full brand workshop.

Draft a three-tier SKU architecture with a proposed launch assortment in the eight-to-twenty range, and write the rules that protect it. Choose two core colours and one seasonal palette to develop, and leave the rest on stock bases.

Write the development brief as a one-page document with the sections above. Send the same brief to three or four factories and compare what comes back — the purpose of the brief at this stage is not to order, it is to find out which supplier understands your programme, which is more useful information than a price.

Then order greige samples before anything is decorated, because it is the cheapest point to change your mind about construction. Approve, seal the reference standard, and only then commission decoration.

That is a programme that can start production within a season, built on decisions you can explain to your team and defend to your customer. The sophistication can come afterwards; the difficulty of changing any of it later is why the order of the decisions matters so much.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement leads most often ask when they are setting up a private label ribbon programme: how many SKUs to launch with, whether to develop bespoke colours or use the factory's stock range, how to plan capacity for an untested range, what has to be ready before launch, and how to keep hold of the brand afterwards. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How many SKUs should a private label ribbon range launch with?

Fewer than most brands assume, and the number is driven by how the range will be merchandised rather than by how many colours or widths the factory can make. The working rule is that the launch assortment should fit on one shelf facing or one display fixture at your primary retail channel, because a range that cannot be merchandised as a coherent block will be bought as individual items and will not build recognition. In practice this usually means somewhere between eight and twenty SKUs for a first launch: a core set of constructions across your two or three most-used widths, a colour story of perhaps six to eight shades that a customer can understand as a family, and one or two items that carry the brand rather than the range. Add to this only when a specific buyer or retailer has asked for it. The risk in launching wide is not inventory cost — it is that each additional SKU carries its own colour development, sample, artwork and minimum order quantity, and your development budget is usually far smaller than your buying budget.

2. Should I develop a bespoke colour palette or start from the factory's stock range?

For a first private label programme, start from the factory's stock range and customise selectively. Stock colours and stock constructions carry no colour development charge, need no lab dip approval, and can usually be brought to a shippable state faster than a bespoke development, which means your first order can be a test of the market rather than a test of the supply chain. Bespoke colour is where a private label programme earns its differentiation, so it is worth paying for in the specific places it shows — the hero shade that anchors the brand, the seasonal colourway that cannot be bought off the shelf, the printed designs that carry your own artwork. Develop those deliberately, one or two per season, and let the rest of the range sit on proven bases. A programme that requires a bespoke match for every SKU will take four to six times as long to launch and will carry development cost into every reorder that needs a colour re-match.

3. How do I plan capacity with a ribbon factory for a launch I have not sold yet?

Ask for the factory's honest position on what it can hold for you and when, and treat that as the constraint that shapes your launch rather than an obstacle to it. Ribbon capacity is largely a question of loom allocation and dyehouse scheduling, and a factory that has run seasonal programmes for years will know its own realistic peak limits. The practical approach for an unproven range is to plan a launch order that is deliberately smaller than your forecast, spread across a wider window of dates, and to agree the re-order trigger in advance rather than discovering it after the first good month. Most factories will hold capacity for a programme once they can see a credible forecast, and some will reserve loom time for a new range on the strength of a first order plus a twelve-month commitment. What does not work is a large speculative opening order: it ties up cash in a range with no sell-through history, and it puts you in a weak negotiating position for the reorder.

4. What should be ready before I announce a private label ribbon launch?

Four things, in this order. First, a fully approved specification with a signed physical reference standard for each colour and construction — the sample on your desk is not the standard, a sealed length cut from production material is. Second, confirmed supply in the market, meaning you know your factory's lead time, your freight route and your realistic arrival date, and the launch date is set behind that rather than in front of it. Third, retail readiness: packaging that carries the brand clearly at shelf distance, a barcode on each unit, and the carton configured so that a store can open it and display it without damaging the contents. Fourth, a reorder mechanism — a planned reorder date, a trigger based on actual sell-through rather than a fixed guess, and an agreed price. Most launch delays in this category are not manufacturing delays; they are packaging and logistics problems discovered in the final week.

5. How do I protect a private label ribbon brand once it is on sale?

Three practical protections. Take out a trademark on your brand name and your distinctive product names in your main markets before launch, because establishing rights later is considerably harder and more expensive. Write the artwork and tooling ownership clause into the supplier agreement so that the print files, dies, screens and any physical tooling remain your property and are returned or destroyed on exit — a clause on this is one of the most commonly negotiated terms and is worth agreeing early rather than at the end. And control distribution tightly in the first year: if you sell to a retailer, and that retailer sells to another retailer, and that one sells online under a different listing, your brand is now associated with a product you did not make and did not warrant. Most private label programmes that lose control of their brand lose it through the second tier of distribution in the first eighteen months rather than through copying.
