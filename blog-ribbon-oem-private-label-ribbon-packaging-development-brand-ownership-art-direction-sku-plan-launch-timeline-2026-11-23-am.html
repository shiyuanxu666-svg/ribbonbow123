Private label ribbon is a packaging project, not a trim purchase. The ribbon itself is often the simplest part of the brief. What makes a private label programme difficult is everything that surrounds the ribbon: the printed packaging, the colour story across a range, the barcode that has to match your retail system, the legal copy that differs by market, and the launch window that will not move. Branded ribbon that arrives on a plain polybag and is over-wrapped by your own packaging house is a one-week job. Branded ribbon that arrives in your own printed retail pack, on your own core, with your own barcode, is a ten-week job. The difference is entirely in the packaging layer, and it is worth understanding before you commit to a launch date.

A factory that makes ribbons for 50 countries will tell you that most of what arrives in their sample room is a generic product with a colour change. The mill knows the construction, the width, the dye recipe and the tolerance. What it does not know is who the ribbon is for, how it sits on a shelf next to three other products in your range, and what the back of the pack has to say in two languages.

This guide is written for the brand owner or category manager who has been given a budget to launch a branded trim range and is trying to work out what to ask a supplier for. It covers what private label development actually involves, how to write a brief that produces a usable first round of samples, how packaging structure changes the economics, how many SKUs to launch with, how to make sure you own the artwork, and how the calendar actually works.

The Generic Ribbon Problem

There is a specific commercial dynamic that sits underneath most private label ribbon programmes, and naming it early saves a lot of money.

When a brand buys a generic satin ribbon from a mill at trade price, the mill has no commercial incentive to help with anything beyond the ribbon itself. The ribbon is a commodity in the eyes of the factory - it comes off the same loom as the ribbon another buyer ordered last month, in a colour from a shared dye book. That is fine, and it is also the cheapest possible entry point.

Private label changes the relationship, because it asks the factory to carry something that is specific to you: a printed core, a branded polybag, a custom colour that nobody else orders, a width nobody else buys in volume. Once the mill holds your specific colour and your specific packaging components, your programme has a switching cost in both directions. You have made them dependent on your volume, and they have become dependent on your artwork and your colour approvals.

That dependency cuts both ways, and experienced buyers understand it early rather than discovering it later. It is worth noting three practical consequences.

  • The quoted price for a private label order is not the generic price plus decoration. Private label carries setup costs that a generic order does not: screen or plate setup for a printed core, a polybag print run, sometimes a custom master carton print, and a colour development that has to be repeated if the recipe is retired. These are one-off or amortised costs, and they change the shape of the order. A 500-metre minimum on a generic ribbon and a 500-metre minimum on a fully branded programme are not the same commercial proposition.
  • The factory has a genuine interest in your repeat volume, and will invest accordingly. A mill that is holding your colour and your print plates has a strong reason to help you succeed and reorder. This is the commercial argument for choosing a factory with real capacity and a real packaging line rather than the lowest quoted unit price on a trading platform.
  • The relationship changes the negotiation surface. You are no longer negotiating per metre. You are negotiating a programme: setup cost, unit price at each volume tier, artwork and file retention, colour retention, lead time and reorder terms. That is a better conversation and a more durable one, but it needs to be had explicitly.

None of this means a trading platform is the wrong route. For a small brand buying a single generic colour in a single width to over-wrap itself, a trading platform is efficient and sensible. It becomes the wrong route once you need a branded pack, a custom colour held for you, or a consistent repeat of the same shade across several SKUs.

What Private Label Ribbon Development Actually Involves

Private label development for ribbon has five distinct workstreams. Buyers frequently plan for two of them - the ribbon and the print - and are surprised by the other three. It helps to see them laid out.

The product layer

Material family, construction, width, weight, finish and decoration method. This is the layer the factory controls most directly, and it is usually where the sample round is spent. Colour sits here too, though it behaves differently from the rest because colour has to be approved against something physical and because a colour held for your account is a colour the mill has to keep alive.

The artwork layer

Your brand mark on a core or a card, the range name, the fibre and care content, the country of origin, the material claims, the care instructions, the barcode, the EAN or UPC, the importer's address if you are the importer of record in that market, and any regulated copy. This layer has its own approval cycle, and it is the layer where legal and compliance review usually lands.

The packaging layer

The physical structure: what the ribbon is wound on, what it is wrapped in, what the consumer sees when they pick it up, and how the pack survives a retail environment. A pack has to stack, hang, survive a delivery, and display. This layer also determines how much space the product occupies, which affects freight and shelf productivity - a topic that is rarely considered at development stage and always considered later.

The data layer

Barcodes, SKU codes, colour references, size references, and the mapping between all of those and your retail or e-commerce systems. This layer is unglamorous and it is where a lot of launch schedules are lost, because it depends on systems on your side that are not part of the physical supply chain and therefore are not on anyone's critical path until they are.

The logistics layer

Master carton configuration, carton labels, how many retail packs per carton, the gross and net weight, the carton dimensions, and the relationship between the retail pack and the carton. This layer determines the freight cost per unit and whether your distribution centre can receive the delivery without a manual step.

When all five layers are planned together, private label ribbon is a well-understood project with a predictable timeline. When four are planned and one is not, that layer becomes the critical path and the launch moves.

The Development Brief and What It Must Contain

The single largest determinant of how long a private label programme takes is the quality of the brief that starts it. A vague brief produces a long sample round and a lot of rework; a precise brief produces two rounds at most.

A workable development brief for ribbon private label contains the following. Each of these is a field that a factory can act on without asking a follow-up question.

Brief fieldWhat it must specify
ApplicationWhat the ribbon is used for. Gift packaging, apparel trim, hair accessories, home fragrance, pet accessories, wedding. The application constrains performance, width and decoration method more than any other single field.
Material directionFamily and construction rather than only fibre. "Polyester satin, 100 percent polyester filament" constrains less than "a soft matte satin that drapes without body, for gift boxes that will be ribbon-tied rather than glued". Give both a preferred and an acceptable alternative.
WidthNominal width and tolerance, and whether the tolerance applies to the woven or the finished cut width.
ColourReference system and target value, not a colour name. A Pantone code, a physical swatch, or an existing production standard the factory can match against. Colour names are not reproducible across mills.
DecorationWhich of your marks appears on the ribbon itself and which appear only on the packaging. A woven jacquard brand mark and a printed polybag mark are entirely different cost and lead-time commitments.
Retail pack structureCore size and type, whether the ribbon is wound on a card, the polybag format, whether the pack is header-hung, and the language count on the pack.
Copy requirementsThe exact text that must appear on the pack, in which languages, and where each item sits - brand mark, range name, size, fibre content, care, origin, importer address.
Data requirementsBarcode symbology, whether you will supply the barcode artwork or need it generated, and any code that ties the pack to your internal SKU.
Volume planFirst order quantity per SKU, expected reorder cadence, and the volume you would reach in twelve months if the line performs. This is what allows the factory to quote setup amortisation honestly.
Market and complianceDestination countries, and whether any of them requires specific labelling, traceability or recycled-content disclosure.
Budget positionTarget landed cost per retail pack, and the target unit cost per metre. A factory cannot propose alternatives against a budget it has not seen.

Two fields in that table are routinely left out and consistently cause problems. Colour reference system - if the brief says "dusty rose" without a reference, the mill will approve whatever comes back from the first dye house run that looks dusty rose to them, and you will spend a round correcting it. Volume plan - without a twelve-month horizon, the factory quotes the setup at the first-order quantity, which makes your first order look expensive and makes the reorder look like a windfall. Give the horizon and the amortisation becomes visible from the start.

Art Direction: Working With Your Factory Designer

Factory design support is one of the most underused advantages of working with a manufacturer that has a design room. Most Chinese trim and ribbon factories of any real size keep at least one graphic designer and often a print technician in-house, because the packaging artwork is part of every order and has to be adapted constantly. Used well, this compresses the artwork cycle dramatically. Used badly, it produces artwork that satisfies the printer and not the brand.

The distinction is about who directs. A factory designer works best when they are given a design brief with clear constraints and a reference point, not when they are asked to "make something nice".

A workable factory-design brief specifies the artwork's physical canvas before it is drawn. For a printed polybag, that means the flat dimensions of the bag, the print area excluding the seal and the gusset, the number of colours available, and whether the print is on both sides or one. For a printed core card, it means the card dimensions, the ribbon winding direction relative to the print, and how much of the print is visible once wound - which is often much less than the designer assumes, and which is the single most common reason a beautiful core card looks cheap in the finished pack.

Two practical points on working with a factory designer.

Ask for a print-ready proof, not a screen mock-up.A mock-up on a laptop shows colour and layout. A print-ready proof with the actual print process and the actual substrate shows what will happen when the mark meets a polypropylene bag or a coated card - which can shift in tone, lose fine detail, and behave differently on a textured surface. The proof is the artefact that ends the approval cycle.

Get the artwork templates before you design. Ask for the factory's existing template files for the pack component you intend to use, with their exact die lines, safe areas and print specifications in them. Designing to the actual template is faster than designing a concept and then rebuilding it to fit, and it eliminates the class of change requests that come back as "the artwork exceeds the bleed area".

Where the brand has an identity that must be protected - a retail brand with a design team and a strict identity guide - the artwork is usually produced by the brand and handed over as a print-ready file. That is also the better route for brand protection, and it is discussed under artwork ownership below.

Packaging Structure: Roll, Spool, Card or Pouch

The packaging structure decision is a commercial decision as much as a design one, because the pack determines the unit cost, the freight cost and the shelf behaviour. These are the structures that matter in ribbon.

Plain polybag with a printed label or sticker. The cheapest option and entirely legitimate for a basic product. The label is applied by hand or by the brand's own packer. Choose this when the pack will be over-wrapped by your own gift packaging, or when the ribbon is a component rather than a product in its own right.

Printed polybag, heat-sealed. The standard for a branded ribbon sold at retail. The bag carries the brand mark, the range name, the size, the fibre content and the care instructions. It is a modest print run and a fast process. Choose this when the ribbon is a visible part of a gift product but is not sold on its own.

Core card, printed, with the ribbon wound onto the card. The ribbon is wound onto a printed card rather than a plain core, which makes the product legible on shelf without any separate packaging. This is a strong choice for craft, florist and gift retail where the customer buys the ribbon as a discrete product. It costs more than a polybag and it constrains the ribbon length per pack, because the card has a diameter.

Header-hung card with a pouch or band. The retail-ready format for a ribbon sold on its own in a craft or department store. Highest development cost of the four because the card, the punch hole, the hang tab and the film all have to work together, and the hanging hardware has to match the store's fixture standard.

Spool or reel for a wider or heavier ribbon. Relevant when the width exceeds about 38mm or the ribbon is heavy enough that a core becomes unstable. The spool changes freight efficiency significantly - a reel of ribbon is a rigid, dense object that packs into a carton efficiently, where a soft polybagged roll does not.

Three cost consequences of the structure choice are worth modelling before you commit.

  • Unit cost per metre rises with the pack. A branded core card adds the card, the print and the winding operation to the metre price. At a 500-metre minimum for a colour, that setup can exceed the value of the ribbon itself, which is why structure decisions are usually made across a range rather than per SKU.
  • Freight cost per retail pack depends on the pack's volume, not the ribbon's weight. A 20-metre retail pack of ribbon weighs almost nothing; what it costs to ship is the space it occupies. A lightweight pack that is voluminous is expensive to ship and cheap to make. This is the calculation that makes reel formats attractive for wide ribbon and card formats attractive for narrow ribbon.
  • The pack has a shelf life in the buyer's warehouse. A complex pack with a hang tab and a film has a longer development cycle, which means a larger initial commitment and a longer period before you know how it performs. For a first range, this is a real risk to weigh.

SKU Rationalisation: How Many Ribbons a Brand Should Launch With

The most common private label ribbon mistake is launching too many SKUs. It feels productive to show a retailer twelve colours, but each SKU carries a setup cost, a minimum order quantity, a stock position and a forecast obligation - and the cost falls on the factory as well as on you.

Three mechanisms make wide ranges expensive, and it is worth being precise about each.

Setup costs multiply, they do not scale. A printed polybag design per colourway, a dye recipe per colourway, a minimum of 500 metres per colourway. A range of ten colours at 500 metres is 5,000 metres of inventory committed before a single metre is sold, and ten setup charges amortised across five hundred metres each. A range of four colours at 500 metres commits 2,000 metres and four setup charges.

Colour needs a real demand signal. A private label colour is a forecast, and forecasting a colour is harder than forecasting a width. Width can be derived from a known product format. A colour is a style decision, and style decisions are made by customers, not by buyers. This is why a private label range built around two or three proven neutrals and one or two accent colours is a much safer construction than a broad rainbow range.

Each SKU is a separate forecast obligation with your customer. A buyer who takes eight colours from you has eight things to replenish, and a replenishment on a slow colour is a write-off. A buyer who takes three has three. Fewer SKUs is frequently the stronger commercial position with the retailer, not the weaker one.

A workable first-range structure for a brand entering private label ribbon is three to five SKUs, structured as: two core neutrals that carry the bulk of volume and pair with the widest range of gift packaging; one or two accent colours that give the range a point of view and carry higher margin; and one hero SKU with the highest specification, used for the brand's own photography and merchandising. If you need a sixth colour, wait until the first reorder cycle and add it against real data rather than pre-launch data.

One further note on the relationship between range width and factory choice. A mill that runs a narrow range and keeps that range in stock will let you reorder a colour in days. A mill that dyes to order will require a lead time on every reorder regardless of volume. This is a real operational difference and it should be part of the supplier evaluation, not discovered in your second quarter.

Artwork Ownership and Brand Protection

This is the item that most often gets handled informally, and it is the one with the longest tail of risk. If your name is on the pack, the pack is your brand asset, and you need to know where the master files are, who can edit them, and what happens to them if the relationship ends.

Four questions to settle, in writing, before the first print.

  • Who creates the master artwork file? If your own designer creates it and the factory prints it, you own it outright and the factory holds a production file for the purposes of reproducing your order. If the factory creates it, ownership should still be assigned to you in writing as part of the artwork quotation - this is the norm in the industry and it should be stated, not assumed.
  • Who holds the editable master and in what format? An editable layered file, not a flattened PDF. A PDF is a print instruction, not a master. Ask where the master lives and get a copy. If the factory holds it and the factory's system is not exported on request, you have outsourced the control of your own identity to somebody else's file server.
  • Who holds the print plates, screens and printing setup? This is a manufacturing asset, not a brand asset, and you generally do not own it. What you should own is the right to retrieve the artwork and the right to have the setup reproduced or transferred. Ask what it costs to reissue the setup if you ever move to a different factory - because at some point a factory change is likely, and an unrecoverable setup cost is a leverage problem for the buyer.
  • How long is the artwork retained, and at what cost to reissue? Ask specifically about both the digital file and the physical components, and ask how long each is kept. A factory that retires a customer's setup after eighteen months of inactivity and charges a reissue fee has effectively told you that you should not allow a long gap between orders.

On the factory side, be equally clear with your own team. The factory needs the artwork file, the brand mark in vector format, the copy in the exact final wording, and the barcode. It needs these to be reproducible months later from a single controlled source, because a rollout of one artwork file across a range of SKUs is where a small typographic error propagates into a full production run and reaches the shelf.

The Private Label Development Timeline

Below is a realistic calendar for a brand launching its own branded ribbon pack, with no new construction and a print decoration only. It assumes the factory has the pack component in production and that artwork is brand-supplied.

PhaseDurationWhat happens
Brief and supplier shortlist1 weekBrief issued, three to five factories approached, technical questions answered, samples requested from each.
Samples and colour work2 weeksConstruction samples received and compared, colour swatches dyed and returned, width and hand-feel assessed.
Specification lock1 weekConstruction, width, tolerance, colour reference, decoration method and packing format agreed in writing and acknowledged.
Artwork and packaging development2 to 3 weeksBrand artwork adapted to the pack template, print-ready proof reviewed and approved, copy and legal content signed off, barcode issued.
Pre-production sample1 to 2 weeksA packed pre-production unit produced with the final artwork and final packing, then reviewed as a physical pack rather than as a ribbon sample.
Production3 to 5 weeksYarn and dyeing, weaving or the selected construction, decoration, packaging, packing. Sequence depends on whether your colour is held or dyed to order.
Quality control and dispatch1 weekInspection against the approved standard, carton packing, loading.
Freight to destination3 to 6 weeksSea freight, or air at cost. At this point the product is complete and the calendar is out of your hands.

Two things to read out of that table. First, the development work before production - brief through pre-production sample - is seven to nine weeks and is entirely within your control, because it is decisions and artwork rather than manufacturing. That is where a launch date is actually won or lost. Second, from purchase order to arrival is eight to twelve weeks, and only the last two of those are freight. If the retailer wants the product on shelf by a given date, work backwards from the arrival date, not the production date, and build in a buffer for the arrival rather than assuming it.

One method to compress the schedule meaningfully: run the artwork development and the ribbon sample work in parallel rather than in series. The two do not depend on each other until the pre-production sample. Running them in series - sample the ribbon, then develop the pack for that ribbon - adds two to three weeks for no reason. The ribbon construction and the pack structure are independent decisions that meet at the pre-production sample.

A second method: order the packaging components as a separate line in the same production order rather than in a later one. Polybag and card suppliers frequently have shorter lead times than a dyeing and weaving cycle, so ordering them together with the ribbon usually costs nothing and removes two to three weeks of waiting at the end.

The Programme That Works

The private label programmes that work are not the ones with the most elaborate packaging. They are the ones where the brand decided, early and in writing: what the ribbon is for, what the colour reference is, what goes on the pack, how many colours are launching, who owns the artwork, and what the timeline is. Everything else - the sample round, the pricing, the launch - follows from those decisions.

And the single most useful thing a brand can do in the first conversation with a factory is to ask the factory what it has done before that is structurally similar. A factory that has launched private label ribbon packs for other brands can tell you, in a few minutes, which pack structure worked for which application, what the realistic timeline was, and where the previous client got it wrong. That institutional knowledge is worth more than any price list, and it is available to anyone who asks.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How long does it take to develop a private label ribbon pack for retail?

For a brand-new programme with a print decoration and a brand-supplied artwork, a realistic timeline is seven to nine weeks of development work before production begins - brief and supplier shortlist, samples and colour work, specification lock, artwork and packaging development, and a pre-production packed sample - followed by three to five weeks of production and three to six weeks of freight. Running the ribbon sample work and the artwork development in parallel rather than in series typically saves two to three weeks, because the two streams only meet at the pre-production sample.

2. Should the factory design our packaging or should our own designer?

It depends on whether your brand has an identity that must be protected. If you have an in-house designer and a strict identity guide, have them produce the artwork against the factory's templates and give the factory a print-ready file - that keeps control of the master with you and avoids reworking artwork that does not meet the brand system. If you are launching a range without an established pack design, using the factory's design room is faster and usually cheaper, and you should still ask for ownership of the master artwork file in writing as part of the quotation.

3. How many colours should we launch a private label ribbon range with?

Three to five SKUs is the safest starting structure for a first range: two core neutrals that carry the volume and pair with the widest range of gift packaging, one or two accent colours for margin and point of view, and one hero SKU for photography and merchandising. Each additional colour carries a dye recipe, a setup charge, a 500-metre minimum and a separate forecast obligation, and colour is harder to forecast than width because it is a style decision rather than a derived specification. Add colours against reorder-cycle data rather than pre-launch data.

4. Do we own the packaging artwork and the print setup?

You should own the master artwork file and have that ownership stated in writing, whether your own team or the factory created it. You generally do not own the physical print plates or screens - those are manufacturing assets - but you should have the right to retrieve the artwork and to have the setup reproduced or transferred at a stated cost. Also settle how long the digital file and the physical setup are retained, and what reissuing the setup costs, because a factory that retires a setup after a long gap between orders is telling you not to allow one.

5. What is the minimum order quantity for branded ribbon packaging?

Ribbon minimums typically start at 500 metres per colourway for standard constructions, with larger minimums where a custom construction or a specific decoration method is involved. Branded packaging adds its own minimum on top - the print run for the polybag, card or reel is a separate setup with its own quantity - so the effective commitment per SKU is higher than the ribbon minimum suggests. Ask for the minimum broken down by component, and ask what the print run minimum is separately, because a printed component minimum can exceed the ribbon minimum on a short run.

6. Should the ribbon be wound on a core, a card or a spool?

The structure should follow how the product is sold and where it is used. A printed polybag suits a ribbon that is part of a gift product and will be over-wrapped. A printed core card suits a narrow ribbon sold on its own in craft and gift retail. A header-hung card suits a product bought as a discrete item in a department store, at the highest development cost. A reel suits ribbon wider than about 38mm or heavy enough that a core becomes unstable, and reels are far more freight-efficient. Also check the effect on freight: a lightweight retail pack costs to ship by the space it occupies, not by its weight.

7. Can we use our existing brand colours, and will the factory hold the dye recipe?

You can use your brand colours, but they must be expressed in a reproducible reference system - a Pantone code, a physical swatch, or an existing production standard the mill can match against - because colour names alone are not reproducible across mills. On holding the recipe, the answer depends on the volume pattern. A factory that dyes to order will need a lead time on every reorder no matter how small the quantity. A factory that runs a narrow stocked range will hold colours and let you reorder quickly, but you should ask explicitly how long a held colour is retained and what the re-development cost is after a period of inactivity.
