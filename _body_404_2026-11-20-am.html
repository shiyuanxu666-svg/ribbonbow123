What a Packaging Line Review Actually Is

A ribbon packaging line is not a photograph on a supplier website. It is a set of machines, a set of people, and a set of order books that somebody else is competing for. Before you award a private label ribbon programme to any factory, you need evidence that the line as configured today can hold your standard six months from now - not a slide describing what the line could do if nobody else booked the same week.

The line review is the point in a sourcing programme where you stop comparing quotations and start examining operations. It sits between two different kinds of decision. On one side is the shortlist decision, which is made on paper: capability claims, certifications, price, lead time. On the other side is the award decision, which commits your brand to a factory's output for a season or a year. The line review exists to check whether the paper matches the floor.

It is worth being precise about what a line review is not. It is not an audit in the regulatory sense. It is not a factory visit taken as a favour. And it is not a quality inspection - you are not looking for defects in the ribbon in front of you, you are looking for the system that prevents those defects from reaching you at volume. What you are assessing is the operating capability of a specific production line under conditions that resemble your own programme.

That last phrase matters more than it sounds. A line configured for short runs of plain ribbon is not a line configured for decorated ribbon in twenty-two colourways. A line that runs weaving is not the same asset as a line that runs printing and finishing. When a supplier presents a capability that spans both, the honest question is which of the two the priority actually sits on when both are running, and who decides when they conflict.

Before You Travel: What to Ask in Writing

The most productive part of a line review happens before you arrive, in the form of documents and evidence you request in advance. A supplier who treats a document request seriously is telling you something about how they will treat a quality complaint later. Use that as a filter.

Send a written request at least two weeks ahead, and ask for the following. Not as a courtesy, but as a condition of the visit proceeding on schedule.

  • Production loading record for the previous eight weeks, showing machine count, scheduled hours, actual hours run and output by construction. This is the single most useful document in the file. It converts a capacity claim into a measurable fact.
  • Machine list with make, model, year and working width range, plus the current planned maintenance schedule for each. Machines that are listed but visibly idle or under repair tell you something the document does not.
  • Three pre-shipment inspection reports from the last six months, in their original form, including the defect classification summary.
  • Two corrective action records - one closed, one in progress if possible. A factory with no corrective action records over six months is either exceptionally good or not recording.
  • Current certification set with issue and expiry dates and the scope of each certificate, not just a list of logos.
  • Colour laboratory capability: how many lab dips are included in the development fee, how long each takes, and what a spectrophotometric report looks like from this factory.

Ask one question that most buyers never ask: which of these documents are you able to show a retailer auditor without notice? The answer tells you whether the documentation system is routine or whether it exists for the buyer who is asking today. If a factory cannot answer that question quickly, the paperwork you are about to see was prepared for you.

Capability Evidence: Machines, People, Method

Capability evidence has three components, and a factory can score well on one while failing on another. Look for all three.

Machines

Ask what the machine does, not what it is called. A jacquard loom that can produce a 32mm wide fabric is irrelevant if your programme needs 40mm. Ask for working width, speed under your construction, and what happens to output when a stoppage of two hours occurs mid-roll. Then walk the line and check the reality against the list: are machines clean, are they running, are there obvious workarounds taped onto machine frames? Machines that are heavily modified are not necessarily a problem, but you should know which machines are standard and which are the workhorse that took the modifications.

People

Ask who sets up the line, who approves the first metres, and who would be on the line for your programme. Key operator turnover is a serious risk in ribbon manufacturing because the setup knowledge for a specific construction lives partly in the person. If the answer is that the line is set up by whichever shift leader is present, ask to see the written setup sheet. If there is no written setup sheet, that knowledge is undocumented and your first program change will be an experiment.

Method

The strongest signal of method is whether the factory has written standards for the things that go wrong. Ask what the standard is for selvedge condition, what the acceptable tolerance is on ribbon width, what the reworked roll threshold is. If the answer is that the operator judges it by eye and by experience, you should expect to spend the first year of the relationship building a specification from scratch. If there is a written document with numbers in it, you can start your programme from a much shorter distance.

Quality Records That Prove the Line Runs Clean

Physical capability tells you what the factory can do when things go right. Quality records tell you what happens when things do not, which is where your programme will actually live.

Work through the records in this order, because each one depends on the previous.

Incoming inspection records. These show whether the factory inspects its own incoming yarn and raw material. Most factories do some form of incoming check, but many do it informally. Ask what happens when an incoming yarn lot fails a colour or count check - does it get quarantined, does production stop, is there a record? A factory with no incoming record trail has no way of containing a problem that originates upstream of it.

In-process records. These show whether defects are caught during the run or at the end of it. Ask specifically about the point in the process at which a roll is judged. If the judgement happens at the packing stage, every defective metre has already consumed yarn, loom time and your colour allocation. Look for a check that happens at the loom or immediately after finishing, before the roll is presented for packing.

Pre-shipment inspection records. These are the records you will receive on every order, so evaluate them as documents you must act on. Two questions: what is the sampling standard, and is the report structured so that a buyer can compare two shipments? A report that only says pass or fail gives you nothing to escalate with. A report that lists defect types and quantities gives you a trend line across orders and lets you identify a degradation before it becomes a rejection.

Corrective action records. Ask to see one in full. The record should identify what happened, what the root cause was, what was changed, and how the change was verified. What you are really testing is whether the factory closes loops or whether findings disappear. Two examples of records that should worry you: the corrective action that says "operator retrained" with no verification step, and the corrective action where the root cause is "material quality" with no upstream action attached, which means the same problem will return with the next delivery.

Capacity and the Booking Reality

Capacity is where line reviews most often produce a pleasant surprise that turns into a delivery problem later. The stated capacity is the theoretical ceiling: the sum of what every machine could produce if every machine ran every scheduled hour. Actual capacity is a different number, and it is the one your delivery dates depend on.

Three comparisons make the difference visible.

  • Theoretical against actual. Divide the weekly output in the loading record by the number of machines in the machine list. A plant running at sixty to seventy per cent of theoretical is normal and healthy. A plant running at ninety per cent is running nothing but your programme, and a plant running below forty per cent is either under-loaded or losing time in a way worth understanding.
  • Booked against available. Ask what the booking position looks like in your delivery window. If the factory is already fully booked for the period you need, your programme will be prioritised against other customers. Ask how priority is decided when two programmes overlap, and whether your volume level would earn that priority under the factory's own rules.
  • Peak against average. Ask what the plant looks like eight weeks before a major retail event. If the answer is that the plant runs at full capacity year-round, the answer is not credible, and you should ask what the number looks like in a normal month to get a realistic baseline.

Two questions carry disproportionate weight here. First: what is the recovery plan if a machine fails with a two-week delivery date pending? Second: what is the smallest quantity the factory will run for you in a recovery scenario? A factory that can flex a small emergency run is worth a great deal at peak, because it turns a lost delivery into a delayed delivery. A factory that requires full order quantities to start anything gives you no lever at the moment you need one.

Reading the Cost Structure Behind the Line

The line review is a good moment to test whether the price you have been quoted reflects how the line actually runs. Quotations are built from assumptions about volume, mix and changeover frequency. If the factory's real operating pattern differs from your assumption, the price is not the price you will pay.

Ask four questions, each tied to something you saw on the floor.

What does the setup cost consist of? Ask what is included in the setup fee: screen or plate preparation, colour matching time, trial run metres, and the operator hours for changeover. If the changeover you watched took thirty-five minutes and your programme involves twelve colourways per season, the setup hours are a material cost driver that a single "setup charge" line may hide.

What is the material yield assumption? Ask what the factory assumes about waste - in the weave, in the dye process, in the trimming, in the rejection rate at inspection. This number moves the unit price more than any other single assumption, and it is the one most often left implicit.

What does the volume curve look like? Ask what volume the quoted price assumes and what happens to the price at half that volume. If the price rises sharply at lower volume, you need to know before you commit to a smaller first order, because the higher rate is what you will pay and it may make the programme unviable.

What is included that is not in the quotation? Ask which costs are excluded: testing, certification, packaging development, samples, tooling ownership, freight beyond the agreed Incoterm. Any of these can absorb a meaningful share of the margin you built into the business case, and finding them on the floor is far better than finding them on the first invoice.

Scoring the Review Without Bias

The scorecard for a line review should be short enough to complete during the visit and specific enough to differentiate between suppliers. Six dimensions, weighted, with written evidence attached to each score, works well in practice.

  • Process capability - can the line run your construction and decoration at your volumes, with evidence rather than assertion.
  • Quality system maturity - written standards, in-process control, records that support a trend rather than a snapshot.
  • Capacity realism - the gap between stated and actual output, and the flexibility available in your window.
  • People and method continuity - setup knowledge documented, key operator retention, training in place.
  • Cost transparency - whether the price can be decomposed and the assumptions stated.
  • Communication and responsiveness - how the factory behaves when it is not being audited, which you will learn from the staff you talk to, not from the management briefing.

Score each dimension 1 to 5 against the evidence you collected, and attach the document, photograph or measurement that justifies each number. The attachment is what makes the score useful six months later when someone asks why this factory was chosen. A score without evidence is an opinion, and opinions do not survive a price increase negotiation.

Six Findings That Should Stop a Line Review

Some findings are strong enough that they should override a good overall score rather than sit in it.

  1. The loading record cannot be produced. If the factory will not show you eight weeks of loading data, capacity is a claim, and you should plan your lead times on the conservative reading of their stated capacity.
  2. Clean lines but no records. A tidy floor with no written standards or inspection records is a presentation, not a system. It may still work at low volume; it will not reliably work at your volume.
  3. Correction without verification. If the corrective action you review ends at "retrained" or "increased inspection" with no check that the problem stopped, the factory has a documented habit of closing findings without solving them.
  4. Key operator knowledge is undocumented and the operator is new. If the person who set up your construction last year has left and there is no setup sheet, your next order is their first attempt with a new team.
  5. The quote cannot be decomposed. A single all-in price with no build-up, no volume assumption and no stated exclusions will drift, and the drift will arrive in your favour or against you without warning.
  6. They will not let you see the rejected material. A factory that segregates rework and rejects visibly is showing you its quality system. A factory where you cannot see a scrap pile is telling you the rejects are not being controlled.

None of these findings requires a supplier to fail a certification requirement. They are the things that separate a factory that will absorb a private label programme from one that will quietly cause problems for two years.
