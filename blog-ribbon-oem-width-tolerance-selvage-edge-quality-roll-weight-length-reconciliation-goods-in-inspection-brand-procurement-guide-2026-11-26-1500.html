Ribbon Width Tolerance and Roll Length Reconciliation

Published: November 26, 2026 (15:00)  |  By Smith Ribbon  |  B2B Ribbon Specification and Goods-In Inspection
The three numbers every ribbon order should carry: nominal width in millimetres with an agreed tolerance, the accepted selvage and edge condition, and the basis on which delivered quantity is reconciled — net metres per roll, or net weight at a stated reference condition. An order that carries only a width and a metre count carries no measurement standard, and therefore no remedy.

Most ribbon orders are specified with two numbers: a width and a quantity. Everything else is implied. The ribbon will be 38 millimetres wide, and there will be 5,000 metres of it. Neither number, on its own, tells a factory what to produce or a warehouse what to accept, and the gap between what was specified and what was received is where a large share of the disputes in this industry actually live. A 38 millimetre ribbon that measures 36.4 millimetres will pass a casual visual inspection and still break an assembly that depends on that ribbon seating correctly in a 38 millimetre channel. A roll that is nominally 100 metres and actually contains 94 metres will reconcile against an order sheet if the sheet does not say how the count is established, and the shortfall will be discovered downstream, usually at the worst possible moment.

This is not a complaint about ribbon mills. It is a description of how a commercial specification works. The purpose of this guide is to make the specification measurable, so that both sides are working from the same numbers, and so that an inspection outcome can be compared against a pre-agreed acceptance rule rather than argued about after the goods have arrived.

Nominal Width Is Not a Measurement

A nominal width is a reference point, not a tolerance band and not a measurement method. The word "nominal" acknowledges that no ribbon comes out of a loom exactly on a figure, and that both parties expect some variation. What it does not do, on its own, is establish what variation is acceptable.

Three separate things must be fixed before a width specification is enforceable, and most orders that generate disputes are missing one or more of them:

  • The nominal width, in millimetres, with the relevant construction in mind. A woven grosgrain and a woven satin of the same nominal width behave differently because their structures differ, and a printed ribbon adds a further dimension once the print and the finishing process are taken into account.
  • The tolerance band, stated as a plus or minus figure or as an explicit statement that the width must fall within a range. This is a commercial decision, not a technical one, and it belongs in the purchase order.
  • The measurement method, including where along the ribbon the measurement is taken, how many points are measured, and the condition of the ribbon at the moment of measurement.

That third item is the one most often left out, and it is the one that decides whether a dispute can be settled. Ribbon is a woven textile and it relaxes. A width taken on ribbon that has just come off a roll under tension may not be the width the same ribbon measures a week later, after it has been stored and has equilibrated with the ambient conditions. A buyer who measures cold, flat, relaxed ribbon and a mill that measures under a defined tension will consistently get different numbers from the same material, and both will be right.

The practical resolution is to agree a method in writing and keep using it. In our own inspection practice we measure relaxed ribbon, laid flat without tension, at points taken at regular intervals across the roll length rather than at a single position, and we state that method on the purchase order and on the inspection record. A buyer who adopts the same method on their own goods-in bench is comparing like with like, which is the whole purpose.

How Width Tolerance Is Actually Set and Enforced

The tolerance band a mill will work to is shaped by the material, the construction and the loom. Woven fabrics are held to width by the reed and by the warp tension, and those two mechanisms together define the practical band. A buyer who asks for a tolerance tighter than the construction can hold will receive either a longer lead time at a higher price, or a ribbon that meets the tolerance on the rolls the mill likes and misses it on the rest.

This is worth stating plainly because it is a recurring source of disappointment on both sides. The mills that quote tight tolerances are not being difficult; they are usually working to a narrow band because their process supports it. The mills that quote wide bands are not being careless; they are being honest about the process.

What matters is that the tolerance is matched to the use. A ribbon used as decorative trim on a gift bag, where a point of variation is invisible, does not need the same band as a ribbon that has to feed a moulding machine or sit in a rigid channel. Establish the end use first, then ask what the mill will commit to, and check whether the mill's answer is a number or a conversation. A confident mill will give you a band and a measurement method. A hesitant answer usually means the number will be negotiated later, at your cost.

A second point on enforcement: tolerance applies per roll and per inspection point, and the acceptance rule has to say which. If your rule is "no roll outside tolerance", a single out-of-band measurement on a 500-metre roll fails the roll. If your rule is "a roll passes if its average is within tolerance and no more than a stated proportion of points fall outside", the same roll may pass comfortably. Both rules are legitimate, but they are not interchangeable, and choosing between them is a commercial decision that should be made before the shipment arrives, not during the inspection.

Selvage and Edge Quality: Where Ribbon Ends First

Width is the specification everyone measures and the edge is the one that causes the most downstream disruption. A ribbon with a correct width and a poor selvage will still cause problems, because the selvage is what the ribbon is held by, sewn through, or folded over at the point of use.

A clean selvage means the edge holds together under ordinary handling. In practice that means no loose weft ends standing proud of the edge, no thread dropouts, no fraying that extends along the roll, and no laddering where the edge structure has begun to unravel in a way that will progress with handling. On a satin ribbon, the characteristic failure is a fine fuzzy edge that grows with handling. On a grosgrain, it is the weft ends pulling out of the edge binding. On a printed ribbon, it is compounded by the fact that printing and finishing can stiffen the edge and make it more brittle than the base cloth.

The reason to specify the edge by description and illustration rather than by standard number is practical. Descriptive acceptance criteria with photographs attached to the purchase order are unambiguous, easy to apply at goods-in, and easy to appeal. Standard references are ambiguous in the way that matters here: they describe a test method or a class of material, and the judgement still has to be made by a person against something. If you attach a photograph of an accepted edge and a photograph of a rejected edge to the order, the goods-in decision becomes a comparison rather than an argument, and it can be made by a quality assistant rather than an engineer.

One practical addition that pays for itself: specify the edge condition in the same document as the width tolerance, and require the mill to include edge condition on the inspection report it accompanies with the shipment. Edge problems are frequently visible at the mill and only visible at the customer if nobody is looking. Making the mill look is the cheapest fix.

Statistical Sampling for Dimensional Inspection

Nobody measures every metre of every roll in a 5,000-metre order, and any inspection regime that claims to is either impractical or dishonest. What matters is that the sampling plan is defined before the goods arrive, and that the acceptance rule follows from the sample.

The standard structure has three parts: the sample size, the measurement points per roll, and the decision rule that connects them. A workable version for a ribbon order is to select rolls across the shipment rather than from one end of it, to take measurement points at a stated interval along the length of each sampled roll rather than at a single point, and to define in advance what the results mean. The reason for spreading the sample across the shipment is straightforward: a production run has a chronology, and a sample drawn from the beginning tells you about the beginning.

The measurement points matter as much as the roll selection. Width can drift along a roll as the loom runs, and a single reading taken near the core tells you very little about the outer windings, where the conditions are usually most representative of the material a customer will actually use. Taking points at a defined interval and recording them individually, rather than averaging them into a single number on the spot, preserves information you may need later.

For buyers without a dedicated quality function, the workable version is simpler: define the sample size, state the interval, record the individual readings, and write down the decision rule in the purchase order. It does not need to be sophisticated to be effective. What it must not be is undefined, because undefined sampling is what turns a straightforward inspection into a dispute.

Roll Length Reconciliation: Metres, Weight and the Order

Reconciliation is the arithmetic of confirming that what arrived is what was ordered, and it has three inputs: the order quantity, the delivery note, and a measurement that establishes the actual quantity. The third input is where the confusion usually sits, because a roll of ribbon does not carry its length on the label in any universally reliable form.

Counting metres per roll is the straightforward method and the one we use by default. The question is how the count is established: by the mill as the roll is wound, by a mechanical counter on the winding machine, or by the receiving team after arrival. Each has a different accuracy, and the mill's own count is generally the most reliable because it is taken at the point where the metre counter is most accurate. Where a roll is counted at the mill and the customer recounts at goods-in, a small difference is normal and should be expected as part of the agreement rather than treated as a defect.

Weight reconciliation is used where ribbon is sold by weight, where rolls have been consolidated and the original roll count has been lost, or where the buyer wants to verify quantity independently of the supplier's count. It works, with one condition: the reference condition must be stated. Polyester, rayon, cotton and blends all respond to ambient humidity to some degree, so the same roll of ribbon can weigh differently on a humid day and a dry one. A weight-based claim that does not specify the conditioning basis is not a claim anyone can evaluate, and it will usually be rejected.

Whichever method is used, the reconciliation has to account for the items that legitimately reduce the delivered total against the ordered total, or the arithmetic will never close:

  • Sampling rolls and length supplied for development and approval, which should be agreed separately from production quantity.
  • Setup and trim waste on the production lot, which is a manufacturing cost rather than a quantity deduction, and should be priced into the order rather than silently deducted from it.
  • Any agreed overage, which for continuous materials is often a percentage rather than a fixed number.
  • Short rolls, which are a quantity issue and should be recorded and reconciled rather than rejected wholesale.

The last point is the one most often mishandled. A short roll is not a defective roll. It should be counted, recorded, and either made up or credited, and it should not be set aside with the rejected material, because doing so conflates two different problems and inflates the apparent defect rate of the shipment.

Roll Construction: Core, Wind, Count and Order Data

The roll is the unit of delivery, and its construction carries information. A ribbon order that specifies only the finished dimensions leaves the roll construction to the mill's default, which means the customer receives whatever the mill's standard practice is rather than something selected for the customer's use.

Core diameter and core material matter for handling. A narrow core on a wide ribbon creates a cone-shaped package that is unstable on a shelf and can unwind under its own weight during transport. Core type matters for downstream handling, and for whether the ribbon can be used on a specific type of cutting or rewinding equipment. Direction of winding matters where the ribbon will be used on a continuous-feed line, because a ribbon wound the wrong way for the equipment will not feed properly and will cause production problems at the customer rather than at the mill.

Roll count per order is a further practical decision. A customer with small batch requirements may prefer more, shorter rolls, because a shorter roll is easier to consume in full and less likely to be partly used and partly discarded. A customer running a high-volume line may prefer fewer, longer rolls to reduce changeover time. This is a packaging decision that costs nothing to agree and is expensive to change later.

Alongside the physical construction, the roll label is what ties a physical object back to a paper order. The fields worth insisting on are the article or colour reference, the lot or batch number, nominal width, roll length, production date and roll sequence. When something goes wrong on receipt, a properly labelled roll turns a multi-week investigation into a single afternoon.

Building the Goods-In Inspection Routine

A goods-in inspection routine for ribbon does not need to be elaborate to be effective. It needs to be repeatable, documented, and performed before the shipment is accepted. The essential steps, in order, are:

  1. Verify identity. Check the article, colour and order reference against the delivery note before anything is opened. A mismatch at this stage is free to fix; a mismatch found halfway through the shipment is not.
  2. Count the rolls. Record the count against the order, and check the reconciliation against the agreed sampling and deduction allowances before reaching for a calculator.
  3. Measure width. On the sample rolls and points defined in the purchase order, using the agreed method. Record individual readings rather than a single averaged figure.
  4. Assess the selvage against the photographic acceptance criteria in the order.
  5. Check colour against the approved shade, in the agreed light condition. This is not the main subject of this article, but a shipment that passes every dimensional check and fails on shade still failed.
  6. Check the labelling and record the lot numbers so that the shipment can be traced.
  7. Record the outcome as accepted, accepted with concessions, or rejected with reasons — and keep short rolls and rejected rolls in separate categories with separate counts.

The single most valuable habit in that list is recording individual readings and lot numbers. Once the results are aggregated into a single verdict, the evidence is gone and the record cannot be used to settle a later question about whether the shipment was typical. Records also allow the trend to be seen: if the same supplier's widths drift consistently in one direction across several orders, that is a process signal worth acting on long before anyone files a claim.

Handling a Quantity Discrepancy Without Losing the Claim

Quantity claims are lost more often on process than on merit. A claim arrives weeks after delivery, with no inspection record, no lot numbers and no reference to the agreed reconciliation method, and the supplier has no way to determine whether the shortfall arose in production, in transit, or in the customer's own counting. At that point the claim cannot be evaluated, and the only available outcome is a commercial settlement that neither party is satisfied with.

The claim that survives is the one that was documented at receipt. It states the order reference, the agreed reconciliation method, the recorded roll count and length, the sampling results, the lot numbers from the roll labels, and the requested remedy. That is a short document, and it can be assembled in minutes at goods-in, while the facts are still in front of the person who observed them. Reconstructing it months later from memory and from a partial packing record is a different and much weaker exercise.

Two further points on the supplier side of the same claim. First, production-set and short-length rolls should be identified at the mill, at winding, and recorded on the delivery note, so that a discrepancy has an explanation before it becomes an accusation. Second, a supplier that proactively reconciles before invoicing — disclosing a short run rather than waiting to be found out — converts a potential dispute into a routine credit. It is worth knowing which side of that line your suppliers sit on before you need to find out.

Writing Measurable Terms Into the Supply Agreement

Everything above reduces to a small number of terms that belong in the purchase order and, for ongoing business, in the supply agreement. The list is short, and each item is specific enough to be applied by someone who was not in the negotiation:

  • Nominal width in millimetres, with the tolerance band stated, and the measurement method described precisely enough that two people would measure the same way.
  • Sampling plan: how many rolls are sampled, how they are selected across the shipment, and how many measurement points are taken on each.
  • Acceptance rule: what the results mean, and what happens to a roll that falls outside the band.
  • Selvage and edge condition, described and illustrated with photographs of accepted and rejected examples.
  • Reconciliation basis: net metres per roll or net weight at a stated reference condition, together with the number of sampling rolls and any agreed overage.
  • Roll construction: core diameter and type, wind direction, roll length and roll count per order.
  • Label content: the fields the mill must put on every roll label.
  • Documentation to accompany the shipment: the inspection report showing the measurements actually taken, not merely a conformance statement.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary content of a well-run quality agreement, and its absence is what turns ordinary commercial friction into formal claims. The difference it makes is not that it prevents every problem. It is that when a problem does occur, there is a shared reference to point at.

The reason this matters at scale is cumulative. A business placing hundreds of ribbon orders a year across dozens of specifications is running a large number of small measurement disputes, each of which consumes real time on both sides and none of which is individually worth escalating. Agreeing the measurable terms once, in a template, and applying it to every order removes that recurring cost. It is the least dramatic and most reliable improvement available in ribbon procurement, and it is entirely within the buyer's control.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the usual width tolerance for woven and printed ribbon?

For most woven polyester and satin ribbon produced for branding and gift packaging, the commercial norm is plus or minus 0.5 mm to plus or minus 1 mm on the nominal width, with the tighter band usually applying to the core width used in moulded or woven trims and the looser band to wide-format decorative ribbon. The important point is not the number itself but that the number is written down, attached to a named measurement method, and applied to every roll in the shipment. A mill working to an unstated tolerance will apply its own default, which may be a band wide enough to break an assembly that depends on a 38 mm ribbon fitting a 38 mm channel.

2. How wide should the tolerance be for ribbon used in moulded bows and trims?

Tighter than general decoration, and narrower than most buyers assume. Where the ribbon is moulded or woven into a finished bow, the width tolerance effectively becomes a bow-to-bow consistency problem: two bows made from the same nominal 38 mm ribbon should be dimensionally close enough that the finished product is uniform on shelf. We recommend specifying a band of plus or minus 0.5 mm and adding a finished-article check against a retained golden sample, because the width tolerance alone does not control the finished result. The moulding process itself adds variables that a width tolerance cannot capture.

3. What is selvage and how do I specify it?

The selvage is the finished edge of a woven ribbon, where the warp threads are bound or finished rather than left to fray. A clean selvage means the edge holds together under handling: no loose weft ends, no laddering, no thread dropouts, no fraying that propagates along the roll. It is specified by describing the condition you will accept rather than by naming a standard, for example: no loose threads visible on either edge, no more than one thread dropout in any one metre, and edges that do not fray after being pulled at normal handling force. Photos of accepted and rejected edges, attached to the purchase order, remove almost all ambiguity.

4. How do I reconcile delivered metres against an ordered quantity?

There are two methods, and they are used for different reasons. Net metres per roll is the primary method for most orders: each roll is counted and the sum compared with the order quantity, with the difference explained by the agreed number of sample rolls and any short or over-length rolls. Net weight at a reference condition is the method used when the ribbon is sold by weight or when the roll count cannot be reconciled because the rolls were consolidated. Weight reconciliation requires the reference condition to be stated, typically a specific moisture and temperature, because hygroscopic fibres change weight with ambient humidity. A weight figure without a stated conditioning basis is not a usable basis for a claim.

5. What information should a supplier put on the roll label?

At minimum: the article or colour reference, the lot or batch number, the nominal width, the roll length in metres, the production date, and the roll sequence within the order. Everything else is useful but optional. The critical fields are the ones that let you tie a physical roll back to a purchase order and to a batch of dye. When a discrepancy appears on receipt, a roll label that carries the lot number and roll length converts a dispute into a routine correction; a roll label without them turns a correction into an investigation.

6. How much length should be allowed for sampling and setup on a production order?

A reasonable commercial allowance is a small number of sampling rolls per colour per order, typically expressed as a fixed count rather than a percentage, plus a stated tolerance on total delivered length. The allowance should be stated on the order as an explicit deduction so that the mill is not guessing at reconciliation time. It is also worth separating the two ideas: samples taken during development and approval are normally charged or supplied free, whereas length sacrificed to setup and trim waste on a production lot is a manufacturing cost. Keeping them separate prevents the same metres being deducted twice.

7. What is the difference between a short roll and a rejected roll?

A short roll is a roll that is within specification but shorter than the agreed roll length; it is a quantity issue and is normally reconciled by adding to the order or crediting the difference. A rejected roll is one that breaches a stated specification, such as a width outside the tolerance band, a defective selvage, a colour outside the approved shade range, or contamination. A rejected roll is a quality issue and is normally replaced. Both need to be recorded separately in the same inspection session, because mixing them leads to disputes about whether the whole shipment or only part of it is in question, and it makes the corrective action unclear.

8. How should shrinkage be considered when specifying ribbon for finished products?

Dimensional stability matters wherever the ribbon is processed after it is received. Washing, heat setting, moulding and even prolonged storage under variable humidity can change ribbon dimensions, and the change is not always in the same direction for different constructions. A satin ribbon and a grosgrain ribbon of the same nominal width will not behave identically under the same condition. If the finished product is dimensionally sensitive, the specification should state the dimensional change allowed after a defined condition, for example a stated percentage change after a defined wash or heat exposure, and the test should be referenced in the purchase order rather than assumed to be covered by a general width tolerance.

9. What causes width variation within a single order?

The most common cause is loom tension variation across a set of looms and across a production run on a single loom. Reeds, tensioning systems, yarn characteristics and the setting of the take-up mechanism all feed into finished width. A second cause is finishing: dyeing, heat setting, washing and calendering can introduce further dimensional change, and different finishing routes produce different results on the same base cloth. A third cause, common and easily missed, is that the mill used several lots of base cloth across the order. When a width trend appears across a shipment, looking at the lot numbers usually explains it faster than looking at the inspection records.

10. How can a buyer reduce the number of dimensional disputes altogether?

Three habits do most of the work. First, agree the measurement method in advance, including where on the ribbon the width is taken, how many points are measured, and whether the measurement is taken on the relaxed ribbon or under a stated tension. Second, define the sampling plan and the acceptance rule in the same document as the order, so there is no argument about which rolls were measured. Third, keep a retained sample from every approved lot and send it back with the purchase order reference. The retained sample turns a disagreement about what was ordered into a comparison that anyone can perform, which is a far cheaper way to settle a dimensional argument than a claim.
