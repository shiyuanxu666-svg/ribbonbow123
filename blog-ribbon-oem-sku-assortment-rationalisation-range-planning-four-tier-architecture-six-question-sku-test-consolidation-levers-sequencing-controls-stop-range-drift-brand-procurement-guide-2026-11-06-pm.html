Why Assortment, Not Price, Is Where Ribbon Programmes Lose Money

Most brand buyers run a ribbon programme as a pricing exercise. They negotiate a price per metre, they concede a minimum order quantity, and then they discover eighteen months later that the programme has drifted to 140 active SKUs, each one with its own dye lot, its own carton configuration, its own reorder lead time and its own forecast error. The unit price was never the problem. The range was.

Every extra colour in a palette carries a dye-lot minimum. Every extra width carries a loom setting. Every extra decoration method carries a plate, screen, foil block or jacquard card. Every extra packaging format carries a carton, a label and a shipping unit. None of these are charged as a line item on your quotation, so they never appear in your cost model — but they are paid for in working capital, in warehouse space, in forecast error and in the slow loss of attention that comes from managing 140 things badly instead of 60 things well.

This guide is a working method for rationalising a ribbon range: how to build an assortment architecture, how to decide what to keep, what to standardise, what to kill and when to kill it, and how to sequence the whole exercise so you do not damage the programme you are trying to save.

Step One: Understand What an SKU Actually Costs You

Before you cut anything, you need a defensible number for the true cost of one more SKU. Buyers routinely compare unit prices and conclude that a low-volume, high-margin colour is a good thing. It usually is not, because the cost of an SKU is not its unit price. It is the sum of six separate costs, most of which sit outside the quotation.

The six components of true SKU cost

  • Unit production cost. The quoted price per metre at your actual volume, not at the price break you hope to reach in eighteen months.
  • Setup and tooling amortisation. Dye-lot setup, screen or plate cost, foil block, jacquard card, die tooling. Spreads across the first order, which is usually the smallest order you will ever place.
  • Carrying cost. Capital tied up in stock, plus warehousing, insurance and shrinkage on the slow-moving tail of each SKU.
  • Forecast error cost. If you order 10,000 m of a colour and demand is 6,000 m, the 4,000 m is not inventory — it is a write-off dressed up as inventory.
  • Operational attention. Every SKU consumes a slot in the master data, a line in the forecast, a place in the reorder report and a decision. At 140 SKUs you are running a different business than at 60.
  • Supplier complexity premium. Many factories price a wide range less favourably than a narrow one. Changeovers cost the mill money, and that money is recovered somewhere. Ask whether your current quote reflects your range.

The arithmetic that decides the argument

A colour that sells 8,000 m a year at a slightly higher unit price can contribute less gross profit than a colour that sells 40,000 m, because the second one carries a third of the setup cost per metre, a third of the forecast error and none of the operational drag. Volume beats margin in trim programmes, and it is not close.

Step Two: Build an Assortment Architecture Before You Cut a Single SKU

Arbitrary cuts are how programmes get damaged. If you remove a colour because it was slow last quarter and it turns out to be a seasonal hero, you will not get it back inside your launch window. The answer is to cut against a structure, not against a spreadsheet.

The four-tier ribbon assortment architecture

Tier 1 — Core franchise (60–70% of volume). A small set of permanent colours and widths that every season, every market and every channel relies on. These are never killed for profitability reasons; they are replaced, not removed. Typically 8–12 SKUs. These carry your inventory investment and they should be your lowest-cost, highest-reliability line.

Tier 2 — Seasonal (20–30% of volume). Colours and patterns tied to a defined selling window. Christmas metallics, Easter pastel sets, Mother's Day florals. These earn the right to be speculative because their demand window is knowable and their deadline is hard. Typically 15–25 SKUs per season, and they must be re-approved or retired at a fixed annual review date.

Tier 3 — Programme and client-specific (5–10% of volume). Custom-dyed or custom-woven SKUs built for one customer, one retailer programme or one specific launch. These are protected from volume pressure because their value is not in the metres, it is in the contract they attach to.

Tier 4 — Bespoke and development (1–3% of volume). Sample-level and low-volume custom work. This is where new ideas live. It is also where money disappears, because a bespoke SKU with a 500 m minimum and a six-week lead time is very expensive per metre, and the buyers who create them are rarely the buyers who order them again.

Step Three: The Six-Question Test for Every SKU

Run every active SKU through the same six questions. Score each 0 or 1. The result is a defensible classification, and it survives the argument in the meeting where someone says "but that colour is our signature".

  1. Does it sell more than 5,000 metres a year? If no, it cannot carry its own setup cost unless it carries a strategic reason. That reason must be written down.
  2. Did it meet its forecast in the last two reorder cycles? Within 20% either way. A SKU that misses by more than half every cycle is not forecasting badly — it is dead, and you are ordering it out of habit.
  3. Can another SKU in the same tier do the same job? Two navy satins at 10 mm and 15 mm serve different products. Two navies at 20 mm in the same construction with the same decoration do not, and the second one is your cut candidate.
  4. Does it carry a decoration, width or construction combination unique to it? Unique combinations carry unique setup cost. If it is unique because a buyer asked for it, protect it. If it is unique because of an accident in the range history, that is your candidate.
  5. Does it have a live order or a forecastable pipeline in the next 60 days? A SKU with nothing ordered and nothing forecast is inventory with a shelf life. Note it as a run-out candidate, not a cut.
  6. Does the gross profit it generates exceed the gross profit of the volume it is using? This is the decisive test. Compare what the SKU earns against what the equivalent metres would earn in a Tier 1 core colour. Frequently the answer is uncomfortable.

Five or six yes answers: keep, and consider whether it should be promoted into a higher tier.

Three or four yes answers: keep but constrain — reduce the reorder minimum, consolidate the width or the construction, or move it to a standing replenishment model so it stops being a forecast-driven SKU.

Two or fewer yes answers: run-out candidate. Stop reordering, sell through existing stock, and formally retire it at the next review.

Step Four: Consolidate Before You Cut

Most of the value in a rationalisation exercise comes from consolidation, not deletion. Deletion is visible and risky. Consolidation is quieter and usually worth more. Work through these levers in order before you touch the range list.

Lever 1: Standardise the construction

If two SKUs differ only in width, and the width difference does not change the product the customer is decorating, choose the one that serves the most programmes and move the other. Width standardisation also improves dye-lot yield, because you are dyeing the same construction in more metres per lot.

Lever 2: Standardise the decoration route

Screen print, digital print, heat transfer and foil are all ways to put a logo on ribbon. Each route carries its own setup cost, its own minimum run and its own colour tolerance. If your volume can absorb it, consolidating your top five designs onto a single route lets you pool their minimums and negotiate one rate.

Lever 3: Standardise the pack format

Roll length, core size, winding direction, carton configuration and piece count per carton drive your ocean freight per metre more than almost anything else in your supply chain. Two SKUs that ship in identical cartons can be pooled into one shipment; two that do not cannot. Standardising pack format is free money and it makes the range easier to manage.

Lever 4: Standardise the core colours across programmes

If three different customer programmes each have their own version of black, cream and blush, that is three dye lots of each instead of one. Pooling the palette across programmes reduces both setup cost and stock, and it makes your factory relationship simpler rather than more complex.

Lever 5: Merge low-volume into a base colour

A custom-dyed navy at 3,000 m a year is almost always more expensive per metre than a stock navy. If the custom shade is within a tolerance your end product can absorb, buy the stock colour and save the setup. If it is not, you have found a genuine reason to keep the SKU — document it and stop re-litigating it every year.

Step Five: Sequence the Rationalisation so Nothing Breaks

The order in which you make changes determines whether the programme survives the exercise. A rationalisation that starts with deleting SKUs and ends with renegotiating will produce a factory who is unhappy, a forecast that is wrong and a customer who noticed.

  1. Month 1 — Measure. Build the SKU master with the six component costs. Publish the true cost, not the quoted cost, internally. No changes yet.
  2. Month 2 — Consolidate the easy wins. Standardise pack format, core colour pooling, width rationalisation. None of these require telling a customer anything, because the product in their hand does not change.
  3. Month 3 — Negotiate on the new, narrower range. Now go back to the factory with a cleaner specification. This is the stage where a rationalised range turns into a real price improvement, and it is the reason the exercise is worth doing in this order.
  4. Month 4 — Run out the dead SKUs. Stop reordering, communicate a final-order date to affected customers, and sell through remaining stock.
  5. Month 5 — Fix the process. Introduce the quarterly assortment review, the SKU creation gate and the reorder-minimum rules that stop the range drifting back out.

What a good outcome looks like

A programme that had 140 SKUs and now has 85, where the 85 are better forecast, better consolidated and cheaper to make, where Tier 1 runs at 72% of volume instead of 44%, where the factory quotes a better price because the job is simpler, and where nobody lost a colour the customer was actually buying. That is the target. Cutting SKU count for its own sake is not the goal; cost and complexity reduction is.

How to Stop the Range Drifting Back Out

Almost every rationalisation that fails, fails the same way: it works, and then within eighteen months the range is back to where it started. The reason is that nobody installed the controls that prevent drift. Three controls prevent most of it.

Control 1: A SKU creation gate

No SKU enters the master without answering: does this replace something? If it does not replace something, it must be a Tier 3 programme SKU with a named contract behind it, or a Tier 4 development SKU with a shelf-life date. Every SKU gets an owner and a review date at creation.

Control 2: Quarterly assortment review with a fixed agenda

Same four questions every quarter: what is growing, what is dying, what should be consolidated, what should be launched. A standing date with a standing agenda is what turns a one-off project into a management routine. It takes ninety minutes and it prevents six figures of drift a year.

Control 3: Reorder minimums that reflect true velocity

Tier 1 core colours get a high reorder minimum and standing replenishment, because the cost of holding them is low relative to the cost of being out of stock. Tier 2 seasonal colours get a strict minimum aligned to the forecast. Tier 4 development colours get no reorder minimum at all — they are made to order. Setting one rule for all tiers is why ranges become unmanageable.

What to Ask Your Factory

A factory that runs range programmes daily can tell you where your money is going faster than you can model it. Ask these questions directly — they are normal questions to ask, and the answers are free.

  • How many distinct dye lots do you run for my programme in a year, and what is the setup cost on each?
  • Which of my SKUs are below your economic run length, and how much does that cost me versus moving them to standard minimums?
  • If I consolidated my pack format, what would the freight per metre become?
  • Which two of my colours would you expect to be the same shade, and could I move to one?
  • What is the changeover time between my most common constructions, and would a narrower range reduce it?

The factory will also tell you which of your SKUs they actually prefer to make and which they quietly complain about. That information is worth more than a spreadsheet model, and it is available for the asking.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand buyers, product managers and procurement teams most often ask us about rationalising a ribbon range: how many SKUs a programme should carry, how to protect a strategically important colour, how to sequence the exercise, and how to stop the range drifting back out again. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How many SKUs should a ribbon programme have?

There is no universal number, but the four-tier architecture gives a useful sanity check. Tier 1 core franchise SKUs should typically total 8-12 and carry 60-70% of volume. Tier 2 seasonal should add 15-25 per season. Programme-specific and bespoke SKUs should remain a small minority. If you have more than 60 active SKUs across all tiers, the range is very likely carrying cost that is not visible in your unit price. The test is not the count but the distribution: if your core franchise is under 50% of volume, your range is fragmented.

2. Should we cut a SKU that is slow but strategically important?

Yes, but never silently. A slow SKU can be retained as a Tier 3 or Tier 4 SKU with a written strategic justification, a named owner and a review date. What you should not do is keep it as a normal replenished SKU with a forecast-driven reorder, because that is where the money disappears. Retain the SKU, but change how it is managed. Moving it to made-to-order with no reorder minimum preserves the option at near-zero carrying cost.

3. How do I handle SKUs that are only slow because they are new?

Give every new SKU a defined evaluation window before the test in this guide applies. A launch colour typically needs one full reorder cycle, and often two, before its velocity is meaningful. Judge new SKUs on whether they are tracking towards their forecast, not on absolute volume, and judge them at a fixed date rather than continuously. Cutting a launch SKU because its first cycle was slow is the most common self-inflicted range wound.

4. What is the fastest way to reduce cost without removing any SKUs?

Consolidation, not deletion. Standardising the pack format - roll length, core size, carton configuration and pieces per carton - reduces your ocean freight per metre and lets you pool shipments. Pooling core colours across customer programmes reduces dye-lot setup cost and stock. Standardising the decoration route across your top designs lets you negotiate one rate and pool minimums. These three changes typically produce a visible cost improvement with no change whatsoever to the product the customer receives.

5. Does a narrower range actually get a better price from the factory?

Frequently, yes, and it is worth asking explicitly rather than assuming. A mill that runs 140 SKUs for you absorbs changeover time, more dye lots and more setup cost across your programme. Some factories will quote a lower rate on a consolidated range because the operational cost to you genuinely falls. The most common version of this is that the factory will absorb your custom-dyed SKUs into standard colour minimums if you are willing to accept a defined shade tolerance. Ask, and put the answer in the quotation.

6. How do I stop the range drifting back after rationalisation?

Three controls, installed at the same time as the cuts. First, a SKU creation gate that requires every new SKU to either replace an existing one or carry a named contract or a development shelf-life date. Second, a fixed quarterly assortment review with a standing agenda covering what is growing, dying, consolidatable and worth launching. Third, tiered reorder minimums, so Tier 1 core colours get standing replenishment while Tier 4 development SKUs are made to order with no reorder minimum. The controls matter more than the cuts, because without them the range returns to its original shape within about eighteen months.

7. Should assortment rationalisation come before or after negotiating a new price?

Consolidate first, negotiate second. If you negotiate on a fragmented range, you are negotiating against your own complexity and you will get a worse price. The sequence that works is: measure the true cost per SKU, apply the safe consolidations that do not change what the customer receives, then go back to the factory with the narrower and cleaner specification. That is the point in the process where rationalisation converts into actual margin improvement rather than just tidier management.

8. What about SKUs that are custom-dyed for a specific customer?

Treat them as Tier 3 and protect them, because their value is the contract behind them rather than the metres. But still test whether the custom shade is genuinely required. If the customer's end product can accept a stock shade within a defined Delta E tolerance, moving to a stock colour removes the dye-lot setup cost entirely and is one of the highest-value changes in the whole exercise. If the shade is genuinely required, write that reason down so it is not re-argued at every annual review.
