Most private label programmes do not fail on ribbon. They fail on the first audit, the first shipment that arrives differently from the sample, or the first claim that cannot be answered with a document. By the time a brand owner discovers this, the packaging is already on shelf and the cost of correcting it is multiplied by the size of the run.

The factories that look identical from the outside are not identical in the ways that matter. Two suppliers quoting the same 20 mm printed grosgrain at the same unit price may differ in how they handle a colour tolerance dispute, how they respond when a retailer raises an audit request, and whether they can still produce the same quality in eighteen months. Those differences are only visible if you go looking for them deliberately.

This guide is about looking. It describes how to assess a ribbon factory before you commit, what evidence to ask for and how to read it, and what to do with the answers once you have them. It is written for brand owners, category buyers and procurement managers who need a repeatable method rather than a list of things to feel good about.

The Assessment Is Not an Audit

Buyers often treat supplier assessment as a compliance exercise: collect certificates, tick boxes, file the report. That approach satisfies a checklist and tells you almost nothing about whether your programme will run. A certificate held by a factory says that something was true on a date, in a scope, for a product class. It does not say that the specific ribbon you will receive will arrive on the date you need it in the condition you approved.

The useful version of assessment asks a different set of questions. Can this factory see my order in their production plan? Can they hold a colour across a lot boundary six months from now? Do they know who owns the tooling when the programme ends? If they stop answering clearly, what do you do?

Think of it in three layers. The first layer is capability: does the factory have the equipment, the people and the process to make your specification at your volume? The second layer is evidence: can they prove it, in documents you can verify, rather than in assertions you have to accept? The third layer is behaviour: how do they act when something goes wrong, when you ask a difficult question, and when the schedule slips?

Most buyers spend all their assessment time on layer two, because layer two produces the artefacts that a file can hold. That is a reasonable place to start, but it is not where the risk actually sits. Layer one predicts whether you will get what you asked for. Layer three predicts whether the relationship will survive the first difficult quarter.

Twelve Things to Establish Before You Commit

These are the questions we would want answered by any factory before placing a first order, grouped by what they reveal. They are framed as questions because the answer tells you more when the factory has to write it out.

Capability

What is your actual production capacity in metres per day, and how is it broken down across weaving, dyeing, finishing and assembly? A headline capacity number tells you little. A number broken into stages tells you where your order sits in the queue, and whether a 20,000 metre order displaces someone else or waits for a free week.

What is your order book structure across the year, and which quarters are structurally tight? Peaks in gift packaging, confectionery and Christmas run earlier and harder than most buyers plan for. A factory that is honest about a September bottleneck is more useful than one that promises everything is available in November.

Which of the processes you need are in-house, and which are subcontracted? Screen printing, foil stamping, heat transfer, jacquard weaving, die cutting, assembly and packing are all sometimes done in-house and sometimes outsourced. There is nothing wrong with subcontracting, but you need to know, because you need to know who is accountable when the subcontracted step produces a defect.

What is your minimum order quantity per colour and per width, and how does it change with construction? A factory that quotes a minimum of 1,000 metres will usually have a different minimum for a jacquard construction, a different one for foil, and a different one again for a fully bespoke print. Ask for the matrix, not the headline.

Evidence

Which certificates do you hold, for which product classes, and when do they expire? Most factories hold a portfolio of certificates covering different sites, different product classes and different years. Asking which certificate applies to your specific ribbon, and what the expiry date is, separates the factories that hold evidence from the factories that hold certificates.

Can you show a recent social compliance audit report, including the corrective action closures? A social compliance audit that found issues and closed them is a better signal than a clean report, because it demonstrates the process works. Ask to see the closure evidence, not just the summary.

What does your incoming material inspection look like, and can you show the records? Yarn arriving from a spinner carries the risk of the dye house. Ask how incoming yarn is checked against the approved specification and what happens when it fails.

Can you provide traceability from finished roll back to the dye lot? This is the question that determines whether a quality dispute can be settled with evidence or with opinion. It also determines how fast you can answer a retailer's traceability request.

What is your AQL level, and have you ever had it specified by a customer rather than set by you? The answer tells you whether the factory operates a sampling system or a judgement system.

Behaviour

What is your quoted lead time for a first order, and what is it for a repeat? The difference between the two is the number that matters, because it reveals how much of the schedule is setup and how much is queue time. A factory quoting 25 days first-time and 15 days repeat is telling you that changeover is half the schedule.

How do you handle a colour complaint on goods already shipped? Ask for the actual sequence, in order. Where does the claim go, what evidence is required, what is the remedy ladder, and who has the authority to approve it? This is the question that most reliably separates a supplier relationship that will last from one that will not.

What happens to my tooling, my artwork and my specification if we stop working together? The answer should be written down. Dies, screens, plates and printed sample references have real value and real ownership questions attached to them.

Who is my day-to-day contact, and what is the escalation path when they are unavailable? This is a small question that predicts a lot. A factory with a named account contact, a named production contact and a named quality contact can resolve most problems without escalation. A factory with one contact for everything cannot.

Turning the Answers Into a Decision

Twelve questions produce more than you can act on. The output that works is a short written assessment with three parts: a score by category, a list of specific findings, and a decision.

Score capability, evidence and behaviour separately rather than as one blended number. A factory with excellent capability and weak evidence is a different proposition from the reverse, and the two require different mitigations. Excellent capability with weak evidence means you build an audit and inspection regime around the supplier. Excellent evidence with weak capability means the paperwork is good but the goods will not be. Both factories score 7 out of 10 on a blended scorecard and only one of them is a good supplier for you.

Then convert findings into conditions. "Does not hold FSC chain of custody for the paper core" becomes "supply the core pre-certified, and receive the transaction certificate with the shipment, or we specify an alternative core." A finding without a condition is just an observation. A finding with a condition is a negotiation.

Finally, decide what kind of relationship you are buying. If the factory is excellent on capability and behaviour but weak on evidence, you are buying a partner you will manage closely, and you should plan the management effort. If it is strong on evidence and weak on behaviour, you are buying a compliant supplier that will need contractual cover, and you should get the contract right before the first order rather than after the first dispute.

The Questions to Ask on a Factory Visit

Many first-time buyers never visit. That is a legitimate choice for a small brand and a poor one for a large programme. When you do visit, the sequence matters more than the checklist, because a factory can present well and still be difficult to work with.

Arrive at the start of a shift rather than the middle of one. Look at the floor before you look at the showroom. The questions that matter are the ones asked in production: how many open orders do you have, what is the longest one, what happens if a loom stops mid-lot. Stand at the end of the weaving line and watch a lot change. The condition of the floor, the age of the machines and the amount of material stacked in aisles tell you more about the next twelve months of your supply than any certificate does.

Ask to see the testing room and watch a colour measurement being taken. If the spectrophotometer has a calibration log and the log is current, the factory understands colour. If it does not, your colour continuity requirement will be met approximately and you will find out at inspection.

Ask to see the yarn store and the finished goods warehouse together. The distance between them tells you how many people touch your material before it reaches you. Then ask whether the same people, with the same work instructions, are on the line that produces your order as on the line that produces the standard product.

Finally, ask the person who would actually run your order where your order would sit in the schedule right now. Ask them to show you the plan. A factory with a live, updated schedule can answer this question in ninety seconds. A factory without one will tell you the lead time again.

What to Do When the First Order Goes Wrong

It will. Somewhere in the first three orders, something will arrive short, late or different from the approved sample. What happens next is more informative than anything you assessed beforehand, because it is the first unscripted evidence of how the factory behaves under pressure.

Keep the first disagreement procedural rather than emotional. Document what arrived, with photographs, measurements and the retained sample. Reference the specification and the purchase order. Ask for the factory's own assessment in writing before offering yours. Resist the urge to reach a quick settlement — the purpose of the first claim is to establish the process, and a process established in the calm first year will hold under pressure in the third.

Watch what the factory does, not only what it says. Does it stop production to investigate, or continue and hope the lot passes inspection? Does it propose a corrective action, or does it propose a replacement shipment? Does it notify you proactively, or do you discover the issue at receiving? A factory that tells you about a problem before you find it is telling you something important about its internal communication.

The remedy ladder should be agreed before you need it. A typical structure moves from replacement of the affected units, to credit, to rework, to a price concession on the next order, with a defined threshold above which the programme moves to a second source. Agreeing it in advance means neither side is negotiating from a position of annoyance in month seven.

Building an Ongoing Relationship

The factory that looks best on paper on day one is rarely the one that performs best over three years. The strongest supplier relationships in this industry share a pattern: the buyer's volume grows, the factory learns the product well enough to anticipate problems, and both sides spend less time verifying and more time improving.

This has to be built deliberately. A quarterly review that covers quality data, delivery performance, open corrective actions and forecast changes takes two hours and does more for the programme than a yearly audit. Share the forecast, not just the purchase order — a factory that can see a demand signal six months out can plan capacity, and a factory that only sees purchase orders reacts to orders. Give and accept feedback on both sides, including about your own behaviour: late artwork changes, unclear specifications, forecast swings that damage the factory's planning.

Most importantly, once a factory has earned your trust, widen the relationship. Add a second construction, move a category, extend payment terms or ask them to present your programme to a retailer alongside you. Suppliers who are being asked to do more work are more likely to invest in making it work well than suppliers who are being asked for the same thing at the same price every quarter.

What Good Looks Like in Practice

At the end of a careful assessment, you should be able to answer four questions without hesitation. Do they have the capacity and the process to make my specification? Can they prove it, and can I verify the proof? Do I know who to call when something goes wrong? And have I watched how they behave when something has gone wrong before?

If you can answer all four, you have a supplier programme rather than a purchase order. If you can only answer the first two, you have a certificate collection and a product sample, which is where most buyers stop.

The difference between those two positions shows up two years later, in the programmes that run without drama and the programmes that need rescuing. A factory that has been assessed properly — on capability, on evidence and on behaviour — is still not guaranteed to be good. But a factory that has been assessed properly is the only kind worth discovering the difference about.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners, category buyers and procurement managers most often ask us about assessing a ribbon factory, verifying supplier certificates and handling quality claims. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. How do I verify a ribbon factory's certifications are genuine and applicable to my product?

Request the certificate numbers themselves rather than badge images or PDF summaries, then check each one directly with the issuing body. OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 certificates can be confirmed against the label check on the institute's website; FSC chain of custody numbers can be validated against the FSC public certificate search; BSCI and SMETA audit reports can be confirmed with the issuing social compliance body. For each certificate, read three things: the licence holder name (does it match the legal entity on your purchase order and on the bank account), the product class covered (does it include your construction, not just the word 'textiles'), and the expiry or audit date. A certificate that names a different company, a different site, or a broader product class than yours is not evidence for your programme.

2. What is the difference between a factory assessment and a factory audit?

An audit is a compliance check against a defined standard — BSCI, SMETA, ISO 9001 — and it produces a pass, a fail, or a list of findings against a fixed set of criteria. An assessment is broader and answers a different question: is this factory the right partner for my programme. It covers capability (capacity, equipment, in-house processes), evidence (certificates, traceability, quality records) and behaviour (how they communicate, handle complaints, escalate problems). A factory can hold a clean ISO 9001 certificate and still be a poor operational fit for your volume or your construction mix. Many large retailers require both.

3. Should I visit the factory before placing a first order?

For a first order with a significant value, yes — and for a repeat programme, definitely. If the order is small and the commodity is standard, a virtual audit with photographs, a video walkthrough of the relevant production line, and confirmed references from other customers can substitute. If you do visit, the sequence matters more than the checklist: go at the start of a shift, look at the floor before the showroom, ask to see the current production schedule, and ask the person who would run your order where your order would sit in that schedule right now. A factory with a live schedule answers in ninety seconds.

4. What questions should I ask a supplier about handling a quality complaint on shipped goods?

Ask for the actual sequence in order: where the claim goes, what evidence is required from you, what the remedy ladder is, who has authority to approve a concession, and what happens if the parties cannot agree. The answers reveal more than any other part of the assessment, because most factories have an informal practice here rather than a written one, and the informal practice is what you will experience. A factory that can describe a remedy ladder with thresholds and approval authority has a functioning quality system, whether or not it has a certificate for it.

5. How much volume do I need to commit to before a factory will treat my programme seriously?

There is no universal threshold, and the answer depends more on the factory's strategic interest than on the volume. Small programmes are handled well by mills that specifically build a business around them, and poorly by mills that only want large seasonal runs. Two things matter more than the order size: whether your reorder pattern is predictable enough to plan against, and whether the factory can see a business case for investing in your programme specifically. A 3,000 metre first order with a 60% reorder rate is often more attractive than a 20,000 metre trial order with no follow-up.

6. What should be in the supply agreement so that the tooling and artwork are protected?

Name the tooling and materials explicitly — dies, screens, plates, colour cards, physical swatch books, digital files — and state who owns each one, from when. Specify the conditions under which ownership transfers, whether it is at order placement, at final payment, or after a defined period of inactivity. Include a defined exit event: what happens to the tooling if orders stop for six months, if the programme is terminated, or if the factory ceases to trade. Add a hand-over obligation with a time limit, so that materials cannot sit in a warehouse indefinitely waiting for a dispute to be resolved, and specify who pays for the hand-over.

7. How should I handle a first quality claim so that it improves the relationship rather than damaging it?

Keep it procedural. Document what arrived with photographs, measurements against the approved sample and the retained production sample, and the purchase order and specification references. Ask for the factory's own assessment in writing before offering yours. Resist settling quickly: the first claim establishes the process, and a process agreed in the calm first year will hold under pressure later. Watch what they do rather than only what they say — whether they stop production to investigate, whether they notify you before you find the issue, and whether they propose a corrective action rather than only a replacement shipment.
