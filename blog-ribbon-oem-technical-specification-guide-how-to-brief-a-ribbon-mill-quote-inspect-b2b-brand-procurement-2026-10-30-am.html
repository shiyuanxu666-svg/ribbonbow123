Why Most Ribbon Specifications Get Quoted Wrong

Most ribbon buyers send a specification that is short, and then get back a quotation that is wrong. The quotation is not usually a bad-faith response to a vague request — it is a rational response to one. If a brief says "satin ribbon, 20mm, red, printed with our logo, 5,000 metres", every factory that receives it will fill the silence with its own standard assumptions: its own satin construction, its own idea of the right red, its own default print process, its own width tolerance, its own edge treatment, its own carton format. Five factories will return five different prices for what appears to be the same product, and all five are correct in the sense that they quoted the request they were given.

The problem compounds as the programme grows. The first order is fine, because the buyer and the factory share an implicit understanding. By the third order, the buyer has changed, the buyer has moved on, the factory has changed shift leadership, and nobody can say precisely what was agreed — because the agreement was never written down. This is where programmes quietly lose money: not in the negotiation, but in the re-specification that happens every eighteen months, and in the defects that arrive because two parties were each working from a different mental model of the same product.

A written specification is the mechanism that stops that. It makes quotes comparable, because everyone is quoting the same object. It makes inspection objective, because the acceptance criteria are stated in measurable terms. And it makes the approved sample and the delivered roll provably the same thing — which is the single most useful property a specification can have, because it ends the most expensive argument in this industry: whether the delivered goods match what was approved.

This guide sets out the fourteen fields that, taken together, make a ribbon specification complete, with the wording that works, the tolerance that can actually be measured, and the wording errors that most often cause a wrong quotation. It is written for brand owners, private-label managers and procurement teams who need the mill to build exactly what is intended — and who will be living with the consequences for the life of the programme.

At Smith Ribbon, in Xiamen since 2004, we work from a written job specification for every OEM programme, and the sheet we use is built around these fourteen fields. It is a modest document. It is also, in a large number of cases, the difference between a programme that runs for years without drama and one that spends a third of its life in dispute.

The 14 Fields That Make a Specification Complete

The fourteen fields below are the ones that, when specified, remove the ambiguity that produces wrong quotes and uninspectable stock. Not all are technical in the narrow sense — several are commercial or procedural — but each is something that gets decided one way or another, and the specification exists to make the decision explicit rather than implicit.

1. Construction and process route. What the ribbon is made of and how it is made: woven or non-woven, warp and weft arrangement, whether it is a single fabric or a composite (a wired ribbon with a plastic or wire core, or a laminated ribbon with a film backing), and the intended end use.

2. Base material and fibre content. Polyester, satin weave, cotton, RPET recycled polyester, nylon, paper, bamboo fibre, linen, or a blend — stated as a composition with a tolerance, and with the certification status attached where relevant.

3. Width. Nominal width in millimetres, plus the tolerance, plus whether the width is finished width or greige width before further processing.

4. Thickness and weight. Nominal thickness or weight per linear metre (g/m or g/m²), with a tolerance.

5. Weave and surface. For a woven ribbon: satin, grosgrain, twill, plain, herringbone, or a jacquard structure. For the surface: matte, lustre, brushed, or velvet pile — and for velvet, the pile height and the pile direction.

6. Colour. The reference system, the specific reference, the tolerance, and the approval mechanism.

7. Decoration. The process, the artwork status, the colour count, the placement and repeat, and the surface coverage.

8. Edge quality. Selvedge construction, whether the edge is cut or selvedged, the edge treatment, and the weft or shrinkage allowance at the edge.

9. Finish. The finishing operations applied: heat setting, coating, calendering, edge softening, winding, and any anti-static or moisture treatment.

10. Dimensional and physical tolerances. What is allowed to vary, and by how much, measured how.

11. Performance and test requirements. The specific tests, the specific methods, and the specific acceptance grades.

12. Packing and carton configuration. How the ribbon is wound, the length or metre weight per roll, the carton quantity, the carton dimensions and the gross weight.

13. Labelling and traceability. What identification travels on the core, the carton and the shipment documents, and what lot information the factory must retain.

14. Approval mechanics. Which sample must be approved, in what form, by whom, and what happens if production does not match the approved sample.

Fourteen fields sounds like a lot for a ribbon, which is a fairly simple object. But each corresponds to a decision that would otherwise be made by default, silently, by whoever happens to be running the line. The purpose of writing them down is not control for its own sake — it is to make sure the decisions are yours.

Fields 1-5: Material, Construction, Width and Thickness

These five fields are where a quotation is most often wrong by a large margin, because each can move the price substantially.

Construction and process route. "Satin ribbon" does not identify a product. A woven polyester satin and a non-woven (extruded or calendared) ribbon can be sold under similar names and differ in hand, drape, print behaviour and cost by a wide margin. State the route explicitly: woven polyester satin, 4/1 warp-faced construction, or non-woven polypropylene-faced satin. If the ribbon is composite, describe the stack — "19mm grosgrain with a 0.4mm PVC wire core inserted at the centre", or "polyester grosgrain laminated to a 25-micron BOPP film". The composite description matters especially for wired and laminated ribbon, because the core changes the handling characteristics, the cutting behaviour, the recyclability and the destination market at once.

Base material and fibre content. State the composition and the tolerance, and attach the certification status where it matters. "100% polyester" and "100% recycled polyester (RPET)" are different products with different documentation, different market access and different price. If you require GRS-certified recycled content, say so in the specification rather than in a covering email, and state the minimum certified recycled content — because 30% recycled and 100% recycled are not the same claim, and the certification covers a stated scope, not a general belief in sustainability. For blended constructions, give the percentages and note which component the blend is designed to deliver, because a rayon-blend organza and a silk-blend organza are specified by entirely different logic.

Width. Nominal width with a tolerance, and the question of whether it is finished width or greige width. This distinction has bitten more programmes than almost any other single field, because the width changes between greige and finished, and a specification written against greige width produces a finished ribbon narrower than the buyer expects. The tolerance should reflect what the buyer can live with rather than what the mill can produce: a tolerance tighter than the natural variation of the process generates disputes on every delivery, while a tolerance much wider than needed gives away quality that was paid for. For most finished decorative ribbon, half a millimetre either side of nominal is a reasonable working figure; for narrow trims below 10mm, tighter is often justified.

Thickness and weight. Where the ribbon is sold by weight rather than by metre, or where thickness affects the end use, state it. Weight per linear metre in grams is usually the more stable and more useful reference for a satin, because it correlates with the yarn count and the construction and can be weighed on a calibrated scale on site. Thickness measured by micrometer is harder to reproduce consistently and is sensitive to the pressure applied during measurement — so if thickness matters, specify the measurement method and the pressure, not just the number.

Weave and surface. The weave determines the surface, the hand, the drape, the print behaviour and the price. Satin is lustre and fluid and takes printed decoration well. Grosgrain is ribbed, firm and textural, holds a bow shape better and takes embroidery well. Twill and plain weaves are utilitarian. Jacquard is a woven pattern rather than a printed one. For the surface, state the finish: matte, lustre, brushed, or velvet. For velvet, pile height and pile direction are not optional details — pile direction determines whether the ribbon reads light or dark depending on which way it is viewed, which is a common surprise in retail displays where the lighting direction is fixed and the ribbon is not.

Fields 6-8: Colour, Decoration and Edge

Colour. A colour specification needs four parts: the reference system, the specific reference, the tolerance, and the approval mechanism. Name the system — Pantone for solid colours, or a named standard — and give the reference. Then state the tolerance against a measurement method, and define how it will be judged. For dyed ribbon the usual approach is a spectrophotometer reading under a stated illuminant, expressed as a colour difference value with an agreed limit. For printed ribbon the colour comes from the ink rather than the dye, so the reference applies to the ink, not the ground, and the tolerance should be stated against the approved strike-off rather than against a Pantone chip in a drawer.

The single most important element is the approval mechanism. State that a physical strike-off or lab dip must be approved in writing before production, and state what happens if the production lot falls outside tolerance — whether it is reworked, re-dyed, accepted, or rejected. Without this, a marginal colour becomes an argument.

Decoration. Name the process rather than describing the appearance. Screen print, heat transfer, foil stamping, sublimation and jacquard weaving are different processes with different properties and different prices, and a buyer who writes "printed with our logo, gold" will receive screen-printed metallic ink where foil was intended, or heat transfer where a jacquard was expected. Alongside the process, state the artwork status — whether print-ready files are supplied, in what format, and who generates separators or screens — the colour count per design, the design repeat and how it is measured, the position of the design across the width, and whether the print runs edge to edge or is inset. Where the ribbon has a visible selvedge, specify the unprinted margin. And state the minimum line weight and minimum text size, because a mill cannot reproduce a line the brief never told it was too thin.

Edge quality. The edge of a ribbon is where most visible defects live, and it is the field most often left entirely unmentioned. State the weave construction — a selvedge or a cut edge — and whether the edge is to be soft, sealed, overlocked, or heat-finished. For woven ribbon, state the weft insertion or shrinkage allowance at the edge, because a selvedge that pulls in during finishing produces a ribbon narrower at the edge than in the body, which shows immediately in a finished bow. For wired and laminated constructions, state how the core is terminated and how the ribbon is to be cut without fraying or exposing the core. Edge quality is also the difference between a ribbon that looks finished and one that looks like an offcut, so it deserves a line of its own rather than being absorbed into a general quality clause.

Fields 9-11: Finish, Tolerance and Performance Testing

Finish. State the finishing operations the ribbon requires: heat setting for dimensional stability, coating for gloss or stiffness or abrasion resistance, calendering for a smooth printable surface, edge softening, and any anti-static treatment where the ribbon will be handled by an automated packaging line. Also state the winding condition — the ribbon should arrive on a core, wound in a consistent direction, at a tension that does not create a set in the roll, because a ribbon wound under excess tension arrives already relaxed and behaves differently on the customer's line.

Dimensional and physical tolerances. This is where most specifications fail, because they say "good quality" instead of saying what good quality means. Write the tolerance against a named characteristic, a stated measurement method and a stated limit. Width within plus or minus 0.5mm of nominal. Weight per linear metre within plus or minus 5 per cent. Edge waviness within a stated number of millimetres over a stated length. Colour difference within a stated value under a stated illuminant. A defect limit expressed as a percentage of inspected units rather than as a named criterion. The rule of thumb is simple: if a customer service team could not settle a dispute about it from the specification alone, it is not yet a tolerance — it is an aspiration.

Performance and test requirements. This is the field that most often saves money, because an unstated performance requirement defaults to whatever the mill's standard product happens to achieve. Name the test, the method and the acceptance grade. For a ribbon that will be laundered: wash fastness grade 4 or better per ISO 105-C06. For a ribbon that will rub against skin or another surface: crocking grade 4 or better per ISO 105-N02. For a ribbon exposed to light: a stated light-fastness grade against a named method. For skin contact: the applicable chemical compliance, and for OEKO-TEX Standard 100, the product class and the certificate number covering the specific article. For a ribbon that will be twisted and tied: a stated number of flex cycles and a stated result.

The point is not to specify more than the product needs. It is to specify only what the product actually needs, in a form that an inspector can apply. A ribbon for a gift box does not need a wash-fastness grade, and specifying one adds cost without adding value. A ribbon for a hair accessory does need a crocking grade, and omitting it is what produces the complaint about colour transferring onto a child's hair in warm weather.

Fields 12-14: Packing, Labelling, Traceability and Approval

These fields are procedural, and they decide what happens when something goes wrong.

Packing and carton configuration. State the winding: the core type and size, the metres per roll or the roll weight, the direction of winding, and the maximum roll diameter that will fit the customer's existing handling equipment. Then the carton: rolls per carton, carton dimensions, gross and net weight, and the maximum stacking weight the carton will bear. Do not skip this. A programme that arrives in cartons the customer's warehouse cannot handle — because the carton is 20% larger than standard, or 30kg instead of 20kg — generates a long, entirely avoidable chain of storage problems that has nothing to do with the ribbon and will be attributed to the ribbon.

Labelling and traceability. State what identification travels where: the core label, the carton label, the packing list, and the shipping documents. At minimum it should carry the buyer's order number, the factory's article or style number, the colour reference, the width, the lot or batch number, the production date, the metre quantity, and the certification marks that apply to that lot. State how long the factory must retain the production records for that lot, and what must be retrievable if a claim is raised eighteen months later. This is not bureaucratic overhead; it is what allows a root-cause investigation to happen at all. Without a lot number that traces back to a dyeing batch, an investigation becomes an argument about whose fault it is, which is a way of saying that no one will find out.

Approval mechanics. State which sample must be approved before production release — a lab dip, a strike-off, a woven sample, a full pre-production sample — in what form, by whom, and by what date. State what the approval covers: does approving the colour also approve the print, or is each approved separately? State what happens if the production lot does not match the approved sample: is it held for inspection, reworked, re-produced, or shipped with a concession? And state the point in the process after which changes are chargeable.

This is the field buyers most often omit and most often regret. A programme where the approval rules are written down has a defined path when a roll misses. A programme where they are not has a corridor, and the negotiation that happens in that corridor happens at the moment you least want to be negotiating — usually the day before a container needs to leave the port.

The Ambiguity Checklist: Twelve Common Loose-Wording Errors

These are the specific phrases that cause the most expensive misquotes, with what each one actually leaves open.

"Good quality." Leaves every measurable characteristic open. Replace with named tolerances, or drop the phrase entirely — it adds nothing a tolerance list does not cover.

"Standard satin." Leaves the construction, the fibre, the weave and the finish open, and those four set the price. Replace with the construction and the composition.

"Red." Leaves the reference system, the reference, the tolerance and the approval method open. Replace with a full colour clause.

"Print our logo." Leaves the process, the artwork status, the colour count, the placement, the repeat and the coverage open — and the process alone can vary the price several-fold. Replace with a decoration clause.

"Same as last time." Assumes the previous specification still exists in the same form, that the buyer is the same person, and that nothing has been substituted since. Replace with a reference to the article number and the revision, plus the fields that have changed.

"As per sample." Leaves the question of which sample, in what form, and what it is evidence of. A swatch proves appearance, not construction; a production sample proves construction, not batch behaviour. Replace with an approval clause naming the sample type.

"Ready stock." Leaves the origin of the base ribbon open — whether it was woven for this order or bought in — and with it the colour depth, the certification status and the consistency across a multi-year programme. Replace with a construction and certification clause.

"Ship to our warehouse." Leaves the named consignee, the address, the Incoterm, the packaging requirement and the documentation requirement open, all of which become a customs or a delivery problem. Replace with a full delivery clause.

"No colour variation." Cannot be achieved and cannot be inspected. Replace with a stated tolerance against a stated method.

"Strong." Leaves the characteristic — tensile, abrasion, crocking, tear — open. Replace with a named test and grade.

"Subject to sample approval." Leaves the sample type, the approval form, the approver and the consequence of a mismatch open. Replace with a full approval clause.

"Details as per our previous order." Places the entire burden on memory across a supplier relationship that may outlast the people in it. Replace with an article number, a revision and a date.

Writing a Specification That Can Actually Be Inspected

The purpose of all fourteen fields collapses into one test: could an inspector who has never met you settle a dispute using only this document?

Make every clause observable. Replace every adjective with something that can be seen, measured or counted. "Soft" becomes a stiffness or thickness figure. "Vivid" becomes a colour tolerance. "Premium" becomes a construction and a composition. "Durable" becomes a test method and a grade. "Even selvedge" becomes a stated maximum deviation over a stated length.

Make every clause actionable. If a clause cannot be checked at the receiving warehouse or on the production line, it is context, not specification. It may still be worth writing — but put it in a note, not in the requirement.

Attach the numbers to the method. A tolerance without a method is not reproducible. ISO 105-C06 and "a wash test" are not the same requirement. A micrometer reading at a stated pressure and a stated number of measurement points is reproducible; "thickness" alone is not.

Keep it versioned. Put a revision number and a date on the document, reference it from the purchase order, and keep the revision history. Every change to a specification should be a new revision with a note, not an email. This prevents the silent drift that turns a two-year programme into something nobody can describe accurately.

Agree the mechanism before the need arises. Decide, while everything is fine, what happens when a lot misses: who inspects it, in what time frame, with what evidence, and who decides. A buyer who has decided this in advance has leverage and a calm process. A buyer deciding it after the container is at the port has neither.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a ribbon specification be?

Long enough that no meaningful characteristic is left to the factory's default assumption, and no longer than that. For a standard decorative ribbon sold into a retail programme, the fourteen fields described here typically fit on two sides of A4. What determines the length is not complexity but ambiguity: a technical jacquard programme for an automotive trim application may need three pages, while a one-colour screen-printed satin for a gift-box programme may need one. The test is whether an inspector who has never met the buyer could settle a dispute from the document alone. If yes, it is long enough. If no because a clause is missing, add the clause; if no because a clause is unmeasurable, rewrite it.

What is the difference between a ribbon specification and a purchase order?

A specification describes the product; a purchase order describes the commercial transaction. In practice many buyers merge them, and that is the source of a common problem: the purchase order gets amended to reflect a change the factory made, and the specification — the document that defines the product and the acceptance criteria — never gets updated. The result is an order that no longer matches any specification, and an inspection with no criteria to apply. The two should be separate documents that reference each other. The purchase order should carry the specification's article number and revision, so that any change to the product is automatically a change to the specification, which is reviewed, approved and versioned.

How tight should the tolerances be?

Tight enough that a good lot is unambiguously good and a bad lot is unambiguously bad, and no tighter than the process naturally delivers. A width tolerance tighter than the mill's own process capability generates a dispute on nearly every delivery, because some fraction of every shipment will fall outside it; a tolerance much looser than the product needs gives away quality the buyer paid for. Practical working figures for finished decorative ribbon are around plus or minus 0.5mm on width, plus or minus 5 per cent on weight per linear metre, and a colour difference value agreed against the approved strike-off. Narrower trims below 10mm often justify tighter width tolerance because the absolute variation is proportionally larger. The discipline is to set the tolerance at the point where it changes a decision about the product, not at the tightest number the factory can quote.

Should I specify colour by Pantone reference or by physical swatch?

By Pantone reference for solid colours, and by physical swatch where you are matching an existing product or a specific physical standard. A Pantone reference is a communication anchor that both sides can resolve independently, which makes it the right primary method for a new colour. A physical swatch is more accurate for matching an existing product, but it introduces a physical sample that must be controlled, labelled and stored, and it becomes ambiguous if the swatch is lost or if two suppliers are working from different copies. Where the programme runs across more than one mill, a Pantone reference plus a stated tolerance is the more robust basis, because every site resolves it the same way.

How much does a full ribbon specification cost to prepare?

Almost nothing in direct effort, and the savings are usually larger than the effort. The realistic time to write a complete specification for a standard ribbon programme, using the field structure in this guide and reusing clauses across SKUs, is a few hours for the first SKU and considerably less for each subsequent one because most fields are shared. The material cost is the factory's quotation time, which drops because a clear brief produces a single precise quote instead of three rounds of clarification. The larger return is in the disputes avoided: a specification that closes an ambiguity at the brief stage costs nothing, while the same ambiguity discovered at inspection costs a rework, a delay, a freight rebooking and a claim.

What tolerance should I set on ribbon width?

For most finished decorative ribbon, plus or minus 0.5mm against nominal is a workable figure that a competent mill can hold on a consistent production run. Narrower trims below 10mm often need a tighter absolute tolerance because the natural variation of the process is proportionally larger relative to the width — a 0.5mm tolerance on a 6mm ribbon is more than 8 per cent of the width and is visible on a finished bow. Wide ribbon above 50mm can usually be held within plus or minus 1mm without difficulty. Two things matter more than the exact number: stating whether the width is measured on finished or greige ribbon, and stating the measurement method, since width measured taut but not relaxed will not match width measured after conditioning.
