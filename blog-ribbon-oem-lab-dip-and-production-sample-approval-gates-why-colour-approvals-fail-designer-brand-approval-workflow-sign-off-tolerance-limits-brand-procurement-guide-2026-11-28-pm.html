Lab Dip and Production Sample Approval: Why Colour Approvals Fail

Published: November 28, 2026 (13:00)  |  By Smith Ribbon  |  B2B Ribbon Colour Approval and Sample Management
The core idea of this guide: a colour approval is not a moment, it is a chain. Every hand-off between the lab, the dye house, the decorator and the brand is a place where the colour can drift. Most colour failures are not dye house failures. They are failures of the approval chain, where nobody with the authority to accept a deviation ever looked at it.

Two Approvals, Not One: Lab Dip and Production Sample

The most consequential structural mistake in a colour approval process is treating it as a single approval. In practice a new ribbon colour passes through two gates that test different things, and a programme that collapses them into one is losing information at the point where it is most valuable.

The first gate is the lab dip. The supplier prepares a small piece, typically one or two metres, on a laboratory dyeing machine using a hand-prepared recipe. It exists to answer a narrow question: is this recipe in the right region of colour space, and is the shade direction correct. It is a fast, cheap, low-commitment way of ruling out a recipe that is nowhere near the target. Its weakness is equally well defined, and it is severe. A lab machine has a small liquor volume, a very high liquor-to-fabric ratio, a slow and easily controlled heating curve, and typically no turbulence comparable to production equipment. Scale changes all of these.

The second gate is the production sample, dyed on production equipment from the same recipe family. It answers the question the lab dip cannot: what does this colour actually do when it is made at scale. This is the gate that matters commercially, because the bulk lot will be made on production equipment and it is the production sample that predicts it.

When a programme runs both gates as one approval, the failure modes are predictable. Either the buyer treats the lab dip as final and is surprised by the bulk, or the buyer treats the lab dip as meaningless and declines to comment on it, in which case the supplier has had a round of dyeing with no useful feedback and both parties have spent a week for nothing. The correct posture is that the lab dip is a direction check that must be passed, and the production sample is the acceptance check that must be signed.

A third gate sometimes applies in practice, and it is legitimate: the decorated sample. Where the ribbon will be printed, embossed, metallised or finished, the decorated article must also be approved, because decoration shifts perceived colour in ways that are not predictable from the base. A print that is approved on the greige ribbon may not be acceptable on the decorated article, and the reverse is also true. What must never happen is the reverse ordering, where the decorated sample is approved and the base is assumed to be fine because it looks acceptable underneath. A finish can mask a base colour problem that becomes visible as the finish wears off, and a buyer who discovers that in the field has no recourse.

Why Approved Colour Comes Back Different

When an approved colour arrives in bulk and does not match, the instinct is to blame the dye house. Sometimes that is correct. More often the root cause is a difference in process conditions that was never a variable anyone controlled, because the lab and the production line were never required to match each other on it.

The variables that matter most, and that differ most between lab and production, are these.

  • Liquor-to-fabric ratio. The amount of dye bath relative to the weight of fabric. A high ratio in the lab means the dye is more concentrated relative to the fibre, and uptake and shade are more sensitive to small changes in timing.
  • Heating rate and curve. Temperature rise in the lab is typically slow and linear, because the operator is watching a small vessel. On production equipment the rise is governed by steam pressure, machine load and the rate at which the operator opens and closes valves.
  • Turbulence and liquor movement. How the fabric is moved through the bath. This affects the accessibility of dye to fibre, and it is one of the reasons exhaustion on production equipment is not identical to exhaustion in the lab.
  • Dye affinity and the choice of reducers and assistants. A recipe scaled from lab to bulk may need different amounts of assistant depending on the equipment and the liquor ratio.
  • Batch size and loading geometry. A roll packed differently in a large machine dyes differently, particularly in the shaded areas of a tightly wound package.
  • Ambient and water quality. The water itself is a variable, and it varies between plants and between seasons.

None of these are exotic. They are the ordinary conditions of dyeing. The point is that a programme which has never asked a supplier to align lab and bulk conditions has a colour process built on an assumption that was never true, and it will find out on a bulk lot rather than on a sample.

There are three practical responses, in increasing order of cost and effectiveness. The first is to require the supplier to submit, with the lab dip, a statement of the dye recipe and the machine and process conditions used, so that at least the divergence is documented. The second is to require the production sample as a matter of course, so that a divergence is discovered on a lot that can still be re-dyed. The third is to specify that the production sample is dyed on the same equipment and with the same process conditions as the eventual bulk lot, which is entirely practical where the dye house runs both, and which is the only approach that makes a lab dip reliable enough to approve on its own.

Who Has the Authority to Approve

Colour approval fails in a distinctive way: the colour is approved by someone who could not have known what they were approving, or by someone who had the authority but was not the person who would be accountable for the outcome. Both are common and neither is visible until the goods are on the shelf.

The remedy is to name the approval authority in writing before the first sample is produced, and to name a deputy for when the primary approver is unavailable. A short paragraph in the purchase specification is sufficient, and it should cover three things.

First, who approves a lab dip and who approves a production sample. These are often the same person and they are sometimes not, because a lab dip is a colour question and a production sample is a question about the colour plus the decoration, the width, the finish and the handling. Second, the number of people whose agreement is required before a sample is deemed approved. In a programme with a designer, a quality lead and a regulatory reviewer, it may be appropriate to require all of them. In a smaller programme it may be one. What is not acceptable is an implicit number, because an implicit number means that whoever answered the email approved it.

Third, and least often written down, whether the approver is being asked to accept a colour or to accept a colour against a standard. These are different acts with different implications. Accepting a colour because it looks right on a screen in a designer office is a subjective judgement that will be reopened later. Accepting it against a defined reference under a defined lighting condition is a technical judgement that can be repeated and defended. The second is worth the extra effort of writing down.

One further point is worth raising with suppliers early, because it is much cheaper to fix in advance than later: what happens to an approval when the person who gave it has left the organisation. Programmes lose the thread of colour decisions through staff turnover and then find that nobody can explain why a particular shade was chosen. The colour book described later in this guide is what prevents that, and it is a much cheaper insurance than it looks.

The Lighting Condition Is Part of the Specification

Colour matching is a comparative judgement, and a comparative judgement is only as good as the conditions in which it is made. This sounds obvious once stated and is nonetheless the most frequently omitted element of a colour specification.

The mechanics are straightforward. A ribbon is usually specified against a target that was itself established under some particular viewing condition, often the light of a design studio during the day. If the bulk is assessed under a warm retail light, the relative contribution of red and blue to the perceived colour changes, and a colour that matched under north daylight may appear warmer and more saturated than the standard. The reverse happens under cool light. The magnitude varies with the hue, and it is generally larger for the more saturated colours, which are exactly the colours that brands are most likely to specify by name.

For a programme with retail exposure, the recommendation follows directly. Specify the viewing condition, and specify it in a form the mill can reproduce. The recognised textile illuminants give a common language: D65 represents average daylight and is the standard reference for most material work, TL84 simulates a typical warm shop fluorescent and is widely used for retail simulation, CWF represents a cool retail fluorescent, and there are others for specific retail conditions. Where the goods will be sold under a particular light, assessing under a light that approximates it is more useful than assessing under the most neutral light available, because the question being answered is not "is this the right colour" but "is this the right colour where it will be seen".

Two practical instructions follow from this and are worth building into the procedure. The first is that samples should be assessed as physical objects against a physical reference, not as images on a monitor, and never against a memory of an earlier colour. A monitor is unreliable, a photograph inherits the camera's white balance, and a human memory of a shade is close to worthless after a few weeks. Where a photograph is used for communication between a brand and a mill, it should be treated as a prompt for a conversation and not as a reference standard.

The second is that the viewing condition should be stated in the same breath as the colour standard, so that when the two disagree, it is clear which one governs. In practice most disagreements about colour are not disagreements about colour at all. They are disagreements about the conditions under which the colour was being judged.

Setting a Measurable Colour Tolerance

A colour specification that says the bulk must match the approved sample is not enforceable, because it leaves open every question that matters when the two do not match: which sample, seen how, measured how, judged against what threshold, and who decides.

Two measurement approaches are in normal use, and the right approach depends on whether the programme can justify the cost of an instrument and the training to use it.

Instrumental measurement uses a spectrophotometer to measure the spectral reflectance of the sample and computes a color difference value, conventionally delta-E. The standard formulation used in the textile industry is CIEDE2000, which is a better model of perceptual difference than the earlier CIELAB-based formulae, particularly in the blue region and for low-chroma colours. A spectrophotometer is not expensive, and for a programme running many colour approvals it pays for itself quickly, because it turns a subjective negotiation into a number that both parties can check. A commonly used working threshold is delta-E of 1.0 or less for an acceptable match, with values up to around 1.5 sometimes accepted where visual assessment under controlled lighting agrees, and higher values treated as a fail. The right threshold depends on the colour and on the product, and a saturated shade on a large visible surface justifies a tighter threshold than a pale shade in a concealed position.

Visual assessment uses a standard grey scale to grade the degree of difference and a standard light box to provide a defined viewing condition. It is how most textile colour assessment has traditionally been done, and it remains entirely valid. Its weakness is that it depends on the observer, and its strength is that it does not require the observer to operate an instrument correctly.

Effective programmes use both and specify which governs. A useful formulation is that the instrument reading is the primary criterion, with visual assessment under a stated illuminant as the confirming method, and that in the case of disagreement between the two the visual assessment under the agreed light box prevails, because the commercial question is what the product will look like to a customer. Whatever the formulation, the specification must state the method, the threshold, and the consequence of exceeding it, because a tolerance without a consequence is not a tolerance.

The Bulk Shade Approval Nobody Schedules

Of all the gates described in this guide, the bulk shade approval is the one most often skipped, and it is the one with the best economics. It should be a standing requirement on every dye lot, and it costs nothing more than a length of ribbon and a few days in the schedule.

What it does is give the buyer a rejection point that still has value. Without it, the first moment a buyer can assess the colour of the delivered goods is when the goods have been received into the warehouse. At that point the rejection is expensive in the worst way: the ribbon has been paid for, shipped, insured, received, counted, and possibly allocated to a production order that is now waiting. Rejecting it there is possible but painful, and the pressure to accept it is strong, particularly when the rest of the shipment is otherwise good and the defect is colour that nobody is quite sure is out of tolerance.

With a bulk shade approval, the same decision happens in a materially better place. The buyer receives a length from the actual production dye lot, identified by its lot number, and accepts or rejects the lot while it is still at the dye house. A rejection at that point is a re-dye, not a return. The cost difference is an order of magnitude, and the reputational difference is larger still, because a re-dyed lot never reaches the customer's hands and never appears in a review.

The sample needs to meet a few requirements to do its job. It should be a continuous length sufficient to assess the full width, which for a wide ribbon means at least a metre. It should be long enough to allow a cutting trial and a folding trial, since colour approval is partly a question of how the material behaves under light and shadow when creased. It should carry the lot identification, so that the acceptance is unambiguously attached to that lot and to no other. And it should travel with the shipment rather than ahead of it, so that the assessment and the goods are not separated in time.

One caution. A bulk shade approval that is granted verbally and recorded afterwards in an email is much weaker than one granted on a physical sample and recorded on a form, because the record becomes the evidence that the lot was accepted before it was shipped. Get the acceptance in writing, against a named lot, before the goods leave the factory. That single change converts the most common late-stage colour dispute into a routine pre-shipment check.

Approval Rounds and How to Stop Them Drifting

Every approval process has an implicit stopping point, and in programmes where it is not written down, the stopping point is wherever the schedule pressure happens to push it. This is the mechanism behind most slow colour programmes.

The typical drift looks like this. A first sample arrives and is close but not right. The correction is small. A second sample arrives, and against the second sample the difference is harder to see because the eye has already adjusted. A third sample arrives with a slightly different character again, because each round was made as a correction to the previous round rather than to the original standard. By the third or fourth round the colour is no longer a deliberate choice. It is an accumulation of compromises, and nobody can say what it was supposed to be.

Two rules prevent this. The first is that corrections are always made against the original standard, never against the previous sample. If sample three is compared to sample two rather than to the approved reference, the process drifts by definition. This needs to be stated explicitly in the correspondence, because the natural instinct on both sides is to treat the most recent physical sample as the reference, since it is the one physically in front of them.

The second rule is a hard limit on rounds, with a defined consequence when it is reached. Two rounds for a new colour is normal, and three is generous. Beyond that, something is wrong with the recipe, the communication, or the target, and continuing to sample is not going to fix it. The consequence should be a review rather than another round, typically a conversation between the supplier's technical lead and the brand's approver, with the target re-examined.

There is also a variant worth anticipating, because it is common in programmes with a strong design function. The request for one more small adjustment arrives late, close to the shipment date, and it is framed as an improvement rather than a change. The right response at that point is procedural rather than personal: the rule about the reference not changing after the production sample stage is not there to obstruct design intent, it is there to ensure that whatever colour is finally approved is the colour that was actually checked against a production sample. If the change is accepted without that, the programme has produced an approved colour that no production sample ever demonstrated, and the next reorder will start this entire process again from a different position.

Keeping a Colour Record That Is Still Usable in a Year

The approval conversation is over the moment the sample is signed. What determines whether the programme runs well over two years is the record, and the record is usually the weakest part of the system.

The central requirement is a physical reference. A retained swatch or a short length of the approved goods, stored away from light and heat, is what the eye will be compared against when a reorder arrives eight months later. A photograph cannot do this job. Screens vary, photographs inherit the camera's white balance and the ambient light of the day they were taken, and a compressed image that has been copied between systems several times is not a colour reference at all. The physical reference should be large enough to be assessed comfortably and should be labelled with the colour identifier and the date it was taken from production.

Alongside it, the record should carry enough to make a comparison meaningful without asking anyone. The colour identifier and the shade name as the programme uses it. The dye recipe or lot reference where the supplier provides one. The viewing condition and illuminant used for approval. The instrument measurement and delta-E value, if an instrument was used. The date, and the signature or name of the person who approved it. And a note of the application, so that someone picking up the file in two years knows whether the swatch was approved for a 15 mm bow or a 40 mm display ribbon.

Physically, a colour book with one page per colour is the simplest structure that works, and it scales well enough for programmes with dozens of active colours. The alternative, a shared digital file with photographs, is genuinely useful for communication and search, and genuinely unsafe as the sole record. Keeping both is the right answer, with the physical book as the authority and the digital file as the index.

Two operational details make the difference between a record that works and one that does not. The first is that the book must have a single owner, because a colour book without an owner becomes a box of swatches in a drawer. The second is that it should be reviewed periodically, with expired or superseded shades archived rather than deleted, because the history of a colour programme is genuinely useful when a customer asks why two shades that look similar are in the book. The cost of maintaining this is perhaps an hour a month. The cost of not maintaining it is an argument with a factory about whether the shade on the floor is the shade that was approved, which is exactly the argument this entire guide exists to prevent.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. The lab dip was approved, so why did the bulk dye lot come out different?

Because a lab dip and a bulk dye lot are not the same experiment. A lab dip is a small piece, usually a metre or two of fabric, dyed in a laboratory on a small dyeing machine using a hand-prepared recipe. A bulk lot is hundreds or thousands of metres dyed on production equipment with a scaled recipe, a different liquor-to-fabric ratio, a different heating profile and a different dyeing assistant. The variables that a small lab cannot fully reproduce are the ratio of dye to water, the rate at which temperature rises, the pressure and turbulence inside the vessel, and the number of times the fabric is moved. A lab dip approved to a standard tells you the recipe is close. It does not promise the bulk lot will match. This gap is where most colour approval failures live, and it is why a good approval process treats the lab dip and the production sample as two separate approvals with two separate criteria rather than as one approval with a formality attached.

2. Who should have the authority to approve colour, and why does it matter?

In a well-run programme, colour approval authority is explicitly named, and it usually needs to sit with more than one party. The person who signs off should be the person who will be accountable for the result on the shelf, which is typically a brand or design lead, and in a regulated category it may also need a quality or regulatory signature. What matters commercially is that the authority is named before the sample arrives rather than negotiated after it does. Programmes fail at this point because the sample goes to whoever is easiest to reach, that person approves on a screen in daylight, and the approval is later discovered to have been made by someone without the authority to make it. The supplier has then produced to a specification that nobody with the power to accept the deviation actually reviewed. Naming the approver, and naming a deputy for when that person is unavailable, removes this failure mode at essentially no cost.

3. What lighting conditions should colour be assessed in, and does it really matter?

It matters more than most buyers expect, because colour matching is a comparative judgement and a comparative judgement depends on the viewing conditions. A ribbon that matches perfectly under daylight can look noticeably different under warm retail lighting, and a colour that matches under a warm shop light can appear cooler and duller under daylight. The practical recommendation is to specify a lighting condition explicitly, and the standard industry choices are a controlled daylight illuminant such as D65 or TL84 for a shop light, or a specific warm source such as CWF or an incandescent-equivalent for retail simulation. Assessment should be done on the sample against a retained reference panel or a physical production sample of the approved goods, not against a screenshot on a monitor, and never against a memory of a previous colour. Where a product will be sold in a specific retail environment, assessing under a light that approximates that environment is more useful than assessing under the most neutral condition available.

4. What tolerance should a buyer set for colour, and how should it be measured?

Set the tolerance as a measured value in a standard unit rather than as a description, and there are two practical approaches. The first is instrumental, using a spectrophotometer to compute a delta-E figure between the production lot and the approved standard, which is objective, repeatable and fast. A commonly used acceptance threshold for textile brands is a delta-E of 1.0 or less for a match, with values up to around 1.5 sometimes accepted under visual assessment and a few thresholds beyond that treated as a fail, though the right number depends on the colour and the product. The second is visual, using a standard grey scale for assessing the degree of difference and a standard light box for the viewing conditions. Most effective programmes use both, and importantly they define which one governs. A specification that says only colour must match an approved sample is not enforceable, because it leaves open which instrument, which illuminant, and which threshold applies.

5. Should a bulk shade be approved separately from the lab dip, and what should it contain?

Yes, and this is the single change that most improves approval outcomes. The bulk shade approval sample is a length of ribbon from the actual production dye lot, and it should arrive with the bulk shipment, not with the lab dip. It gives the buyer a chance to accept the specific lot that will be delivered before the goods are consumed, and to reject a lot while it can still be re-dyed rather than after it has been cut into pieces and assembled into finished product. The sample should carry the lot identification and should be a continuous length long enough to be assessed across its full width and to allow a few cutting and folding trials. A buyer who accepts a bulk shade and then rejects the same shade when the goods arrive has created the most expensive form of colour dispute available, because the rejection arrives at the point where it cannot be fixed.

6. How many approval rounds should a programme allow, and what happens when it overruns?

Most programmes need two rounds for a new colour: a lab dip stage and a production sample stage, sometimes three if the product requires a decorated sample as well as an undecorated one. The important part is not the number of rounds but what happens when the limit is reached. An approval process with no defined stopping point drifts, because each round generates another small change and another round, and the programme ends up with a colour that nobody chose deliberately. Set a rule in advance: for example, the approved reference colour does not change after the production sample stage, and any change at that point requires a new production sample and a schedule review. This is uncomfortable at the point where someone asks for one more small adjustment, which is precisely why it needs to be written down before anyone is in a hurry.

7. How should the colour approval record be kept so it is usable months later?

Keep a physical reference, not just a record. A retained swatch or a length of the approved goods, stored away from light, is what the eye will be compared against in eight months when a reorder arrives, and a photograph on a screen is not a substitute because screens vary and photographs shift with white balance. Alongside the physical reference, the record should carry the colour identifier, the dye lot or recipe reference, the lighting condition used, the measurement result if an instrument was used, the date, and the signature of the person who approved it. A colour book maintained for the programme, with one page per colour containing the swatch, the data and the signature, is a low-cost document that prevents a great deal of argument and it makes onboarding a new buyer or a new factory contact straightforward.

8. What is the most common mistake buyers make in colour approval, and what should they do instead?

The most common mistake is treating colour approval as a visual formality performed once, in a hurry, by whoever is nearest, rather than as a controlled process with a named approver, a defined lighting condition, a measured tolerance and a retained reference. The second most common is approving the decorated article without approving the underlying dyed ribbon, which means a print or a finish can shift the perceived colour and the approval is made against an effect rather than against the material. The practical alternative is a short written procedure with four elements: a named approver and a deputy, a defined viewing condition, a stated tolerance with the method to measure it, and a rule that the production shade is approved separately from the lab dip. Nothing about that is complicated. The reason it is not always in place is that it takes an hour to write and saves weeks.
