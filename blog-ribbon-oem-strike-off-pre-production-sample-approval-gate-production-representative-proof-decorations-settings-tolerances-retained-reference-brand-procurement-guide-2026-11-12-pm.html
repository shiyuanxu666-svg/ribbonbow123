The gate most buyers never name

There is a moment in every custom ribbon programme where the artwork is approved, the colour is approved, the price is agreed, and the order is officially "ready to run". Everyone in the room believes the programme is de-risked. And yet a meaningful share of programmes still arrive at the buyer's warehouse with a defect that was visible, printable and preventable five weeks earlier.

The reason is almost never a supplier cutting corners. It is that the buyer and the supplier were each using the word "approved" to mean something different, and neither of them wrote it down.

What one side called a "sample" the other side called "the sample". What one side treated as a production-representative article the other side treated as a rough visual reference. And then the production run happened, and the ribbon that arrived was 4 mm narrower, the print was a shade off the approved swatch, and the edge had a drawdown that only shows up under tension.

Industry practice has a name for the controlled version of this step: the strike-off. In print and textile decoration, a strike-off is a production-representative proof run, made on production equipment, using production materials, by production operators, through production settings. It is not a sample. It is a rehearsal.

A sample tells you whether an idea is acceptable. A strike-off tells you whether this machine, this material, this operator and these settings can repeat it. For a buyer running a branded ribbon programme across dozens of SKUs and multiple order cycles, that is the difference between a one-off conversation and a repeatable system.

This guide covers what a strike-off actually is, what it must include for it to be worth anything, the four approval layers that sit on top of it, the documentation that makes it enforceable, and the failure modes that show up when the gate is skipped.

Sample vs strike-off: the distinction that carries the whole programme

The word "sample" is used loosely enough in sourcing that it has lost its discriminating power. Four distinct artefact types get called a sample, and they each answer a different question.

ArtefactProduced byAnswers which questionCost
Development swatchHand-built or off-line sample loom, small quantity"Is the construction and colour direction acceptable?"Low
Lab dipDip dyeing at lab scale"Is the dye formula and colour match on target?"Low
Production sampleOff-line or low-speed production setup"Is the decorated article representative of what production will make?"Medium
Strike-offThe actual production machine, production settings, production operator"Can this machine repeat this specification reliably?"Medium-high

The critical point: only the strike-off is predictive. A lab dip tells you the dye chemistry is right. It tells you nothing about drawdown tension, nothing about edge quality under a running loom, nothing about how the print registration behaves on a webbing at speed, and nothing about whether the operator can hold the specification across a full run.

Many buyers think they are approving a strike-off when they are actually approving a development swatch. The gap is invisible on day one and expensive on day thirty.

The eight conditions a valid strike-off must satisfy

A strike-off only carries authority if it is genuinely production-representative. If any one of the following conditions is broken, the artefact downgrades into an illustrative sample and the approval it carries should not be treated as a release to produce.

  1. Production equipment. Run on the same machine type that will run the order. A proof printed on a flatbed press that will be produced on a rotary or digital line is not a strike-off; it is a mock-up.
  2. Production materials. The actual ribbon article — same construction, same width, same face and back, same substrate, same finish. Printing a strike-off on a cheaper stock to save cost invalidates the result on decoration adhesion and edge behaviour.
  3. Production settings. Same tension, speed, temperature, pressure and drying parameters that will be used on the order. This is where the real information lives.
  4. Production operator. Where the operation is operator-sensitive — screen printing, hand-folding, hot-foiling, tying — the strike-off should be run by the team or shift that will run the order, or at minimum by a team working to the same written method.
  5. Full decoration set. The strike-off must carry the complete decoration package. Approving the print but not the edge finish, the edge treatment or the trim length leaves a whole family of defects undiscovered.
  6. Declared length. A measured, stated length — not "looks about right". Length tolerance is where a surprising number of programmes quietly go wrong.
  7. Retained and referenceable. The approved strike-off is a physical asset. It should be labelled with a unique reference, retained by both parties, and available for comparison against the production lot when a question arises.
  8. Signed by a named authority. Approval needs an owner, not a general assent. "Looks good to me" in a chat thread is not a specification release.

Rule of thumb: if any of the eight conditions cannot be met for a given SKU, write that SKU down as a development-article approval and carry the residual risk explicitly into the production order. Do not let it masquerade as a strike-off in your documentation. The distinction is what protects you when the first bulk delivery arrives.

Four approval layers above the strike-off

In practice the strike-off sits inside a wider approval structure, and buyers who understand the whole stack handle objections much faster, because they know which layer a question belongs to.

Layer 1 — Specification approval

The construction, width, material, weight, finish and decoration method are agreed in writing before anything is made. This layer answers "what are we making". It is a paper approval, but it is the layer that most disputes return to, because a strike-off cannot fix an ambiguous specification.

Layer 2 — Colour and material approval

The lab dip or colour standard confirms the colour direction. This answers "is the colour right". It is necessary but not sufficient, because a correct dye on the wrong article, or on the right article with a different finishing route, will still not match the final product.

Layer 3 — Strike-off approval

The production-representative proof. This answers "will it come out this way". This is the layer most programmes skip or downgrade without noticing.

Layer 4 — Pre-shipment / final inspection approval

Confirmation that the finished production lot matches the approved strike-off. This answers "did we get what was approved". It is a comparison against a retained reference, which is precisely why Layer 3 matters.

The dependency chain: Layer 4 is only as strong as the reference it compares against. If the strike-off was never made, or was made on non-production equipment, then the pre-shipment inspection has nothing authoritative to compare to — and both sides fall back on subjective judgement, which is exactly how a dispute starts.

What to specify on a strike-off request

A strike-off request that says "please send us a sample of the new ribbon" will receive something generic. A request that names conditions will receive something useful. Below is the structure we work to, and it is the same structure we ask our customers to use with their own suppliers.

The documentation that makes the gate enforceable

An approval that only exists in an email thread decays. Over a programme that runs twelve months with a dozen SKUs, email threads lose attachment versions, forwards truncate context, and the buyer who approved is replaced by someone who never saw the conversation. The approval record needs to be a structured artefact with a small number of fields, and it needs to be attached to the purchase order rather than stored separately.

What we recommend is a one-page strike-off record that carries, at minimum:

  • Programme reference, SKU, artwork revision and colour standard reference
  • Date the strike-off was produced, and the machine or line it was produced on
  • Declared length and measured width
  • The approval decision, the approver's name and role, and the date
  • Any deviations that were accepted — stated explicitly, with a reason
  • Retention location and a retention period

The deviations line is the one that matters most. A strike-off that differs from the specification and is approved anyway is a legitimate and common event — it just has to be written down. An unrecorded deviation is a liability that surfaces at the next audit, at the next inspection, or at the worst possible moment in a customer conversation.

How long does a strike-off add to the programme?

Buyers planning a seasonal programme need to know the cost of this gate in days, because in a compressed calendar it is the step most likely to be dropped.

In our own operation, the realistic added time from strike-off request to written approval is 5 to 8 working days for a single-decoration article, provided the artwork revision and colour standard are settled and the request reaches us complete. A multi-decoration article — for example print plus foil plus edge finish — typically takes 7 to 12 working days, because each decoration has its own setup and its own evidence.

Two things make the difference between five days and fifteen. The first is whether the request arrives complete, because an incomplete request will not be refused — it will be worked on, and the clock keeps running while we ask questions. The second is the number of revision rounds; each round of artwork change resets the decoration setup, and two rounds is normal, three is a signal that the brief was not agreed before it was sent.

Five failure modes when the gate is skipped

1. The approval that was really a mood

Someone looked at an off-line sample, said it was fine, and the programme moved on. Nothing was wrong with the sample — it simply had no predictive power. The failure surfaces as a mismatch between the approval and the delivery, and because there was no strike-off, the buyer has no reference to argue from.

2. The artwork revision drift

The buyer approved revision A. Production ran revision D, because the file that reached the factory was the last one in the folder rather than the last one approved. A strike-off solves this indirectly and very effectively, because the strike-off carries the artwork revision number physically on it — you compare what you hold against what was approved and the drift is obvious.

3. The decoration that behaves differently on production webbing

Print adhesion, foil blocking, edge definition and hand-feel all change when the substrate, the tension and the speed change. A proof on a flatbed tells you almost nothing about a roll of 19 mm grosgrain moving at production speed.

4. The tolerance nobody wrote down

The strike-off carries a width, a length and a weight. If those are declared with tolerances, the production lot has a measurable specification to be checked against. If they are not, every incoming inspection becomes an argument about what "correct" means.

5. The seasonal programme that runs out of time

When the peak calendar compresses, the strike-off is often the first step sacrificed, because it produces no physical benefit for the buyer and appears purely as delay. The result is a delivery that arrives either late or wrong — and for a seasonal programme those two outcomes are frequently the same outcome.

What we would tell a buyer who can only add one step: if the programme has a decoration method that is operator- or equipment-sensitive, and if the delivery deadline is fixed, then the strike-off is not optional. Add it to the schedule and protect it. Everything else on this list can be worked around; a production release made without a production-representative proof cannot.

The practical sequence we recommend

  1. Freeze the specification. Construction, width, material, weight, finish and decoration method agreed in writing and attached to the purchase order.
  2. Approve the colour separately. Lab dip or colour standard approved and referenced by its own code.
  3. Issue a complete strike-off request. Using the field structure above, with artwork revision, colour reference, declared length and named approver.
  4. Receive and review against the specification. Not "does it look right" — measured against the agreed specification, item by item.
  5. Record the decision. Approved, approved with stated deviations, or not approved. Signed, dated, with the deviations written down.
  6. Retain the reference. Both parties keep a labelled strike-off, and the programme record points to where it is.
  7. Release production against the record. The production order references the strike-off record and the retained reference number.
  8. Inspect the finished lot against the same reference. Which is only possible because steps 5 and 6 happened.

None of this is exotic. It is a small amount of structure, applied consistently, that converts a decoration that is approximately right into a decoration that is reliably right — and it is the difference between a ribbon programme that runs on trust and one that runs on evidence.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement teams most often ask us about ribbon sample approval: what a strike-off is, how long it takes, whether it is needed for every SKU, and how to record it so it holds up later. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What is the difference between a strike-off and a production sample?

A strike-off is produced on the actual production machine, with production materials, at production settings, by production operators, through the production process. A production sample is made off-line or at low speed and is representative of the decorated article but not predictive of the production run. A strike-off answers whether the line can repeat the specification; a production sample answers what the article looks like. Only the strike-off carries predictive authority.

2. Is a lab dip the same as a strike-off?

No, and this is the most common confusion. A lab dip confirms the dye formula and colour match at lab scale. It tells you nothing about drawdown tension, edge quality under a running loom, print registration at production speed, or whether an operator can hold the specification across a full run. A lab dip is normally an upstream approval layer to the strike-off, not a substitute for it.

3. How long does a strike-off add to my production lead time?

In our factory, a single-decoration article with artwork and colour already approved typically runs 5 to 8 working days from request to written approval. Multiple decorations usually take 7 to 12 working days, and a new colour development running alongside adds roughly a week. These are our own planning figures for our own article range, not a contractual commitment — the actual window depends on the article, the decoration and the current schedule.

4. What should a strike-off request include to get a useful result?

At minimum: a programme reference, the SKU, the construction, the decoration method, the artwork revision with its file name and date, the colour standard reference, an explicit instruction that the strike-off be run on production equipment with production materials at production settings, a declared length, a named approval authority, and retention instructions. An incomplete request will not be refused — it will be worked on, and the approval clock keeps running while the questions are answered.

5. Do we need a strike-off for every SKU in the programme?

Not necessarily, and a good supplier should say so. The gate earns its cost where the decoration is equipment-sensitive or operator-sensitive — screen print, hot foil, hand-folding, hand-tying — and where the delivery deadline is fixed. For simpler articles, an approved production sample against a written specification is usually adequate. We will tell you which is which for your specific SKU list rather than applying the same process to everything.

6. What happens if the strike-off differs from the original specification?

That is a normal and legitimate outcome, and it should be recorded rather than treated as a failure. Approve the strike-off with the deviation stated explicitly, with a reason, and the deviation becomes part of the approved specification. What creates problems downstream is an unrecorded deviation, because the production lot is then compared against a document that was never true.

7. Can we approve the strike-off by email or photo?

A photograph can confirm appearance but not measurable properties. Width, length, weight, hand-feel, edge quality and decoration registration under physical conditions cannot be assessed from an image. If the programme is physically delivered, we would always recommend that the retained strike-off reaches your own hands so your incoming inspection and our production line work from the same physical reference.

8. How long should the approved strike-off be retained?

We recommend retaining the reference for the duration of the programme plus one full repeat cycle, because a strike-off is most useful when a repeat order is being placed and you want to confirm that the production line is still set to the same conditions. For seasonal programmes, retaining the reference across the full season plus the following repeat is a sensible minimum. In any case, it should be labelled with a unique reference and its location recorded in the programme file.
