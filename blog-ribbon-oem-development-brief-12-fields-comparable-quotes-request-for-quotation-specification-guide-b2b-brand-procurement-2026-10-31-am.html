Ribbon quotes are notoriously hard to compare. Two factories send back numbers for what sounds like the same product, and the prices differ by thirty percent. One quote includes tooling, the other treats it as a one-off that will be amortised across the next three years. One quotes satin ribbon; the other quotes polyester that will look like satin for about six weeks. Neither factory has done anything wrong. Both were answering a brief that did not contain the information needed to answer it.

This is the core problem with OEM ribbon sourcing that most buyers only discover after their third quotation round: the quote is not wrong, the question was incomplete. The factory priced what you told it. What you told it was a photograph, a colour name and a width, and a photograph does not carry the information that determines cost.

At Smith Ribbon, in Xiamen since 2004, we have quoted and produced private-label ribbon programmes for brand owners across more than fifty countries. We have also read a great many incoming briefs from buyers who then wondered why they were receiving quotes they could not reconcile. This guide is about the twelve fields that need to be in a ribbon development brief before you send it out, what each of those fields actually changes in the price, and how to read the responses you get back.

Why Two Quotes for "The Same Ribbon" Differ by 30 Percent

Before we get into the brief itself, it is worth understanding the mechanism. A ribbon price is not one number. It is the sum of several decisions, each of which can move the total significantly:

  • Material selection — the yarn itself. Polyester satin, polyester grosgrain, organza, velvet, cotton, bamboo fibre, RPET recycled polyester. The gap between a standard polyester satin and a matte or specialty-finish satin can be substantial, and both will be described as "satin" in a casual brief.
  • Width and construction — wider ribbon uses proportionally more yarn per metre, and construction density (ends per inch, picks per inch) determines how much material sits in a given width.
  • Decoration method — printed, solid colour, metallic foil, jacquard woven, screen print, heat transfer. Jacquard is woven into the fabric; printing is applied on top. They are different cost structures and different lead times.
  • Colour matching — whether you supply a Pantone code, a physical swatch, or ask the factory to develop a colour from scratch. The third option is a development cost plus a risk that the first production lot will not match the sample exactly.
  • Finishing — edge treatment (selvedge, wire-edge, raw edge), winding method, width tolerance, whether the ribbon is cut to length and folded, or supplied on rolls.
  • Packaging — spool format, carton quantity, whether each spool is individually bagged, whether barcodes and labels are applied, and whether co-packing with other components is required.

A brief that specifies only three of these six will produce quotes that differ because the factories are quietly filling the gaps with their own defaults — and their defaults are not the same. Two factories can both be doing excellent work and still be pricing genuinely different products.

The Twelve Fields Every Ribbon Development Brief Needs

1. Construction and material, named precisely

Do not write "satin ribbon" or "grosgrain". Write "100% polyester satin, 2.5 cm (1 inch) width, 120 denier, filament count 36/2, lustrous face, heat-set". Every one of those words removes a decision from the factory and puts it under your control. "Polyester satin" covers a material range wide enough that a good factory will ask you to choose; the risk is that a rushed factory will choose for you and you will find out at the sample stage.

2. Width, and width tolerance

Nominal width plus tolerance. A 25 mm ribbon will land somewhere between 24 mm and 26 mm if nobody specifies, and that variance affects both the yield of your finished product and the appearance on shelf. A stated tolerance of plus or minus 0.5 mm tells a factory you are serious and it becomes part of the acceptance criteria rather than a surprise at inspection.

3. Decoration method — and whether it is fixed

State the method. "Printed with your artwork in up to 6 spot colours, repeat length 30 cm, satin face printed on the reverse" is a brief. "Printed" is a wish. If you are open on method, say so explicitly and ask for the factory to propose two options with pricing for each — that comparison is often worth more than a week of your own research.

4. Colour reference and approval route

Give a Pantone code (Pantone 19-4052 TCX for a solid reference) and state what it means in your process: if the ribbon is solid-dyed, the Pantone is a target with a tolerance; if it is printed, the factory will match the print to the Pantone. Also state the approval route: do you approve a lab dip (dyed swatch), a printed strike-off, or a woven sample? Naming the route removes the single most common cause of a slipped schedule — the factory sending the wrong sample type and both sides waiting three weeks for the right one.

5. Material certification requirements

If you are selling into a retailer's supply chain, they will ask. If you are selling into a children's product, a skin-contact product, or a food-contact application, they will definitely ask. State which certifications the finished ribbon must carry: OEKO-TEX Standard 100, GRS or GOTS for recycled content, FSC for paper components, ISO 9001 for the quality system, BSCI or SMETA for social compliance. Also state whose certificate must it be — yours, the factory's, or the yarn supplier's, held by the factory. These are not interchangeable, and this is the field that most often turns a straightforward quote into a negotiation.

6. Finished format and packaging

Are you buying ribbon on rolls, on spools, cut to length, or as finished bows? State the roll length, the core size, the winding direction, and the carton configuration. If you are buying pre-made bows, state the construction: single-layer or double-layer, whether the tails are cut at a specific angle, whether the bow is heat-sealed or glued, and whether the elastic or cord is included. Bow geometry is where most of the hidden cost hides.

7. Quantity, split between forecast and firm

Two quantities, always: your firm first order and your forecast for the following six to twelve months. A factory pricing a 50,000 metre order amortises setup differently from one pricing a 500,000 metre order, and the per-metre price will reflect that. Giving both lets you see the real curve. It also opens the conversation about a capacity reservation for the forecast period, which is often where the better pricing lives.

8. Target price and target market

State your target price and the retail price point of the finished product. The second is more useful than the first. A factory that knows your product retails at a specific price can advise on material substitutions that protect your margin, because they understand the constraint. A factory that only knows your target number will either decline the programme or quietly select the cheapest material that technically meets the brief. Tell them the market and they will tell you when your brief is asking for something that cannot be made profitably at that price.

9. Delivery terms and destination

State the Incoterm and the destination port or city. EXW and DDP produce wildly different quotes, and the difference is not margin — it is who arranges freight, who pays it, and who carries the risk in transit. Quote everything on the same term. If you want FOB Xiamen priced and also want a landed figure for comparison, ask for both in the same response, with the freight assumption stated. A buyer who compares an FOB quote against a DDP quote is comparing two different questions.

10. Lead time expectation and the date it must arrive

Say when you need the goods on your shelf, not when you need them ordered. Say whether that date is fixed or flexible, and whether it is tied to a launch, a retail season, or a replenishment. A factory with peak-season capacity constraints can often say no to your date — which is useful information to have before you spend three weeks on artwork. If your date is flexible, say by how much.

11. Sampling expectations

State which samples you need and who pays. A standard sequence is a lab dip or swatch, then a pre-production sample, then a pre-shipment inspection. Some buyers want all three; others want a photo-based confirmation plus a pre-shipment inspection. Tell the factory what you want, because each sample costs money and time, and the factory will either include them in the quote or add them as line items.

12. Who owns the artwork and tooling, and what happens at the end

State that you own the artwork and that you expect the factory to retain nothing from it without written authorisation, and specify whether any printing plate, screen, or jacquard card is charged as a one-time setup fee, is amortised, or is to be destroyed at the end of the programme. This field feels like boilerplate. It is also the field that prevents a dispute eighteen months later when a former customer orders the same design from the same factory.

What a Complete Brief Looks Like in Practice

Here is a condensed example of the same product described two ways. The first is the brief most buyers send:

"Please quote 25 mm grosgrain ribbon, metallic gold, 5,000 metres, printed with our logo. Need it by December. Please advise best price."

The second is the brief that produces a comparable, usable quote:

100% polyester grosgrain, 25 mm (plus or minus 0.5 mm), matte face, textured reverse, single-colour metallic gold dye matched to Pantone 872 C on the face. Firm order 5,000 metres; 12-month forecast 60,000 metres across 6 SKUs. Ribbon supplied on 25 mm paper cores, 100 metres per spool, 20 spools per export carton, polybagged individually. No OEKO-TEX requirement on this SKU; ISO 9001 quality system certificate required. Quote FOB Xiamen and DDP Rotterdam, both on the same specification. Need on our shelf by 15 December, launch date fixed. Pre-production sample and pre-shipment inspection required, both charged to us. Artwork and any printing tooling owned by us; tooling to be destroyed on written instruction. Target FOB USD per metre is stated separately; our finished gift box retails at USD 24.99.

The second brief costs the buyer twenty minutes to write. It returns quotes that can be compared line by line, and it returns a much more useful conversation, because the factory now knows exactly which decisions it is being asked to make.

Reading the Responses You Get Back

Once responses arrive, resist the urge to rank them by the number at the top. Instead check four things.

Check that the specification matches. A quote at a materially lower price usually reflects a materially different specification. Look at the material description, the width tolerance, the decoration method, the certification statements and the packaging. If three of the five differ, you are not comparing offers.

Check that the one-time and recurring costs are separated. A responsible quotation separates setup, tooling and development fees from the per-unit price. A quotation that buries a USD 800 screen charge inside a per-metre figure will either make your life harder or make the headline number look artificially attractive. Ask for the split. Then model the actual spend across your forecast volume rather than your first order, because a setup fee amortised over 60,000 metres is a very different animal from one amortised over 5,000.

Check that the certifications are attributed correctly. "We have OEKO-TEX" is not the same as "we can supply the certificate covering the specific article number you will produce". Ask for the certificate number, the issuing body, the scope and the expiry date. If a factory says it will obtain certification for your programme, ask who pays for that and how long it takes, because it can add four to eight weeks that must be built into your schedule.

Check that the lead time is broken down. "30 days" is rarely the whole answer. The breakdown that matters is: sample approval, material procurement, dyeing, weaving, printing, finishing, packing, and transit. If the factory says 30 days, ask which of those 30 days is your responsibility and which are theirs. The number of business days you control is often much smaller than the total, and that is where the schedule risk lives.

Common Mistakes That Cost Buyers Money

Comparing quotes on different Incoterms. This is the single most common error, and it produces wildly misleading conclusions in both directions.

Specifying colour by name rather than reference. "Gold" is not a specification. "Pantone 872 C" is a specification. Two factories given "gold" will sample two different colours and both will be defensible.

Leaving certification open. If you omit it, the factory will quote without it, and you will discover the requirement at the pre-shipment stage when certification cannot be retrofitted to finished goods.

Not stating the finished-product retail price. This costs you nothing to include and frequently saves you money, because it lets a factory propose a material change that improves your margin rather than protecting their invoice.

Asking for "the best price" without a specification. You will receive the cheapest thing that satisfies your brief, and you will own it for a long time.

Treating the first response as final. Quotes from a factory that has just received a brief are not the factory's best offer. They are the factory's first answer. A second round of clarification, handled well, routinely produces better pricing because the factory now understands the programme rather than the document.

Getting a Second Round of Clarification Right

When you send a follow-up, do not simply ask for a better price. Ask three specific questions: which specification field would you change to reduce cost without changing how the finished product performs; what is the cost of the tooling as a one-time fee and how is it amortised; and what would you need from us in the next two weeks to hold the stated delivery date.

The first question reveals whether the factory has actually engineered your product or just quoted it. The second reveals the real cost structure. The third reveals where the schedule pressure genuinely sits. A factory that answers all three clearly is a factory you can run a programme with. A factory that cannot answer the first one does not yet understand your product, however good the price looks.

A Working Timeline for a First Programme

For a brand owner who has not run a ribbon programme before, a realistic first cycle runs eight to twelve weeks from brief to shipment. Brief written and issued: week one. Factory clarifications, technical proposals, revised quotes: weeks one to two. Sample production and your review: weeks two to four. Colour or artwork revision if required: week four. Pre-production sample, approval, and release to production: weeks four to five. Production: weeks five to eight, depending on decoration method and factory loading. Pre-shipment inspection and booking: week eight. Transit: two to five weeks depending on destination and mode. That is why the brief matters so much: every ambiguity that survives week one comes back as a week of delay in week four.

Two things shorten it. First, a complete brief on day one. Second, deciding your approval authority in advance — know who signs off colour, who signs off samples, and what "approved" means, so that a sample does not sit in an inbox for a week waiting for a decision that nobody owned.

Why Factories Prefer Complete Briefs

It is worth understanding that a complete brief is not a favour to the factory. It is a favour to both sides. From the factory's side, an ambiguous brief means they quote, you reject the price, and both of you have spent a week. From your side, an ambiguous brief means you get a lower price for a lower specification and only discover it when the goods arrive or, worse, when they arrive at your customer.

The genuinely sophisticated buyers we work with are not the ones who squeeze the lowest price. They are the ones who can describe what they need precisely enough that two suppliers will arrive at similar answers — and who then use that comparability to negotiate hard on the terms that actually move the number. Precision is what creates leverage. A vague brief does not create a cheaper ribbon; it creates an ambiguous one, and you pay for the ambiguity later.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions brand owners and procurement managers most often ask us about OEM ribbon briefs and quotations. If your question is not here, ask us directly.

1. What are the most important fields to include in a ribbon OEM development brief?

Construction and material named precisely, width with tolerance, decoration method, colour reference and approval route, certification requirements, finished format and packaging, firm quantity alongside forecast, delivery terms and destination, the date the goods must reach your shelf, sampling expectations, and artwork and tooling ownership. These twelve fields cover every decision a factory would otherwise make on your behalf, which is why they make quotations comparable.

2. How many ribbon factories should I send a request for quotation to?

Between three and five is the practical range. Below three you have no basis for comparison and no negotiating position. Above five you spend more time evaluating responses than you save, and the additional suppliers are usually working from the same commodity mills as the others. Send identical briefs to all of them, request identical response formats, and compare the responses line by line rather than by headline price.

3. Should I give a factory my target price or my retail price?

Give both. The target price anchors the commercial conversation, and the retail price of your finished product tells the factory what margin you are working with. That second number is frequently the more valuable one, because it allows a factory to suggest material or construction substitutions that protect your margin instead of simply maximising their own invoice.

4. What should I do if the quotation is significantly lower than the others?

Check the specification first. A materially lower price almost always reflects a materially different specification, and the usual culprits are material type, decoration method, certification coverage, width tolerance and packaging. Ask the factory to restate the quotation against your brief field by field. If the specification genuinely matches, a lower price is worth pursuing; if it does not, you have not received a lower offer, you have received a different product.

5. How long does it usually take to go from a brief to a first shipment?

Eight to twelve weeks for a first programme is realistic. Roughly one week for the brief and clarification round, two to three weeks for samples and your review, one week for pre-production sample approval, three to four weeks for production, and two to five weeks for transit. Decoration method and factory loading are the two variables that move the number most. Building the schedule in reverse from your shelf date is the reliable way to protect a launch.

6. What does a factory mean by saying it can certify the ribbon for us?

It usually means the certificate will be issued against a specific article number for your programme, rather than covering the factory's whole range. That matters because it may add four to eight weeks to the timeline and may carry a charge. Ask for the certificate number, issuing body, scope and expiry date, and confirm whether the cost is quoted as a one-time fee or amortised. Ask this before you approve the pre-production sample, not after.

7. Do we need a second round of clarification, or should we accept the first quotation?

Run a second round with any factory worth working with. Do not simply ask for a better price; ask which specification field could change to reduce cost without affecting performance, how the tooling cost is structured, and what you would need to provide in the next two weeks to protect the delivery date. A factory that answers those three clearly is telling you it has understood your product. One that cannot answer the first has priced a document rather than engineered a product.
